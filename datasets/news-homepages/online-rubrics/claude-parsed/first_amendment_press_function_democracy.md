# First Amendment Press Function — Democratic Newsworthiness

The constitutional theory of the press defines what kind of journalism deserves protection and what content qualifies as serving democracy.

## Constitutional Press Function (Knight First Amendment Institute)

"The unique constitutional value served by the press [is] the distribution of information necessary for self-government."

## Press Functions per First Amendment Theory

1. **Information distribution** — Citizens need facts for self-governance
2. **Watchdog of government** — Investigate and report wrongdoing
3. **Forum for public debate** — Provide arena for discourse
4. **Education of voters**
5. **Holding power accountable** — Government, business, institutions
6. **Counter-majoritarian voice** — Protect minorities from tyranny

## Implicit Newsworthiness from Democratic Theory

### What Counts as Democratically Valuable News

1. **Government accountability** — actions of elected officials
2. **Policy implications** — what government decisions mean
3. **Voting / electoral** information
4. **Wrongdoing exposure** — corruption, fraud, abuse
5. **Public spending** — how taxes used
6. **Court decisions** — judicial branch
7. **Regulatory actions**
8. **Public health and safety**
9. **Civil rights and liberties**
10. **Election integrity**
11. **Dissenting voices**
12. **Policy alternatives**
13. **International affairs** affecting nation
14. **Economic conditions**
15. **Inequality and access**

### What's Less Democratically Valuable
- Pure entertainment / celebrity
- Lifestyle / consumer
- Sports
- Sensationalized crime
- Tabloid scandal

## Tensions

- **Public service** vs. **public interest** (what people want vs. what they need)
- **Free press** vs. **profit motive**
- **Watchdog role** vs. **economic dependence**
- **Editorial judgment** vs. **algorithmic delivery**

## Historical Codification

### Free Press Cases Defining Function
- New York Times v. Sullivan (1964) — actual malice standard
- Pentagon Papers (1971) — prior restraint limits
- Branzburg v. Hayes (1972) — reporter's privilege debated
- New York Times v. United States (1971) — government secrets

## Implicit Newsworthiness Criteria from First Amendment Tradition

- **Self-government enabling**
- **Watchdog producing**
- **Forum-creating**
- **Independence from power**
- **Plurality of voice**
- **Counter-majoritarian protection**
- **Truth pursuing**
- **Public interest serving**
