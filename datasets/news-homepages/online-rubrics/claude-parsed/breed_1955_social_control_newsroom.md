# Breed (1955) - Social Control in the Newsroom: A Functional Analysis

Warren Breed's classic ethnographic study of how publishers' policy gets enforced on staff reporters even when it is never made explicit. Based on the author's newspaper experience and ~120 interviews with reporters at northeastern papers (10,000–100,000 circulation). Published in *Social Forces* 33(4): 326–335.

Breed's framework explains why news outputs cluster around an outlet's covert "policy" line — the implicit prior that conditions which stories get pursued, played up, or buried.

## How Reporters Learn Policy (Without Being Told)

1. **Reading their own paper** - New reporters infer policy from the paper's existing coverage patterns.
2. **Editorial blue-pencil** - Story changes/cuts signal acceptable angles.
3. **Conferences and gossip** - Watercooler talk transmits taboos and preferred frames.
4. **Anticipatory self-censorship** - Reporters internalize policy and avoid filing stories likely to be killed.

## Six Reasons Reporters Conform to Policy

1. **Institutional authority and sanctions** - Editors hold hiring/firing/assignment power.
2. **Feelings of obligation and esteem for superiors** - Personal loyalty to mentors.
3. **Mobility aspirations** - Career advancement requires fitting in.
4. **Absence of conflicting group allegiance** - No counter-organization (weak unions in 1950s) to back deviance.
5. **The pleasant nature of the activity** - Reporters enjoy the job and avoid disrupting it.
6. **News as a value in itself** - Getting the story trumps fighting policy battles.

## Five Routes by Which Reporters Sometimes Bypass Policy

1. **Norms of policy are not always defined** - Ambiguity creates discretion.
2. **Executives are sometimes ignorant of facts** - Reporters can finesse coverage.
3. **Plant the story with a wire service** - To force editors to run it.
4. **Star reporters have leverage** - Prominence buys deviance.
5. **Reporters with strong policy convictions** - Sometimes assert them successfully.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Policy as covert prior**: Outlet policy operates as an unwritten newsworthiness filter — what counts as a story differs systematically by paper because of socialization, not just market.
- **Conservatism bias**: Policy "usually protects property and class interests" — important information may be slanted, buried, or denied to citizenry.
- **Newsroom socialization > formal rules**: Conformity is achieved without explicit instruction, which means newsworthiness criteria are tacit and learned through immersion.
- **High morale → policy compliance**: When reporters feel part of the team, policy conflicts are shelved in favor of "getting more news."
