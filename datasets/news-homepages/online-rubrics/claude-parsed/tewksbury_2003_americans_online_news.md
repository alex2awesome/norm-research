# Tewksbury (2003) - What Do Americans Really Want to Know? Tracking the Behavior of News Readers on the Internet

David Tewksbury's pioneering empirical study of online news consumption using clickstream data — the first paper to systematically compare what users *click* with what editors *select*. Published in the *Journal of Communication* 53(4): 694–710.

The contribution: Used Nielsen NetRatings panel data to track actual reading behavior on news websites; quantified the audience-editor gap that Lee & Tandoc (2017) later synthesized.

## Core Empirical Finding

> "Online news consumers do not select hard news content as often as they select other news content."

While editors prominently feature politics, foreign affairs, and policy on front pages, users' actual clicks favor:
- Entertainment
- Sports  
- Lifestyle
- Local interest
- Crime
- Weather

Politics and foreign affairs received fewer clicks per impression than soft topics.

## Six Implications Identified

1. **Selection-Display Gap** - Editors display hard news prominently; users click on soft news disproportionately. The audience-editor mismatch is large and consistent.

2. **Clickstream as Revealed Preference** - User behavior reveals preferences that survey responses (asking what people *want* to read) systematically misrepresent. Surveys overstate hard-news interest because of social desirability.

3. **Active Audience** - Online users are not passive recipients of editorial agendas; they navigate selectively to topics of personal interest.

4. **Topic Self-Sorting** - Online news enables fine-grained topic preference expression; users with narrow interests bypass general front-page selection.

5. **Implications for Civic Knowledge** - If users systematically avoid hard news, online news may *reduce* shared political knowledge relative to the print era when readers encountered hard news incidentally.

6. **Predicts Adoption of Audience Metrics** - Tewksbury anticipates that publishers, learning what users actually click, will reshape selection toward soft content (later confirmed by Welbers et al. 2016, Vu 2014).

## Operational News-Value Insights from Online Click Data

1. **Soft-Topic Click Premium** - Per impression, soft topics generate more clicks than hard topics on most outlets.

2. **Headline-Driven Selection** - Online users decide whether to click based on headlines almost exclusively; headlines do disproportionate work.

3. **Topic Self-Selection** - Users with narrow interests use search and topic pages, bypassing front-page editorial choices entirely.

4. **Personalization Premium** - Stories matched to individual interest signals get higher engagement than generic-interest stories.

5. **Brevity Premium** - Online users click on shorter, more digestible content more readily; long-form has lower click-through but higher dwell time.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Two distinct newsworthiness measures online**: editorial placement vs. user clickthrough — each capturing different aspects of "newsworthiness." Models that conflate them are misspecified.

- **Predicts the soft-news commercial pressure**: As editors get clickstream data, they face market pressure to shift selection toward soft content (Patterson 2000's worry quantified).

- **Operationalizable**: Click-through rate per impression is a primary metric; can be modeled separately from editorial placement.

- **Foundational for engagement-based newsworthiness research**: Trilling et al. 2017 (shareworthiness) and Berger & Milkman 2012 (virality) build on Tewksbury's finding that user behavior diverges from editorial selection.
