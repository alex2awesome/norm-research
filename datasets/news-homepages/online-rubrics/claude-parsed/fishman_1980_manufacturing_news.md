# Fishman (1980) - Manufacturing the News

Mark Fishman's ethnographic study of a small California city newsroom, building on Tuchman to argue that news is a *bureaucratic product*: events become news only when they pass through the administrative procedures of governmental and corporate organizations from which reporters work their beats.

Published by University of Texas Press, 1980. The book made the most thoroughgoing case that newsworthiness is *manufactured* by the intersection of newsroom routines and bureaucratic source organizations.

## Core Argument: The Bureaucratic Phase

Reporters cover the world through *beats* (police, courts, city hall, the legislature, school board) where each beat is a bureaucratic organization. An event is newsworthy only if a bureaucracy *processes* it — turns it into a "case," a "filing," an "indictment," a "press conference," a "report."

> "Occurrences which are not part of some bureaucratic phase are simply ignored."

This means the *bureaucratic phase* of an event functions as a master news value.

## Why Bureaucratic Processing Confers Newsworthiness

1. **Bureaucratic events are pre-scheduled** - Court calendars, agency meetings, council sessions are knowable in advance; reporters can be deployed.

2. **Bureaucratic events are pre-classified** - The bureaucracy assigns the event a type (felony, misdemeanor, ruling, ordinance) that the reporter inherits.

3. **Bureaucratic events are pre-evidenced** - Documents, transcripts, official statements are produced; reporter can quote them under objectivity rituals.

4. **Bureaucratic events have authorized speakers** - There is always a designated official who can be cited, satisfying Tuchman's objectivity ritual of attribution.

5. **Bureaucratic events are repeated** - Beats produce a steady supply of similar events, sustaining the reporter's daily quota.

## Fishman's Three Phases of News Construction

1. **Detection** - Becoming aware that "something happened." Reporters detect through bureaucratic outputs (police blotter, court docket), not direct observation.

2. **Interpretation** - Understanding what kind of event it is. Reporters adopt the bureaucracy's frame (e.g., police characterize a fight as "domestic," reporter inherits that frame).

3. **Assembly into a Story** - Drawing the event into a publishable narrative. Wire-copy templates, beat traditions, and house style supply the form.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Beat coverage as a master filter**: Events that fall outside any beat are systematically uncovered. This is a structural exclusion — not bias against the topic per se, but absence of routine detection.

- **Bureaucratic reality > experiential reality**: Police categorizations of crime, agency definitions of pollution, official labels of "protest" become the news's working reality.

- **Status-quo conservatism**: Because bureaucracies define and report their own activities, news systematically reproduces the perspective of established institutions.

- **Crime-wave manufacture**: Fishman's most cited finding — a "crime wave against the elderly" emerged in NYC news from a police-press feedback loop, not a real spike. Demonstrates that bureaucratic source/news cycles can manufacture story themes regardless of underlying frequencies.

- **Why citizen sources struggle**: Stories from non-bureaucratic sources (neighbors, activists, witnesses) require time-consuming verification; default is to wait for the bureaucratic version.
