# Bagdikian (1983, updated through 2004) - The Media Monopoly / The New Media Monopoly

Ben Bagdikian's longitudinal study of US media ownership concentration. Across seven editions (1983–2004), tracked the shrinking number of corporations controlling US media: 50 in 1983, 26 in 1987, 14 in 1992, 6 in 2000, 5 in 2004. Beacon Press.

The contribution: Empirical documentation of how ownership concentration shapes news selection — the most thoroughly documented application of the political-economy frame to US news.

## Three Mechanisms by Which Ownership Concentration Affects News Selection

1. **Conglomerate Cross-Interest Suppression** - When a media company is part of a conglomerate, news critical of any subsidiary's business is suppressed. (Bagdikian documents specific cases of suppressed stories.)

2. **Advertiser Sensitivity** - Concentrated outlets dependent on a smaller number of major advertisers face stronger pressure to avoid stories that would alienate them.

3. **Risk-Averse Editorial Culture** - Large publicly-traded media companies face shareholder pressure for predictable revenue; controversial coverage that risks subscriber/advertiser loss is demoted.

## Six Documented News-Selection Patterns

1. **Underreporting of Corporate Misbehavior** - Cross-ownership creates structural disincentive to investigate corporate practices similar to those of the parent.

2. **Underreporting of Labor Issues** - Major media owned by anti-union conglomerates underreport labor stories favorably to workers.

3. **Underreporting of Media Industry Itself** - Press critic of media practices (concentration, monopoly) systematically thin in mainstream outlets.

4. **Pro-Advertiser Topic Selection** - Topics congenial to major advertiser categories (cars, pharma, finance) get extensive coverage, often favorable; topics critical of those industries get less.

5. **Ideological Convergence** - Concentrated ownership produces editorial-line convergence; divergent perspectives squeezed.

6. **Investigative Resource Depletion** - Cost-cutting under concentrated ownership shrinks investigative units; investigative stories therefore decline in frequency.

## The Affluent-Audience Bias

Bagdikian documents how advertisers' demand for affluent audiences shapes editorial selection:

- Stories appealing to affluent readers (luxury consumer goods, financial markets, investment topics) over-supplied.
- Stories appealing to working-class or low-income readers (food prices, housing, public services) under-supplied at the front-page level.
- Newspapers redesigned to emphasize sections appealing to advertisers' target demographics.
- News audiences of "low purchasing power" treated as commercially uninteresting.

## Operational News-Selection Predictions

1. **Corporate-Subsidiary Story Suppression** - Coverage of business misconduct by a conglomerate's subsidiaries less than coverage of equivalent misconduct by competitors.

2. **Affluent-Audience Topic Premium** - Lifestyle, financial, investment, real estate, consumer-electronics topics over-represented relative to general-audience interest.

3. **Advertiser-Friendly Headline Tone** - Headlines on advertiser-industry topics skew toward neutral or positive even when underlying findings are negative.

4. **Investigative-Cost Constraint** - Investigative outputs proportional to investigative budget, which shrinks under concentrated ownership.

5. **Editorial-Line Standardization** - Editorial positions across owned outlets converge over time post-acquisition.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Empirical foundation for political-economy of news**: Bagdikian's serial editions provide longitudinal evidence that complements Herman & Chomsky's theoretical framework.

- **Ownership as covariate**: Modern news-selection studies routinely use outlet ownership as a predictor variable; Bagdikian's work justifies this practice.

- **Predicts story-suppression effects**: Stories most under-covered should be those threatening parent companies' interests; testable on cross-outlet content data.

- **Operationalizable**: Ownership data are public; effects on coverage measurable via topic-frequency analysis.
