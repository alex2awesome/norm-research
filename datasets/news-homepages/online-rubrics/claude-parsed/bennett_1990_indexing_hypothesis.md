# Bennett (1990) - Toward a Theory of Press-State Relations in the United States

W. Lance Bennett's foundational article on the *indexing hypothesis* of news selection. Tested on four years of New York Times coverage of the US-funded Nicaraguan contra war. Published in the *Journal of Communication* 40(2): 103–127.

The contribution: News selection on political issues "indexes" the range of voices and viewpoints to those *already debated by mainstream government elites*. When elites agree, news is consensus; when elites disagree, news shows the range of elite disagreement. Public opinion outside elite debate gets little coverage even when widely held.

## The Indexing Hypothesis

> "Mass media news professionals, from the boardroom to the beat reporter, tend to 'index' the range of voices and viewpoints in both news and editorials according to the range of views expressed in mainstream government debate about a given topic."

Predictions:

1. **Elite Consensus → News Consensus** - When the White House and congressional leaders agree, news coverage will reflect that consensus, ignoring dissenting voices.

2. **Elite Disagreement → News Coverage of Both Sides** - When elites split, news coverage will fall within the range of their disagreement.

3. **Public Opinion Outside Elite Range** - Largely excluded; even when polls show majority support for a position, if no major elite voice supports it, news will not represent it.

## Operational News-Selection Implications

1. **Source Authorization Threshold** - For a viewpoint to be reportable, an authorized elite source must articulate it. No elite voice → no representation.

2. **Indexed Diversity** - The breadth of perspectives in news coverage is bounded above by the breadth of elite debate, not by the breadth of public opinion.

3. **Critical-Story Restriction** - Critical stories about government policy are gated by whether elites are willing to criticize. Bipartisan elite consensus shields policy from media scrutiny.

4. **Evidentiary Asymmetry** - Reports challenging elite consensus must clear higher evidentiary bars than reports affirming it.

5. **Beat-Channel Capture** - Beats organized around official institutions (White House, Congress, Pentagon) systematically reproduce institutional perspectives.

## Why the Indexing Pattern Emerges

1. **Objectivity Rituals** (Tuchman 1972) - Authoritative sources required; only elites are reliably authoritative.

2. **Routine Channels** (Sigal 1973) - Reporters work official-institutional beats; elite voices flow naturally; non-elite voices require enterprise.

3. **Risk Mitigation** - Stories outside the indexed range expose outlets to political and reputational risk.

4. **Pack Convergence** (Donsbach 2004) - Reporters reassure themselves about story selection by checking what other reporters cover; reproduces indexed range across outlets.

## Empirical Application: Contra War Coverage

- **Phase 1** (Reagan administration support, weak elite opposition): NYT coverage largely supportive, few critical stories.
- **Phase 2** (Iran-Contra revelations, congressional Democrats critical): NYT coverage critical, included contra-critical voices.
- **Public opinion was consistently more critical than NYT coverage in both phases** — coverage tracked elite debate, not public opinion.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Quantitative test**: For any political issue, the diversity of viewpoints in news coverage can be measured against the diversity of elite-source viewpoints; deviation from indexing should be small.

- **Predicts coverage of war and crisis**: In wars, elite consensus typically forms initially → narrow critical coverage in early phases. As elites split, coverage broadens.

- **Operationalizable for homepage models**: Whether a story's framing falls within the established elite-debate range can be coded; out-of-range framings should be rare.

- **Predicts which protest viewpoints break through**: Per Gitlin 1980, only protest viewpoints articulated by an elite ally enter mainstream coverage.
