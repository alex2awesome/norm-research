# Components of Newsworthiness — Purdue OWL

Two foundational frameworks taught in journalism education.

## Galtung & Ruge Model (as taught)

1. **Relevance** - Relevance of news story to the audience. Example: West Coast audiences find California earthquakes more newsworthy than distant events.
2. **Timeliness** - Recency; recent or developing events dominate the modern news cycle.
3. **Simplification** - Stories that are easy to understand receive more coverage.
4. **Predictability** - Scheduled events (elections, sports) gain news value as they approach.
5. **Unexpectedness** - Unpredictable disasters, accidents, or crimes carry significant news value.
6. **Continuity** - Ongoing situations (wars, protests) warrant extended coverage.
7. **Composition** - Editors balance story selection for variety.
8. **Elite People** - Politicians, entertainers, athletes get more coverage.
9. **Elite Countries** - Disasters in wealthy/developed nations get more attention.
10. **Negativity** - Bad news deemed more newsworthy than good news.

## Shoemaker et al. Model (1987)

1. **Timeliness** - Critical for news selection.
2. **Proximity** - Geographic closeness to the audience.
3. **Importance / Impact / Consequence** - How many people the event affects.
4. **Interest** - Stories with human interest appeal.
5. **Conflict or Controversy** - Conflict more newsworthy than peace.
6. **Sensationalism** - Sensational stories earn front-page placement.
7. **Prominence** - Notable figures vs. ordinary citizens.
8. **Novelty / Oddity / Unusual** - Strange occurrences attract media attention.

## Application

Used in introductory journalism courses to evaluate story pitches and editorial decisions.
