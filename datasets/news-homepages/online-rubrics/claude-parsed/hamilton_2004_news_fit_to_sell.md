# Hamilton (2004) - All the News That's Fit to Sell: How the Market Transforms Information into News

James T. Hamilton's economic-theory account of news selection. Hamilton, an economist, applies industrial-organization analysis to the news industry. Published by Princeton University Press, 2004. Won the 2004 Frank Luther Mott Award.

The contribution: Develops the first formal economic theory of news. Models a story's likelihood of coverage as a function of (a) audience demand, (b) advertiser value, (c) production cost, and (d) competitive landscape — providing quantitative grounding for what news sociologists had described qualitatively.

## The Five W's of Economic News Theory (Hamilton's Framework)

Hamilton reframes the journalist's "five W's" (who/what/when/where/why) into economic terms:

1. **WHO cares about the story?** - Audience size, demographic profile, attention.

2. **WHAT are they willing to pay or do?** - Direct payment, ad-attention, sharing behavior.

3. **WHO are advertisers trying to reach?** - Demographic targets that align (or don't) with story audience.

4. **WHEN does the story have value?** - Time-decay function of news value.

5. **WHERE/HOW is the story produced and delivered?** - Production cost, distribution channels.

## Five Economic Determinants of Story Coverage

1. **Audience Demand Curve** - Stories appealing to large audiences with sustained attention preferred.

2. **Advertiser-Audience Match** - Stories whose audience matches valuable advertiser targets receive priority placement.

3. **Production Cost** - Investigative stories cost more per outcome; lower-cost stories (wire copy, press releases) preferred at the margin.

4. **Cost of Substitutes** - When competing outlets cover similar stories, cost of differentiation rises; outlets seek niches.

5. **Externalities** - Some stories produce social benefit (informed citizenry) but private cost (alienated advertisers); these are systematically under-supplied by markets.

## Empirical Findings

1. **Late-19th-century shift to non-partisan reporting** was driven by economics: as cities grew and ad markets expanded, reaching the broadest possible audience became more profitable than serving partisan niches.

2. **Late-20th-century soft-news drift** (1969–1998 evening news) was driven by cable competition, deregulation, and ownership changes. Hard-news supply contracted because hard news has positive externalities (public benefit) but negative private value (lower ad revenues).

3. **Demographic targeting**: Stories reaching the 18–34 demographic (most advertiser-valued in 1990s) received disproportionate coverage; stories reaching older or poorer audiences were demoted.

4. **News deserts emerge** in markets too small to sustain advertiser-funded production.

## Operational News-Selection Predictions

1. **Audience-Size Premium** - Stories with larger predicted audiences favored, all else equal.

2. **Demographic-Match Premium** - Stories appealing to advertisers' target demographics favored even over stories with larger but less valuable audiences.

3. **Cost-per-Story Discount** - Wire-derived, press-release-derived, and cheap-to-produce stories favored over investigative pieces.

4. **Substitution Differentiation** - Outlets choose stories that differentiate them from competitors when competition is high; converge to mainstream when competition is low.

5. **Externality-Insensitive** - Public-interest stories with low private value (e.g., complex policy investigations) systematically under-supplied.

## Five Policy Recommendations

1. Lower costs of access to government information (FOIA reforms).
2. Expand nonprofit funding for journalism.
3. Develop professional norms emphasizing hard news.
4. Define digital/Internet property rights to enable news flow.
5. Subsidize public-interest investigative production.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Quantitative model of newsworthiness**: Story coverage probability can be modeled as a function of demand, advertiser value, cost, and competition.

- **Predicts secular trends**: Soft-news drift, news desert formation, audience-segmentation are all predicted by the model.

- **Operationalizable for homepage models**: Each variable is measurable; can be combined with editorial-judgment variables to predict placement.

- **Bridges Galtung-Ruge tradition with industrial economics**: Provides the missing economic mechanism that supplements sociological accounts of selection.
