# Patterson & Donsbach (1996) - News Decisions: Journalists as Partisan Actors

Thomas Patterson and Wolfgang Donsbach's five-nation comparative survey of 1,000+ journalists in the US, UK, Germany, Italy, and Sweden, testing whether journalists' personal political beliefs influence their news decisions. Published in *Political Communication* 13(4): 455–468.

The contribution: First clean cross-national empirical evidence that journalists' political ideology produces *moderate but consistent* bias in news selection — even though journalists self-define as objective professionals.

## Methodology

Quasi-experimental survey: respondents were journalists across five countries; the natural variation in their political orientations was the quasi-experimental factor; their preferences in hypothetical news-decision scenarios (which story to lead with, which source to highlight, which framing to choose) were the dependent variables.

## Core Findings

1. **Cross-National Stability** - In all five countries, journalists' personal ideology predicted their news-decision preferences.

2. **Moderate Effect Size** - The effect was moderate in magnitude, not overwhelming — consistent with journalists' self-perception that they constrain their personal views, but not consistent with claims of perfect objectivity.

3. **Topic-Variant** - Effects strongest on stories with clear partisan implications; weakest on routine procedural news.

4. **Country-Variant Magnitude** - Italy showed strongest ideology-decision link; US/UK weakest. Hypothesized to reflect Hallin & Mancini-style media-system differences (Liberal vs. Polarized Pluralist).

5. **Resilience to Professional Norms** - Even journalists who endorsed strong objectivity norms showed measurable ideological influence in their decisions.

## Operational News-Selection Criteria Driven by Journalist Ideology

1. **Ideologically Compatible Source Preference** - Journalists more likely to lead with sources whose views align with their own.

2. **Ideologically Compatible Frame Preference** - Among multiple legitimate framings of an event, journalists choose framings closer to their own views.

3. **Story Selection from Among Alternatives** - When multiple stories of equal news value compete, journalists' preferences tip toward stories that resonate ideologically.

4. **Critical-Tone Asymmetry** - Journalists apply more critical scrutiny to actors and policies they oppose.

5. **Threshold Asymmetry** - Stories supportive of journalists' views may need lower magnitude / drama to become news.

## Three Filtering Mechanisms

1. **Pre-conscious Selection** - Journalists may not be aware of ideological tilt in their decisions; filter operates below explicit reasoning.

2. **Routine Reinforcement** - Newsroom routines (which sources to call, which beats to staff) embed ideological assumptions over time.

3. **Self-Selection into Outlets** - Journalists self-sort into outlets matching their views, compounding outlet-level ideological coloration.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Cross-national operationalizable bias**: Outlet-level ideological coloration is empirically detectable; can be used as a predictor of placement decisions.

- **Predicts modern partisan-outlet phenomena**: Patterson & Donsbach's 1996 findings predicted the increased explicit partisanship of US cable / online outlets (Stroud 2008).

- **Refutes both extremes**: News is neither fully objective (against industry self-image) nor fully partisan (against critics' worst claims). Bias is moderate, measurable, and structural.

- **Empirically grounded**: Provides quantitative justification for treating outlet identity as a meaningful covariate in newsworthiness models.
