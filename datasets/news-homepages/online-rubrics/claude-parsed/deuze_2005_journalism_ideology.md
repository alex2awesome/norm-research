# Deuze (2005) - What is Journalism? Professional Identity and Ideology of Journalists Reconsidered

Mark Deuze's influential synthesis of the *occupational ideology* of journalism — the shared self-understanding through which journalists across countries and outlets define what counts as "real" journalism. Published in *Journalism* 6(4): 442–464.

The contribution: Identifies five values that operate as the master criteria for what journalism *is* and what stories therefore deserve to be called news. Each value functions as a newsworthiness filter at the meta-level.

## The Five Core Values of Journalism's Occupational Ideology

1. **Public Service** - Journalists see themselves as providing a service to citizens of a democracy. Stories matter because they help citizens understand their world and govern themselves. Stories that contribute to public knowledge get the "newsworthy" label; trivial stories are suspect.

2. **Objectivity / Fairness / Impartiality** - Journalists must be "impartial, fair, and (therefore) trustworthy." Stories must admit balanced presentation; stories that don't are reframed or downgraded.

3. **Autonomy** - Journalists must be free of external influence (advertisers, governments, sources). Stories that respect editorial independence are favored; stories that appear to serve interested parties' agendas are suspect.

4. **Immediacy / Speed / Actuality** - Journalism's "operational logic" requires reporting on breaking news, getting the story first. Recency is itself a master criterion of newsworthiness; stale stories don't qualify.

5. **Ethics / Social Responsibility** - Journalists hold themselves to ethical standards (verification, harm minimization, source protection). Stories must pass ethical filters to qualify as news.

## How These Values Shape News Selection

1. **Public-Service Filter** - Lifestyle / entertainment treated as "lower-tier" journalism even when popular; hard news / accountability stories accorded higher symbolic value.

2. **Objectivity Filter** - Stories must admit fair presentation; one-sided stories require additional discursive labor or reframing.

3. **Autonomy Filter** - Stories perceived as serving narrow interests (corporate PR, government propaganda) are demoted unless reframed.

4. **Immediacy Filter** - Recency premium so pervasive that even substantive stories age out of newsworthiness within a single news cycle.

5. **Ethics Filter** - Stories obtained through ethically questionable means (privacy violations, deception) face higher hurdles to publication.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Cross-national stability**: Deuze argues these values are remarkably stable across Western democracies — explaining the parallel news-selection patterns researchers find internationally.

- **Journalists' self-monitoring**: Stories selected partly because they pass the journalists' own occupational-identity test; stories that "feel" like real journalism survive editorial scrutiny.

- **Predicts brand differentiation by emphasis**: Outlets differentiate by which value they most emphasize (NYT — accountability/public service; Reuters — objectivity/immediacy; investigative outlets — ethics/autonomy).

- **Operationalizable**: Each value can be coded for in stories (presence of public-interest framing, balance markers, source-attribution markers, recency markers, ethical-disclosure markers); used as features in newsworthiness-prediction models.

- **Tension with audience-engagement metrics**: The five values often conflict with click-driven selection (Vu 2014, Welbers et al. 2016); the persistent norm-behavior gap reflects this tension.
