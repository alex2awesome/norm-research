# Heider (2000) - White News: Why Local News Programs Don't Cover People of Color

Don Heider's ethnographic study of two moderate-size, culturally diverse US local TV newsrooms. Published by Lawrence Erlbaum, 2000. The most thorough empirical analysis of *racial bias in news selection* at the local level.

The contribution: Identifies *incognizant racism* — newsworkers' systematic failure to perceive minority-community stories as newsworthy, not from explicit bias but from background assumptions about what counts as news. Provides a structural account of why local TV news systematically under-covers communities of color.

## Core Concept: Incognizant Racism

Newsworkers do not consciously decide to exclude minority-community stories. Instead, the routinized application of professional news values — combined with the predominantly white, middle-class background of decision-makers — produces selection patterns that systematically miss or distort coverage of communities of color.

## Six Mechanisms of Selection Bias

1. **News-Value Calibration to Majority Audience** - "Universal" news values were calibrated against majority-audience interests; events relevant to minority communities consistently fail the threshold.

2. **Source-Network Whiteness** - Reporters' established source networks are predominantly white, professional, institutional. Minority-community sources require enterprise reporting; default coverage flows through existing networks.

3. **Beat Geography** - Beats anchor in white-majority areas (city hall, downtown, business districts); minority neighborhoods covered via crime beats only.

4. **"White" Content Delivered by Diverse Anchors** - Newsroom diversity at the anchor level masks white-dominated decision-making about what stories run.

5. **Negative-Frame Default** - Coverage of minority communities concentrated in crime, accidents, deficits; community successes, leadership, civic engagement systematically under-covered.

6. **Place-Branding Effects** - Repeated negative coverage of specific neighborhoods (always associated with crime stories) reinforces audience perceptions of those areas as unsafe — a feedback loop with material consequences.

## Empirical Findings

1. **Crime-Heavy Coverage** - Black and Latino subjects appeared disproportionately in crime stories.

2. **Successful Minority-Community Stories Filtered Out** - Positive minority-community events (community organizations, business openings, cultural events) often pitched but rarely selected.

3. **Editor Decisions Based on "Audience Interest" Heuristics** - Editors invoked imagined-audience preferences to justify excluding minority-community stories.

4. **Reporters of Color Frustrated** - Minority reporters struggled to get minority-community stories accepted, even when professionally well-developed.

## Operational News-Selection Criteria Driving Disparate Coverage

1. **Audience-Demographic Match** - Story selection optimized for the imagined-typical-viewer (often a white middle-class suburbanite).

2. **Familiar-Source Premium** - Stories with sources already in the rolodex outpace stories requiring source-finding labor.

3. **Predictable-Frame Compatibility** - Stories that fit established narrative frames (crime, tragedy, community) are easier to compose; minority-community stories often require novel frames.

4. **Visual-Compatibility Bias** - Newsroom assumptions about what looks "right" for general-audience TV news skew against unfamiliar communities and settings.

5. **Speed of Source Verification** - Established institutional sources verify quickly; community sources require time, slowing newsworthiness.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Structural racial bias prediction**: Heider predicts that newsworthiness criteria, applied "neutrally," produce systematically race-disparate coverage.

- **Operationalizable**: Coverage parity ratios (minority-community story counts vs. demographic share) can be measured and benchmarked across outlets.

- **Bridges sociology of news with critical race studies**: Provides empirically grounded link between racial demographics of newsrooms / sources / framings and selection outcomes.

- **Predicts modern findings**: Subsequent research on coverage of Black Lives Matter, immigration, and police violence replicates Heider's structural patterns.

- **Reform implications**: Newsroom diversity, source-network diversification, beat reorganization, and audit metrics all flow from Heider's diagnosis.
