# Tuchman (1973) - Making News by Doing Work: Routinizing the Unexpected

Gaye Tuchman's seminal article on how news organizations process unexpected events through a typology of news categories that match organizational scheduling needs. Published in the *American Journal of Sociology* 79(1): 110–131.

The contribution: News categories ("hard news," "soft news," etc.) are not properties of events but *typifications* — practical labels that allow newsrooms to distribute work, allocate space, and meet deadlines. An event's classification determines its handling, including how, where, and whether it is reported.

## Five News Typifications

1. **Hard News** - Information people should have to be informed citizens. Time-bound, scheduled events of public consequence (legislation, accidents, crime, fires). Drives the front-page agenda.

2. **Soft News** - Human interest, features, lifestyle. Not time-bound; can be held and run when space is available. Lower deadline pressure.

3. **Spot News** - Sudden, unexpected hard news (a fire, a disaster). Requires immediate dispatch of resources.

4. **Developing News** - Hard news whose facts are still emerging during the news cycle (an unfolding hostage crisis). Requires updates.

5. **Continuing News** - Pre-known sequences of stories on a single topic stretching over days/weeks (a trial, a legislative session). Pre-scheduled and resource-allocated.

## Routinization Strategies

1. **Scheduling and Beat System** - Events anchored to known calendars (court dockets, agency briefings, sports schedules) are easier to cover and therefore more newsworthy.

2. **Geographic Centralization** - Resources concentrated near beats; events occurring outside reporter geography are systematically under-reported.

3. **Pre-allocation of Space and Personnel** - "Continuing news" gets fixed budgets, enabling more depth.

4. **Routinizing the Unexpected** - "Spot news" is itself routinized through rotating assignments and pre-built templates (the disaster story, the obituary).

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Schedulability** as an implicit news value: Events that fit organizational scheduling are vastly more reportable than those that don't.
- **Ease of typification**: Events that fit cleanly into one of the five categories receive faster, fuller treatment; ambiguous events get less.
- **Continuing-news momentum**: Once a topic is classified as "continuing news," subsequent installments enjoy a quasi-automatic newsworthiness boost (echoes Galtung & Ruge's *continuity*).
- **Beat capture**: Reporters' assignments shape what becomes news — events outside any beat are invisible.
- **Front-page eligibility**: "Hard" + "spot" or "developing" status is largely required for above-the-fold play.

## Why It Matters

The 1973 article (and the 1978 book *Making News*) made the constructionist case that news is the *manufactured product of organizational routines*, not a mirror of events. It is one of the most cited works in journalism sociology and the foundation for current scheduling/beat-capture critiques of news selection.
