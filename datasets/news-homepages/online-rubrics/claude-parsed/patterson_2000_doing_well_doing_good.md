# Patterson (2000) - Doing Well and Doing Good: How Soft News and Critical Journalism Are Shrinking the News Audience and Weakening Democracy

Thomas Patterson's Shorenstein Center report based on a two-year study including national surveys and content analysis of 5,331 news stories. Published December 2000 by Harvard Kennedy School's Joan Shorenstein Center on the Press, Politics and Public Policy.

The contribution: Quantifies the drift of US news selection from "hard" toward "soft" content over two decades and documents the audience and democratic costs.

## Five Documented News-Selection Trends

1. **Soft-News Increase** - From the 1980s to 2000, the proportion of soft-news stories (lifestyle, celebrity, human interest, sensational crime) rose dramatically across major US outlets — from ~25% in 1980 to ~50% by 2000 in some samples.

2. **Increased Critical / Negative Tone** - Even hard news shifted toward critical / negative framing of leaders and institutions; positive coverage declined.

3. **Personalization Increase** - Greater emphasis on individual leaders' personalities and private lives over institutional actions.

4. **Sensationalism Increase** - More crime, scandal, disaster coverage; less policy and process coverage.

5. **Reporter Voice Increase** - More opinion / interpretation by reporters embedded in ostensibly factual reports.

## Counterintuitive Findings on Audience Behavior

Patterson's surveys overturn the conventional "give the audience what it wants" justification for soft-news drift:

1. **Hard-news consumers are heavier news users overall** - Those who prefer hard news consume MORE news of all types than those who prefer soft news. Soft-news preference correlates with low news engagement, not high engagement.

2. **Soft news has not retained the audience** - Despite the shift, news audiences continued to decline. The strategy is failing on its own terms.

3. **Audience preferences are bimodal** - A serious-news audience and a near-non-audience exist; little middle ground.

4. **Critical tone alienates audiences** - Excessively negative coverage drives audiences away rather than engaging them.

## Implicit Newsworthiness Criteria Operative in the Soft-News Era

1. **Personalization** of policy stories around individual leaders.
2. **Visualizability** - Story selection biased toward photogenic events.
3. **Conflict / Controversy** - Story selection biased toward dispute.
4. **Quick Resolution** - Stories with rapid story-arcs preferred to ongoing complex situations.
5. **Celebrity-Adjacency** - Stories with celebrity hook get higher placement.
6. **Audience Stickiness Predictions** - Editorial guesses about what will keep audiences from switching channels / clicking away.

## Patterson's Five Recommended Reforms

1. **Reduce sensational / soft content**, especially in evening news.
2. **Reduce critical / negative tone**, especially in election coverage.
3. **Provide more substantive policy content**.
4. **Reduce personalization** of policy and process stories.
5. **Slow the reporter's voice** — return to source-based reporting.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Quantitative documentation of soft drift**: Provides baseline for measuring outlets' position on hard-soft continuum.

- **Audience-supply-side mismatch**: News producers' beliefs about what audiences want diverge from what audiences actually consume; a homepage-prediction model can test for this gap.

- **Predicts decline in democratic accountability**: Less hard news → fewer voters with policy knowledge → weaker electoral accountability.

- **Operationalizable**: The soft-news drift can be tracked by coding outlet content over time using Reinemann et al. (2012) three-dimensional scheme.
