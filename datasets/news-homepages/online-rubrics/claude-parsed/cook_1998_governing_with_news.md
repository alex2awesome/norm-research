# Cook (1998) - Governing with the News: The News Media as a Political Institution

Timothy E. Cook's classic argument that the news media should be understood as a *political institution* — coproducing policy alongside government rather than merely reporting on it. Published by University of Chicago Press, 1998.

The contribution: Reframes news selection as a *negotiation between newsworkers and political actors*, both of whom have institutional incentives. News content reflects the equilibrium of this negotiation, not unilateral newsroom judgment.

## Core Argument

The press is itself an institution that:
- Has standardized routines (beats, deadlines, formats).
- Has shared professional norms across outlets.
- Has collective power that exceeds any single outlet's intentions.
- Co-produces policy by setting which issues officials must address.

## Five Newsworthiness Implications of the Institutional Frame

1. **Authorized-Version Premium** - Because reporters base coverage on high-level government sources, news presents an "authorized version" reflecting what officials need to have reported.

2. **Source-Reporter Symbiosis** - Officials need coverage to advance agendas; reporters need access. Stories that serve both interests get told most readily.

3. **Pre-arranged Newsworthiness** - Officials craft events (speeches, ceremonies, press conferences) for media; the staging itself produces newsworthiness.

4. **Cross-Outlet Convergence** - The news institution operates collectively; what is treated as news at the New York Times tends to be treated as news at the Washington Post and CBS, by professional convergence.

5. **Institutional Self-Reinforcement** - Once the press as institution declares an issue newsworthy, the political system responds (officials speak, congressional hearings convene), generating more news — a closed loop.

## How the Press Co-Produces Policy

1. **Issue Surfacing** - Press attention forces officials to respond.
2. **Issue Definition** - Press framing fixes the terms of subsequent debate.
3. **Issue Persistence** - Press follow-up sustains issues; press abandonment kills them.
4. **Resolution Pressure** - Press demand for closure shapes policy decisions.
5. **Accountability Pressure** - Continuing press attention disciplines officials.

## Implications for News Selection

1. **Institutional Beat Coverage** - Because the press is institutionalized, coverage is also institutionalized: White House, Congress, Treasury, Pentagon, State each have permanent press corps. Newsworthiness is heavily skewed toward these beats.

2. **Symbiotic Story Premium** - Stories that serve both reporter and source interests (e.g., trial-balloon leaks, scoops on insider battles) achieve disproportionate coverage.

3. **Press-Setup Events** - Ceremonies, anniversaries, photo ops are inherently newsworthy because they are designed for the press; they pass the predictability and visual newsworthiness tests.

4. **Cross-Outlet Solidarity** - The press defends its access collectively; stories that defend press freedom (e.g., subpoena fights) get cross-outlet coverage.

5. **Personnel Changes** - Cabinet appointments, hires, fires are highly newsworthy because they affect the press's institutional access patterns.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Two-way co-production**: Newsworthiness is not just what newsrooms select but what the press-government system collectively constructs.

- **Predicts institutional inertia**: Newsworthy topics tend to persist because the institutional infrastructure (beat reporters, press offices) keeps producing material.

- **Operationalizable**: Topic salience can be predicted from the existence of institutional press apparatus around a topic; non-institutionalized topics struggle for sustained coverage.

- **Bridges newsroom sociology and political science**: Cook's framework links Tuchman/Fishman with political-institutional analysis.
