# Sigal (1973) - Reporters and Officials: The Organization and Politics of Newsmaking

Leon V. Sigal's foundational empirical study of news sources at the *New York Times* and *Washington Post*. Coded ~2,850 page-1 stories over 20 years (1949–1969) for source type and information channel. Published by D.C. Heath, 1973.

The contribution: First systematic empirical evidence that US national news is overwhelmingly produced via *routine channels* with *official sources*, not via investigative or enterprise reporting. Provides the canonical taxonomy of news source channels still cited today.

## Sigal's Three Channel Types

Sigal classified the information channel through which a story originated:

### 1. Routine Channels (~58% of front-page stories)

- **Official proceedings** (legislative sessions, court hearings, agency meetings).
- **Press releases** from government agencies, corporations, NGOs.
- **Press conferences and briefings** organized by sources.
- **Non-spontaneous events** staged for press attention (speeches, ceremonies, ribbon cuttings).

### 2. Informal Channels (~16%)

- **Background briefings** off-the-record.
- **Leaks** from disgruntled or strategic insiders.
- **Non-governmental proceedings** (think-tank events, conferences).
- **Other news organization reports** (cross-citation).

### 3. Enterprise Channels (~26%)

- **Reporter interviews** initiated by reporters.
- **Spontaneous events** witnessed by reporters.
- **Reporter analysis** based on independent reporting.
- **Investigation/research** by reporter.

## Sigal's Source Findings

- **~78% of US national news sources were US officials**.
- **~46% of front-page stories' sources were US federal officials**.
- **State/local officials** added another ~5%.
- **Foreign officials** added ~14%.
- **Non-government sources** (academics, business, citizens) only ~22%.

## Operational News-Selection Implications

1. **Routine-Channel Premium** - Stories arriving through official channels have systemic advantage in newsworthiness. Official events are pre-scheduled, source-attributed, and risk-mitigated.

2. **Press Release Conversion Rate** - Stories pegged to releases or briefings are easier to publish; this is itself a source of newsworthiness.

3. **Official-Source Authority** - Stories with named federal-official sources outrank stories without; official quotes confer publishability.

4. **Beat Anchoring** - Reporters' beats determine which routine channels they monitor; coverage thus mirrors the beat structure.

5. **Enterprise Cost** - Investigative / enterprise stories require more reporter time and editorial resources, lowering the production rate; structural disadvantage relative to routine channels.

## Bridge to Later Theory

Sigal's empirical findings were the foundation for:
- **Hall et al. 1978** primary-definers concept.
- **Fishman 1980** bureaucratic-phase theory.
- **Tuchman 1978** routinization-of-the-unexpected.
- **Bennett 1990** indexing hypothesis (news indexes elite debate).

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **Channel as a master news value**: Whether a story arrives via routine or enterprise channels is a primary predictor of whether and how it's covered.

- **Press-release reverse-engineering**: A homepage-prediction model can use whether a story has corresponding press releases as a feature; routine-channel stories should receive more reliable / earlier coverage.

- **Official-quote density**: Stories with multiple federal-official quotations should achieve higher placement; predicts which stories survive the objectivity-ritual filter.

- **Predicts elite-driven agenda**: ~80% official-source dependence means news selection systematically reproduces the agendas of elites with PR capacity.
