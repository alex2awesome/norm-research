# Cohen (1963) - The Press and Foreign Policy

Bernard C. Cohen's pioneering study of the relationship between Washington reporters and foreign policy makers. Based on participant observation and structured interviews with press and government in Washington in 1953-54 and 1960. Published by Princeton University Press, 1963.

The book contains the most cited aphorism in agenda-setting research, formulated nine years before McCombs & Shaw's empirical confirmation:

> "The press may not be successful much of the time in telling people what to think, but it is stunningly successful in telling its readers what to think about."

This is the foundational statement of the *agenda-setting function* of news.

## Cohen's Implicit News Selection Criteria for Foreign Policy

Drawing from Washington-press observations, Cohen identified the following operative selection rules:

1. **Government-Source Privilege** - State Department, White House, and Congressional foreign-policy briefings supply most foreign-affairs news. Beats anchored to these institutions confer newsworthiness.

2. **Crisis Salience** - Crises (wars, coups, summits, treaties) command attention; quieter foreign developments are systematically under-covered.

3. **Bilateral US Relevance** - Events involving US allies, US enemies, or US interests are vastly more reportable than events with no US connection.

4. **Proximity to a Briefing Cycle** - Stories that can be hooked to the daily State Department or White House briefing cycle achieve newsworthiness even when their substantive importance is modest.

5. **Reporter–Source Mutual Dependence** - Reporters depend on officials for information; officials depend on reporters for publicity. Stories that serve both interests are preferentially produced.

6. **Background-Briefing Leverage** - Off-the-record briefings shape framing; stories that align with official background framings face fewer hurdles.

7. **Travel-Pool Coverage** - Presidential and diplomatic travel pools concentrate coverage on the leader's location; events elsewhere fade.

8. **Wire-Service Availability** - Stories already on AP/UPI wires receive high salience because they are pre-cleared by the wire's editorial process.

## Three Roles Cohen Identifies for the Foreign-Affairs Press

1. **Observer** - Reporting on what happens (the conventional news role).
2. **Participant** - Sometimes an actor in the policy process (leak conduit, trial-balloon vehicle).
3. **Catalyst** - Coverage shapes both public agenda and elite discussion, even when reporters do not intend it.

## Implications for Newsworthiness

- **First clear statement of agenda-setting**: News selection determines public attention even when it does not change opinions.

- **Structural elite bias in foreign news**: Washington-centered reporting privileges executive-branch perspectives.

- **Bureaucratic phase precedes Fishman**: Cohen's observations about beat-driven news production prefigure Fishman 1980 by 17 years for the foreign-policy domain.

- **Empirical foundation for McCombs & Shaw**: The Chapel Hill agenda-setting study (1972) operationalized Cohen's aphorism.
