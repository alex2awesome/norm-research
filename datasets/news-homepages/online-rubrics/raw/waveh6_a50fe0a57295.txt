ABSTRACT 

Claiming that literary newswriting is not a 
contradiction in terms, that is, an oxymoron, this essay examines 
some of the criteria against which literary newswriting can be 
measured, defines what constitutes literary newswriting in 
contemporary newspapers, and explains how it contributes to modern 
newswriting. The paper argues that (1) modern journalists should no 
longer categorize stories as hard news, feature, or literary feature 
because the complexity of life necessitates a variety of writing 
approaches; and (2) literary newswriting is no less truthful than 
“objective” journalism and may, in fact, represent reality more 
accurately than standard newswriting forms. To find out more about 
the writing styles and reporting methods of literary newswriters, the 
following stories and reporters are examined: "The Illegal Odyssey of 
Don Bernabe Garay” (J. M. Crewdson); "Farmers Harvest Fruit of 
Bountiful Summer" (Mike Sager); "A Job Where Nobody Complains” (Paul 
Shinoff); "The Suspect: From a Lonely Childhood to a D. C. Jail” (D. 
C. Drake); "It Was a Day the Brave Men Cried" (D. Wolf); "A Fire on 
the Mountain: The Story of the Kintzel Brothers” (G. Gaul); 
"Brutality in the Home: Abused Child Strikes Back" (P. Donahue); and 
"Egyptians Excluded in Sorrow" (R. B. Cramer). Over 100 end notes are 
provided. (JD)

MORE THAN A DECADE AGO, Ronald Weber, writing 
about new journalism, observed almost parenthetically that ‘‘the other 
basic strain at work in the new journalism is more strictly journalistic,”’ 
a comment he explained in a footnote. ‘This side of new journalism, 
keeping thoughts about art and rivalry with fiction in the background 
and concentrating more on loosening up rigid journalistic attitudes and 
practices, has lacked a spokesman of [Tom] Wolfe's zeal and stature and 
consequently tends to be of less importance,”’ he wrote. ‘‘But this could 
be wg fee It could be that in the long run, the literary new jour- 
nalists, having switched (or switched back) to writing old novels, the less 
publicized changes at work in newspaper and magazine journalism will 
have the more lasting effect.”"? Weber was right, although the type of 
newspaper writing/reporting I call literary newswriting hasn't exactly 
dominated the front pages of newspapers. 

Some would argue that literary newswriting is a subset of feature 
writing rather than a separate entity. | would argue that modern jour- 
nalists should no longer categorize stories as hard news or feature or 
literary feature, but should recognize that given the complex nature of 
life, the modern journalist needs variety of writing approaches to 


satisfactorily explain the world to readers. This essay examines some of 
the criteria against which literary newswriting in newspapers can be 
measured, defines what constitutes literary newswriting in contem- 
porary newspapers, and explains how it contributes to modern

newswriting. This essay contends that literary newswriting is not a con- 
tradiction in terms, i.e., an oxymoron. 

Further, this essay argues that literary newswriting is no less truthful 
than “objective” journalism and may, in fact, more accurately represent 
reality than standard newswriting forms. Says Tracy Kidder: ‘Some 
people have a very clinical notion of what journalism is. It’s an antiseptic 
idea, the idea that you can’t present a set of facts in an interesting way 
without tainting them. That's utter nonsense. That's the ultimate 
machine-like tendency.’’® This essay does not take up the question of 
whether literary newswriting is art. | have argued elsewhere that 
literary styled newswriting is not art.‘ 

Instead, this essay focuses primarily on technique with an aside look at 
reporting. It first argues that literary newswriting is possible and then 
examines how modern journalists are accomplishing it. In the words of 
the editors of A Treasury of Great Reporting, ‘‘...to have staying 
power great reporting must possess literary quality.’"® This essay ex- 
amines the staying power of eight news stories, relying in part on the ad- 
vice of Wayne C. Booth. “But we gain one great glory from the irreduci- 
ble complexities and fluidities in literary studies: any one of us, at any 
age and in any state of ignorance, can practice the art, not just learn 
about it from other people’s practice. Each of us can work at what is 
always the frontier of the art of narrative and its study: a story-loving 
mind meeting a story that asks to be loved.’’ 

In trying to find stories that might fit the topic of this essay, I used the 
Pennsylvania State University’s main library, which subscribes to 
almost thirty non-Pennsylvania metropolitan newspapers and an equal 
number of Pennsylvania dailies. The following newspapers were examin- 
ed during a three-month period in the fall of 1981: Los Angeles Times, 
San Francisco Chronicle, San Francisco Examiner (and their Sunday 
combination), Denver Post, Washington Post, Miami Herald, Chicago 
Sun-Times, Chicago Tribune, New Orleans Times, Picayune/States-Item, 
Baltimore Sun, Boston Globe, Detroit News, Minneapolis Tribune, St. 
Louis Post-Dispatch, Wall Street Journal, Houston Post, Seattle Times, 
Milwaukee Journal, the Allentown (Pennsylvania) Morning Call, the 
Philadelphia Bulletin (since deceased), and the Philadelphia Inquirer. | 
avoided the New York Times because I already had a story from that 
newspaper, in fact it was the story that initially triggered my research 
on the use of literary techniques in newspaper writing. Except for Allen- 
town, all the cities rank in the top fifty in population.’ 

My assistant, who was working on her master’s degree in Englsh 
literature, examined the newspapers for literarily styled news stories. I 
recruited in the English department because I was afraid that a 
graduate student in journalism would bring to the search the old bias 
that the inverted pyramid is the only form a newspaper story can take. 
My assistant was instructed to examine front pages and section fronts of 
the designated newspapers, but she understood that the articles | 
wanted had to be as close to hard news (current events) as possible, 
meaning that stories in feature (lifestyle/living/leisure/people) sections 
got scant attention. All in all, my assistant provided fifteen stories for

I wanted stories which demonstrated that literary techniques have 
come out of the closet called the feature section and can now be found in 
stories on page one. I wanted stories that might be said to combine what 
Michael Schudson describes as the two journalisms of the 1890s — story 
and information, in which story journalism entertains and interprets 
while information journalism provides data (election results, stock 
market reports, etc.).° My assistant and I examined stories for their use 
of literary techniques, among them narration and scene, summary and 
process, point of view, drama, chronological organization, rhythm, im 
agery, foreshadowing, metaphor, irony, dialogue, overall organization 
(beginning, middle and end) plus verification and documentation, the 
elements of good reporting.

A Definition of Literary Newswritin 
4 Y q 


Today's literary newswriting in newspapers has its roots in the new 
journalism movement of the late 1960s and early 1970s. But that doesn’t 
mean that literary newswriting is something new. Literature and jour- 
nalism have co-existed for at least four centuries. Says Lennard J. 
Davis: ‘“The main form of journalism in the sixteenth century was the 
printed ballad. . . . The printed ballad was basically journalistic — which 
is to say, more likely to be read immediately after the event reported 
than the ballad of a wandering minstrel, which might recount exploits of 
the more distant past, and be sung months or years after its composi- 
tion.”’'? James E. Murphy emphasized that journalists used literary 
techniques more than three centuries ago.'® 

Davis rues the separation that eventually befell literature and jour- 
nalism, noting that ‘‘the history of the subsequent division of fact and 
fiction, news and novel, the movement from untroubled fictionality of 
Cervantes to the inherent ambivalence of Defoe, Richardson, Fielding, 
and later writers, the objectification of factual news and the factualizing 
of fictionality — all can tell us much about the way we now consider the 
social and political role of narrative, journalism, and history.’’!? Subse- 
quently, scholars would not recognize the literary aspects of journalism 
until late in the nineteenth century.”° That century does provide an ar- 
ray of examples of literary newswriting, as attested to in A Treasury of 
Great Reporting.”! 

Literary journalism has obviously been with us for centuries. What we 
see today is perhaps only a polished and sophisticated version of what 
was done many times during the past four centuries. Today’s literary 
newswriting reflects the process of evolution in all newswriting. ‘‘It is 
clear that New Journalism is not new,’’ Murphy said. ‘‘What is now bill- 
ed as a literary genre is the product of gradual development and a reflec- 
tion of the times more than it is a radical innovation.”’?? For the purposes 
of this essay, though, to understand current literary newswriting is to 
first know what scholars, writers and critics consider new journalism 
circa 1970. 

New journalism emphasizes style over substance.?* John Hollowell 
says it is ‘‘a form of nonfiction that relies upon the narrative techniques 
and intuitive insights of the novelist to chronicle contemporary

events.’’24 Weber emphasizes the same point. ‘‘When Wolfe talks about 
finding ‘artistic’ excitement in journalism he has in mind one such strain 
— the effort to draw literary effect from nonfiction materials, to render 
literature from reporting, art from fact.’’?5 In literary newswriting, 
though, reporting is as much of the essence as is writing style. 

In analyzing the evolution of literary newswriting, too much should 
not be made of that type of newspaper writing called feature. One 
literary newswriter, Mike Sager of the Washington Post, makes the 
distinction this way: “By traditional standards, a feature is a collection 
of quotes, strung together with somewhat simple transition paragraphs. 
The detail is sparse and usually limited to stock (what they’re wearing, 
how they sigh, etc.) and the result is a flat story. I’ve heard this type call- 
ed a Talking Head story; it reads as if the reporter had collected the 
heads of his characters, thrown them into a white room, and turned on 
the tape recorder.’’?* Hollowell makes much the same point. ‘In the 
usual news story, the basic units are facts and quotations.’’?? He dif- 
ferentiates between standard journlaism and the new journalism by say- 
ing that new journalists attempt to reconstruct a news event as it might 
have happened. ‘‘The new journalists uses literary techniques to convey 
information and to provide background not usually possible in most 
newspaper and magazine reporting.’’® The point is not new. Two 
decades before anyone used the phrase new journalism, John Hersey 
wrote: “Journalism allows its readers to witness history; fiction gives its 
readers an opportunity to live it.’’® A combination of those in a 
newspaper results in literary newswriting. Literary newswriting 
represents a reporter’s attempt to present reality by using techniques 
usually associated with fiction. The literary newswriter seeks the 
realism achieved by the novelist. 

In what is thought of as typical newspaper writing, the inverted 
pyramid, or retrograde style, holds sway, and it has a virtue. It conveys 
information briefly and quickly. It is utilitarian and functional. It lets the 
reader pick his own stopping-off point. If the first paragraph provides 
enough information, the reader can go on to another story. If the reader 
wants more, the reader can keep reading until satiated. But the inverted 
pyramid is a reality in the newsroom only, an institutional reality clearly 
bearing little relationship to the reality of a news event, or, for that mat- 
ter, life. A news event does not unfold with who, what, why, when, 
where and how in the opening scene, does not begin with the main point 
and fade to the insignificant. But that is the way most journalists usually 
write stories about events. On the other hand, literary newswriters, by 
melding the legwork of reporting, the art of writing, and the techniques 
of fiction writing, may actually get closer to reality than their counter- 
parts using the inverted pyramid — a point I'll return to. 

In discussing fiction, Leon Surmelian argues that ‘‘good writing is pas- 
sionate writing, but with its passion controlled, dispassionate 
passion.’’°° That also applies to literary newswriting it seems so ef- 
fortless as not to be obvious to the reader. Furthermore, the literary 
newswriter recognizes that the news is frequently cyclical and that in 
some cases only the way the news is written makes one story unique

from another. ‘‘The raw material of human existence remains ever the 
same, the molds by which it is given significance and recognizable shape 
are forever being recreated by writers of empirical narrative and 
drama,”’ say Robert Scholes and Robert Kellogg. ‘“The new in empirical 
narrative depends upon an originality of vision, a creation of new types 
of actuality, and not upon a flight of the imagination away from the ac- 
tual.’’*! If there is any art to be found in literary newswriting, Scholes 
and Kellogg say ‘‘it is in the knowing and the telling, and not in the 
facts. .. 82 As Louis M. Lyons put it: “... the effectiveness of the 
reporting is capped by the writing. Its quality may lift a good story to a 
great one or reduce it to run of the mill.’’** 


Narrative Approaches 


Andre Fontaine says this about using narrative in newswriting: ‘‘Nar- 
rative is one of the things a good interpretive writer borrows from fic- 
tion writers. It is telling a story, building suspense, putting your 
characters (and the reader) into a situation, then taking him through it, 
complete with emotional involvements, to a resolution. It’s not changing 
facts, but arranging them. It is plotting.’”’** When newspaper editors 
talk about non-journalistic writing in newspapers, one method that is 
much mentioned is the narrative form, in which a story and a story-teller 
are evident.*&gt; What editors do not mention is that the narrative comes 
with a point of view,°* and point of view means to an editor the loss of a 
reporter’s neutrality. But those objections should be quickly dismissed, 
for no newspaper story, no matter the form, is devoid of point of view. 
The journalist’s selection of information is a point of view that the jour- 
nalist (often unconsciously) brings to a story merely through unrecogniz- 
ed cultural bias. That does not necessarily diminish the journalist’s work. 
Surmelian, writing about fiction, sees point of view as something to 
acknowledge, not hide: 

The perception of the world around him through sense data and 
insight and an awareness of the world within himself, of con- 
sciousness observing itself, the outer vision combined with the in- 
ner: that is what distinguishes the writer, and he stands or falls on 
his capacity for sense impressions and his imaginative insights, and 
on his ability to express them in words. If he is honest, he will 
neither exaggerate nor minimize what he perceives, he will not 
fake it, he will not say something exists when it does not or deny 
that which exists. He will not knowingly misinterpret the facts as 
he knows them and mislead the reader. A good narrative prose is 
language that tells the truth and makes for a lucid presentation of 
the event.%” 

Surmelian, in fact, helps define literary newswriting (without saying so) 
as the “objective re-creation of experience. . . realized through subjec- 
tive means.’’*8 

For the journalist, literary point of view means deciding from which 
perspective a story is told. The perspective can be that of the reporter's 
— neutral omniscience. But it can also be the central character's or

someone close to the central character. Roy Peter Clark cites as one 
such example Charles Bailey’s story on the funeral of Medgar Evers, a 
civil rights leader slain in 1963. The story, written for the Minneapolis 
Tribune, begins: 

ARLINGTON, VA. — The humid haze of early summer lay hot 
and heavy on Washington, D.C., but here across the river, under 
the oak trees, the air was fresh and cool. 

The little girl sat on a folding chair, her mother on one side and 
her older brother on the other. The child’s face was blank and 
bemused, almost dazed. Around her stood a thousand others. 
Closer in, a score of men with cameras crept and scuttled and snap- 
ped their shutters at her.%® 

The little girl is Evers’ daughter, and the story will be told from her 
point of view. “To experience this event through her eyes,’’ Clark 
argues, ‘‘is to feel its full significance.’’4° What point of view offers then 
is not weaker writing, but stronger writing, for it gives a journalist the 
opportunity to let the reader experience the event as its participants are 
experiencing it rather than filtered through the inverted pyramid. 

Crewdson’s story — ‘‘The Illegal Odyssey of Don Bernabe Garay’? — 
represents this approach. His article, one of a series on immigration, 
focuses on the life of Don Bernabe Garay, a Mexican who slips in and out 
of Arizona to pick lemons. The story is told from the viewpoint of Ber- 
nabe Garay, beginning with one of his illegal entries into the United 
States and ending with him at work on another day. In the words of one 
reference book, Crewdson’s story is a short story because it ‘‘focuses on 
a single character in a single episode, and, rather than tracing his 
development, reveals him at a particular moment.’’*! 

The narrative approach needs to be distilled further because literary 
newswriters use a variety of approaches in telling a story. One major 
distinction arises in the tense of the action. Read says that narrative can 
describe events that are taking place or that have taken place.*? Jour- 
nalism is about current events and most journalists write their stories in 
the past tense. When journalists need to distinguish between past and 
the more distant past, they rely on the past perfect verb tense. The 
past/past perfect division works fine in literary newswriting. But other 
literary newswriters write in the present tense, mostly because they 
want to distinguish between ‘‘now’’ (actually recently) and the past. 
They see a sharper distinction when time is broken along present-past 
lines than past-past perfect. Furthermore, a story in the present tense 
shows the process of a news event, a point that will be detailed shortly. 

The different narrative approaches lead to still another distinguishing 
characteristic in literary newswriting, the distinction between narration 
and scene, Surmelian describes narration as ‘Paul reading the paper in 
the college library when Mary came from behind him and blindfolded 
him with her hands.” For that incident to become a scene: ‘‘Paul was 
reading the paper in the college library when he felt two feminine hands 
blindfold him from behind, and as he pulled the hands away from his eyes 
and looked up over his shoulder he saw it was Mary.’’** To Surmelian, 
the second example is present action (not to be confused with present

tense) because the reader sees how something happened, sees the pro- 
cess. “‘The scene reproduces the movement of life,’’ Surmelian says, 
“and life is action, motion.’’*4 The first example, on the other hand, 
describes an action that has already occurred. 

Sager’s story — ‘Farmers Harvest Fruit of Bountiful Summer’ — ex- 
emplifies the use of the present tense and scene to turn a simple story 
about the change of seasons into a highly readable profile of a life on a 
farm in Culpeper, Virginia. The lead typifies Sager’s style throughout — 
the immediacy of the present tense and the vivid description put the 
reader in Greg Smith’s barn: 

The dawn is chill inside the barn, and Greg Smith’s breath hangs 

in the air like puffs from a hand-rolled smoke. A Holstein stands 

motionless before him, and he strokes the cow’s black and white 

face with a rough, stained hand. A few steps away, the sun outlines 
the open barn door on the earth, and there summer lingers, warm 

to the skin. 

The present tense, in Surmelian’s view, may increase a story’s poetic 
value.*® Sager’s use of description affirms a comment made early in the 
century by Remy de Gourmont. “If there were an art of writing, it would 
be nothing more or less than the art of feeling, the art of seeing, the art 
of hearing, the art of using all the senses, whether directly or through

Journalism and History 


The last several paragraphs have developed a parallel between jour- 
nalism and history. On the basic level (reporting of current events), the 
parallel was drawn by the late Philip Graham of the Washington Post 
and Newsweek, who considered journalism the first rough draft of 
history.®! History and literature have been drawing closer by aiming for 
many of the same sensations through the same techniques.®2 

“Both history and literature’ Russell Nye says, ‘‘are records of inter- 
nal and external experience. Both attempt to gain from experience some 
kind of insight into the quality, mood, tempo, and personality of life — 
not just the fact of the past but the feel of it, the total recreation of time 
and place that history and literature strive to embody.’’®? 

Enter literary newswriting. As the examples studied show, they are 
more than fact. The writers have aimed to capture the feel of events, and 
I predict that these stories or stories like them will appear in the 
bibliographies of future history books.

Drake’s story contains drama and describes “‘life in motion.’’®

Drama brings with it scene,®® which suggests that without either of 
those elements, literary newswriting cannot exist. In the new jour- 
nalism, Wolfe talks ‘“‘about the kind of comprehensive reporting that 
enables one to portray scene. .. .’’®° He adds: ‘‘When one moves from 
newspaper reporting to this new form of journalism . . . one discovers

newswriters separate. No good newspaper journalist would place scene 
over information; the journalist would use scene only to create what 
Surmelian calls “‘a sensory representation of reality,’’ ‘‘painting in 
words.’’®? Surmelian uses these phrases to describe writers who want to 
add ‘‘a spatial dimension to fiction,’’®* but the phrases also apply to 
literary newswriting. Still, scene does not take precedent over informa- 
tion in literary newswriting. 

Drama and/or scene alone do not literary newswriting make. As J. 
Michael Sproule points out, some journalists use drama and/or scene as 
“eredibility-boosting devices to enhance their message.’’** Sproule ob- 
jects to the devices because they communicate ‘‘a feeling that the 
reporter is completely knowledgeable about the subject. . . . The use of 
minute detail adds believability to the reporter’s overall conclusions.’’®®

and analysis. Literary newswriting avoids analysis, but does give infor-

Still, drama and scene have their place in literary newswriting. They

scene work on two levels, and their absence can preclude a journalist 
from even attempting to produce literary newswriting. 

At this point it is necessary to salvage chronology as an organizational 
device. Mills made more of drama than he did of chronology, but that 
was in describing new journalism. Literary newswriters can effectively 
use a chronological organization, especially if they follow Lottinville’s 
advice to treat chronology as unfolding time rather than past time, as 
Sager does. Furthermore, as the literary newswriter attempts to 
describe the how of a news event, the implication always is that the jour-

ment — especially in the reconstruction of a recent news event — is dif- 
ficult to make. The chronological pattern removes the burden of proof 
from the writer and puts it on the events. If the events do not show cause 
and effect, so be it. 

Other elements not found in typical newswriting are part of literary

Abuse of the Technique 


One of the major issues in literary newswriting is the problem of 
writers who stress style over substance. As Walker Gibson writes: ‘‘One 
appreciates any effort by journalists to make the reading of the news 
less of a chore and a bore. Nobody wants to be dull. But if the alternative 
to dullness is dishonesty, it may be better to be dull.’’”? 

In 1981, a reporter for the Washington Post admitted that a story 
bearing her byline and the just-announced winner of the Pulitizer Prize 
for feature writing had been fabricated by her. The Pulitizer committee 
immediately gave the prize to a reporter for The Village Voice, and it 
seemed that the tragedy for Janet Cooke and the good fortune for 
Teresa Carpenter would end there. 

But it did not. Soon people came forward to complain about one of 
Carpenter’s stories, basing some of their objections on an unattributed 
sentence that suggested Carpenter had interviewed someone in prison 
when admittedly she had not. At the same time a columnist for the New 
York Daily News resigned after a British newspaper challenged the 
substance of one of his columns and after he admitted using a false name 
for one of the people he wrote about. On the other side of the continent, a 
reporter for the Associated Press resigned after admitting that an arti- 
cle she had written about a 200-mile-an-hour ride down a California 
freeway was a composite based on two rides and on interviews with 
other drivers. Additionally, portions of her article were virtually the 
same as an article in New West magazine. 

If anything can give journalism a bad name in general and literary

reporting. One has to marvel not only at the time literary newswriters 
spend reporting their stories but also the intensity of their efforts. Men- 
tal preparation is important in the preliminary stages of literary 
newswriting. When McCabe was asked how she reconciled reporting and 
writing, she replied: ‘‘It’s the good reporting that underlines the good 
writing. You've got to have the basic facts to build on, and then you work 
with language in a way that makes it not ‘fancier’ — I like ‘plainer.’’’® 

In general, any event a reporter can verify or observe in progress 
(smuggling of aliens, digging of graves, burying of a narcotics in- 
vestigator) can provide the substance for a literary framework. 
Whatever the event, though, the reporting effort must be Herculean. A 
good writer is not enough for a literary news story. ‘‘Pity the stylist 
without a story, lost in the mires of self-expression,” Surmelian says. 
“The style cannot replace the action, nor the action replace the style.’’® 
Clark, who also worked as a reporter for the St. Petersburg Times, says:

Summary and Conclusions 


Literary newswriting is the marriage of depth reporting and literary 
techniques in newspaper writing. Among those techniques are narration 
and scene, summary and process, point of view, drama, chronological 
organization, rhythm, imagery, foreshadowing, metaphor, irony, 
dialogue, overall organization (begining, middle and end) — all girded by 
good reporting. The resulting story represents the reality of something 
as reported and written by one or several reporters. Literary 
newswriting is not a question of fiction or nonfiction. ‘“‘The distinction 
between fiction and nonfiction, epistemologically shaky at best, should in 
no way be called on to justify any distinction between literature and jour- 
nalism, or literature and non-literature,’’ Murphy said.'°' 

Literary newswriting is centuries old. Once used more frequently in 
nineteenth century newspapers, the writing style was subsequently used 
only in stories on newspaper feature pages. After the attention given to 
new journalism in the 1970s began to wane, journalists began using 
literary techniques in stories usually labeled hard news. Literary 
newswriting is now reappearing on the front pages of newspapers. 

Journalists who use literary techniques attempt to replicate reality. 
They try to tell how something happened and to convey the feel of 
events. Journalists use literary techniques as a way of giving shape to 
the facts of an untidy world. “Stories move from truths to facts, not the 
other way around,’’ Lapham writes, ‘‘and the tellers of tales endeavor to 
convey the essence of a thing.’’'®? 

Some of the objections raised about literary newswriting can also be 
raised about ‘‘straight’’ newswriting. All news stories are filtered 
through a reporter who frequently fits the facts gathered to a story line. 
Because ‘‘straight’’ newswriting represents an extreme reduction from 
events to words, literary newswriting presents itself as a better frame 
for the writer to wrap around the information gathered. Because 
literary newswriting does not always use the conventional methods of 
source identification, it requires thorough verification and documenta- 
tion during the reporting process. Without good reporting, literary 
newswriting cannot exist. 

Literary newswriting is just now becoming more accepted in the hard 
news sections of newspapers. Says Clark, an English professor turned 
newspaper writing coach: ‘‘I foresee a great time ahead for newspaper 
writing. We have purged ourselves of the abuses of the new journalism: 
the self-indulgent overwriting, the composite characters, the interior 
monologues. But we have absorbed into everyday news reporting many 
of the useful techniques of that movement: setting scenes, using 
perspective, letting characters speak, using meaningful detail.'’'°* Adds 
Donald M. Murray, a Pulitzer prize winner for editorial writing turned 
English professor: “.. . the daily newspaper is extending its range of 
topics and varying the manner in which those topics are reported and 
written. The newspaper is becoming, stylistically, much more in-
