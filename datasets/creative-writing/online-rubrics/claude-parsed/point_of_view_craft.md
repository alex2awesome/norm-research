# Point of View — Craft Criteria

## Definition
POV is the perspective from which a story is told. It shapes intimacy, reliability, scope, and what readers can know.

## First Person
- "I", "me", "we"
- Pros: intimacy, voice, unreliable narrator possibilities
- Cons: limited to narrator's perceptions; can become claustrophobic; "I" overuse
- Sub-types: First person *protagonist*, first person *witness/observer* (Nick Carraway, Watson)

## Second Person
- "You"
- Rare; works in choose-your-own-adventure, lyrical, experimental fiction
- Pros: implicates reader; creates strange intimacy
- Cons: can feel gimmicky; sustaining length is hard

## Third Person — Limited
- He/she/they; tied to one character's perceptions
- Most common modern POV
- Pros: combines outside view with deep character access
- Cons: can drift toward telling

## Third Person — Omniscient
- Narrator knows all characters' thoughts; can comment, judge
- Common in 19th-century fiction (Tolstoy, Eliot, Dickens)
- Pros: scope, author intervention, panoramic
- Cons: can feel old-fashioned; demands strong narrator voice

## Third Person — Objective / Cinematic
- External only; no thoughts (Hemingway "Hills Like White Elephants")
- Pros: discipline, leaves space for inference
- Cons: no interiority

## Free Indirect Style
- Third-person prose colored by character's thought language
- Pioneered by Austen, perfected by Flaubert and Joyce
- Allows fluid movement between narration and inner voice without quotation

## Quality Criteria for POV
- **Consistency** — POV shifts must be controlled (chapters, section breaks, deliberate effect)
- **Suitability to story** — does the POV give us what the story needs?
- **No "head-hopping"** — uncontrolled shifts mid-scene
- **Information control** — POV determines what readers know when
- **Voice match** — narrator's diction/register fits character or implied author
- **Reliability stance** — clear/intentional whether narrator can be trusted
