# "Show, Don't Tell" — The Craft Principle

## Origin
Generally attributed to Anton Chekhov: "Don't tell me the moon is shining; show me the glint of light on broken glass." Codified by John Gardner and others in modern craft pedagogy.

## Core Definition
- **Telling** = stating facts/emotions directly. ("John was angry.")
- **Showing** = revealing through action, dialogue, sensory detail, or description. ("John's jaw clenched. He slammed the door and muttered something under his breath.")

## How to Show
- **Concrete sensory detail** — sight, sound, smell, taste, touch
- **Action / behavior** — what characters *do* under emotion
- **Dialogue with subtext** — what's said, what's avoided
- **Body language and physiology** — clenched jaws, racing pulse
- **Setting/object as metaphor** — the cracked mirror, the dying houseplant
- **Free indirect style** — narration colored by character thought

## When Telling Is Right
- Bridging time/scenes (efficient transitions)
- Information necessary but not character-essential
- When showing would slow momentum
- Strategic compression for pacing
- Distancing or ironic effects

## Common Anti-Patterns the Rule Targets
- "She felt sad" / "He was scared" / "It was beautiful"
- Adverbs doing emotional heavy lifting ("she said angrily")
- Backstory dumps via narrator
- Direct exposition of theme

## Caveats
- "The Great Lie" critique (Writer's Digest) — over-applied, the rule causes scenes to swell with unnecessary description
- Pure showing without telling is exhausting; great prose alternates
- The skill is *knowing when* to show vs. tell
