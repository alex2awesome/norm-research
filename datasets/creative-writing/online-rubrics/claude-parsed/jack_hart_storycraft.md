# Jack Hart — Storycraft: Writing Narrative Nonfiction

Hart, former managing editor of *The Oregonian*, edited several Pulitzer-winning narratives.

## Chapter Sequence
1. **Story** — Definitions and types
2. **Structure** — Selecting the right narrative shape
3. **Point of View** — POV choices in nonfiction
4. **Voice and Style** — Voice-finding; style as honesty
5. **Character** — Real people on the page
6. **Scene** — The basic unit of narrative
7. **Action** — What happens, dramatized
8. **Dialogue** — Capturing real speech
9. **Theme** — What the story is "about"
10. **Reporting** — Information-gathering for narrative
11. **Story Narratives** — Full dramatic arcs
12. **Explanatory Narratives** — Story as essay
13. **Other Narratives** — Personal, profile, anecdote
14. **Ethics** — Truth, accuracy, attribution

## Key Insight: Match Form to Material
Many writers force material into the wrong shape. Story narrative requires:
- A protagonist
- A complication
- A resolution
- A meaningful change

Material lacking these belongs in a different form (explanatory, profile, personal essay).

## Hart's Five Story-Powers
1. **Causality** — events linked by cause-and-effect
2. **Conflict** — protagonist vs. obstacle
3. **Stakes** — something matters
4. **Rising action** — escalation
5. **Resolution** — change + meaning

## Quality Tests
- Can the material be told as a story (with arc) or only as an essay?
- Does the protagonist face a real obstacle?
- Is there a turning point?
- Does the ending illuminate?
- Are scenes scenes (not summaries)?

## Notable for Fiction Writers Too
Hart's distinctions between "story narrative" and "explanatory narrative" map onto literary fiction debates about whether a piece "has a story" or is "just a sketch."
