# Longinus — On the Sublime (1st century CE)

Foundational treatise on what elevates writing from competent to transcendent.

## The Five Sources of the Sublime

### 1. Great Thoughts (Grandeur of Conception)
The capacity to form noble, expansive ideas. The greatest writing flows from a great soul.

### 2. Vehement and Inspired Passion
Strong, authentic emotion that the writer feels and transmits. Passion that is felt becomes passion that is shared.

### 3. Figures of Thought and Speech
Skillful, effective use of rhetorical devices — metaphor, hyperbole, asyndeton, polysyndeton, apostrophe — deployed to amplify, not decorate.

### 4. Noble Diction
Elevated, dignified word choice. Vocabulary appropriate to the matter.

### 5. Dignified Word Arrangement (Composition)
The architecture of the sentence — rhythm, cadence, structural composition that gives weight to thought.

## Effect of True Sublimity

The sublime "leads listeners not to persuasion but to ecstasy." It generates loss of rationality, alienation leading to identification with the creative process. The reader feels dismay mixed with exaltation.

## Faults to Avoid

- **Turgidity / Bombast (tumor)** — inflated language straining for effect
- **Puerility (meirakiōdes)** — pedantic, finicky, overly polished triviality
- **False Sentiment (parenthyrson)** — emotion in excess of what the situation warrants; misplaced passion
- **Frigidity (psychron)** — strained conceits, far-fetched imagery that falls flat

## Recognizing True Sublimity

A passage is sublime if it:
- Bears repeated reading and yields more on each return
- Pleases all people at all times
- Strikes with thunderbolt force rather than merely persuading
- Combines simplicity of statement with magnitude of conception

## Two Requirements for the Sublime Writer

1. **Innate aspiration / great soul** (cannot be taught)
2. **Cultivated rhetorical skill** (must be learned)
