# Renni Browne & Dave King — Self-Editing for Fiction Writers (1993, rev. 2004)

The standard practical handbook for revising fiction. Each chapter ends with a checklist; collectively they form a comprehensive editing rubric.

## Twelve Principles / Chapter Topics

### 1. Show and Tell
The classic "show, don't tell" — but more nuanced. Telling has its place (transitions, summary). Showing dominates in scenes.

### 2. Characterization and Exposition
Avoid info-dumps about character history. Characterization should emerge from action, dialogue, and reaction over the course of the story.

### 3. Point of View
- Choose POV deliberately (first, third limited, omniscient)
- Don't head-hop within a scene
- Use POV to control reader access to character interiority

### 4. Proportion
Pace by expanding important scenes, summarizing minor ones. The proportion of pages devoted to a scene signals its importance.

### 5. Dialogue Mechanics
- Use "said" almost exclusively as a dialogue tag (not "stated," "exclaimed," "ejaculated")
- Replace many tags with action beats: *Maria poured the coffee. "I never asked for this."*
- Never use adverb-loaded tags ("she said angrily")
- Let dialogue carry tone

### 6. See How It Sounds
Read your work aloud. The ear catches what the eye misses — clunky rhythm, repeated words, dead phrases.

### 7. Interior Monologue
Use it. Movies and TV have made writers visual; novels still have access to thought. Move freely between action and thought.

### 8. Easy Beats
Action beats (small physical actions) carry dialogue better than tags. They:
- Reveal character via gesture
- Control pacing
- Establish setting silently
- Identify the speaker

### 9. Breaking Up Is Easy to Do
Long passages of any kind (dialogue, description, exposition) need breaking up. Short paragraphs let the reader breathe.

### 10. Once Is Usually Enough
Don't repeat for emphasis. Don't restate the same point in different words. Trust the reader.

### 11. Sophistication
Avoid:
- Clichés
- Sentence-level redundancy
- Stating emotions explicitly when shown
- Moralizing
- Author intrusion

### 12. Voice
The cumulative imprint of all the above choices. Voice is what's left when you take away all the techniques. It cannot be added; it can only be uncovered.

## R.U.E. — Resist the Urge to Explain

Browne and King's most-quoted principle. After showing, do **not** then tell. After telling once, do not tell again. Don't explain motivations the dialogue made clear. Don't gloss what the action already showed.

## Evaluative Use

A draft can be checked against each principle. Common problems detected:
- Adverb-laden tags
- Telling after showing
- Head-hopping within scenes
- Disproportionate scene length
- Clichés and dead phrases
- Exposition lumps
