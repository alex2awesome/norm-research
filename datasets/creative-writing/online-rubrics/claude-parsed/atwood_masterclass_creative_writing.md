# Margaret Atwood — Teaches Creative Writing (MasterClass)

23 lessons (~4 hours) by the National Book Critics Circle Award and Booker-winning novelist. Each lesson distills a craft principle.

## The Lesson Sequence (Thematic Outline)

### Foundations
1. **Introduction** — Who gives a book its meaning (the reader, ultimately)
2. **The Writer's Path** — Find your own way; obstacles like fear; just begin
3. **Getting Started** — The writer's life and origins

### Plot and Structure
4. **Story and Plot** — Study myths, fairy tales, and historical works as building blocks
5. **Plot Development** — What makes a strong plot; tension and consequence
6. **Multiple Points of View** — Atwood discusses *Alias Grace* (multiple POVs) and *The Handmaid's Tale* (first person) and the rationale for each

### Character
7. **Bringing Characters to Life Through Detail** — Actions and reactions reveal character; concrete details build dimension
8. **Character Building** — Tools for creating nuanced, well-developed characters

### Style and Voice
9. **Dialogue** — Reveals character and story; must be authentic to time and place
10. **Style and Description** — The difference between style (the writer's voice) and description (the world rendered)
11. **Time in Fiction** — Keeping readers oriented through complex temporal structures

### Openings and Endings
12. **Opening Lines** — Atwood shares favorite openings; first 5 pages must be utterly compelling
13. **Writing the Middle and Ending** — Sustaining engagement through the middle; choice between closed and open endings
14. **Revision** — A fresh-eyes opportunity, not a chore

### Genre-Specific
15. **Speculative Fiction** — Atwood's distinction between speculative and pure fantasy; the historical and research grounding of *The Handmaid's Tale*
16. **Working with Research**

### The Writer in the World
17. **Working with an Editor**
18. **The Business of Writing**
19. **Public Readings**
20. **Q&A and Personal Reflections**

## Atwood's Recurring Principles

- **Don't wait for inspiration** — sit down and write
- **Read widely and historically** — myth, fairy tale, classic literature provide the deep grammar of story
- **Specific details over abstract description** — three concrete things beat one abstraction
- **Dialogue must be heard** — read aloud
- **First five pages decide** — the opening must claim the reader
- **Endings can be closed or open** — both legitimate; the choice is rhetorical
- **Speculative fiction is about the present** — the future setting is a lens for now

## Evaluative Criteria Implied

A piece of fiction can be evaluated on:
- Strength of opening pages
- Concrete-detail density vs. abstraction
- Authenticity of dialogue (time/place/character)
- Coherent handling of time
- Choice and execution of POV
- Specificity of speculative premise (if applicable)
- Engagement of middle
- Earned ending (whether open or closed)
