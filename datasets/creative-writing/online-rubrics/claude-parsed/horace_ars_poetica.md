# Horace — Ars Poetica (c. 19 BCE)

A verse epistle laying out enduring principles of literary craft.

## Unity & Narrative Structure

### In Medias Res
Begin in the middle of things. Don't bore the audience with tedious origins. (Homer wisely didn't begin the Iliad with Helen's conception — *ab ovo*.)

### Desinit in Piscem ("Ends in a Fish")
Works must cohere. Don't produce mythological hybrids — beautiful at the top but trailing into a fish tail. Avoid the unity-killing mismatch of parts.

## Decorum (Propriety)
Match diction, vocabulary, characterization, meter, and invention to the genre and the speaker. Verisimilitude requires fitness — the form must suit the matter.

## Pleasure-Instruction Balance

### Dulce et Utile
"Sweet and useful." Combine pleasure and benefit.

### Aut Prodesse Aut Delectare
Poetry should "benefit or please, or both at once." This became the Horatian platitude defining literature's dual purpose.

## Stylistic Warnings

### Purple Patch (Purpureus Pannus)
Avoid showy passages of flowery language stitched in for their own sake — they break unity.

### Deus Ex Machina
A god should not intervene unless a knot truly worthy of such untangling appears. Don't resolve plots artificially.

## Character & Dramatic Craft

- Maintain **consistent characterization** throughout
- Match characters to their type (age, station, region)
- Special care for propriety in dramatic representation
- Use Greek models as guides

## Process

### The Nine-Year Rule (Nonum Prematur in Annum)
Set work aside; let it mature for nine years before publishing. Revise rigorously.

## The Poet's Qualifications

- Common sense and knowledge of human nature
- High ideals
- Intellectual rigor
- Willingness to accept impartial criticism
