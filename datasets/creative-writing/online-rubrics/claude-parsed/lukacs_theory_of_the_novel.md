# Georg Lukács — The Theory of the Novel (1916)

Pre-Marxist Hegelian work classifying the novel as the form of "transcendental homelessness." Lays out a typology of novels that doubles as an evaluative framework.

## Central Premise

The novel is the "epic of an age in which the extensive totality of life is no longer directly given." The novelist seeks meaning in a world where meaning is no longer immediately available — unlike the epic, which presupposed a coherent world.

## Two Types of Novel

### Abstract Idealism
Exemplar: *Don Quixote*. The hero possesses an interiority that demands the world conform to its ideals. Reality refuses; the hero persists. The narrative tension is between **a soul too narrow** for the wide world's complexity. Failure is heroic.

### Romanticism of Disillusionment
Exemplar: Flaubert's *Sentimental Education*. The hero possesses **a soul too wide** for the world's narrowness. Failure is not heroic but melancholy. Interiority becomes contemplation. The novel becomes a record of disenchantment.

## The Synthesis: Hegelian Bildungsroman

Goethe's *Wilhelm Meister* attempts a synthesis: the hero learns to **adjust** ideals to the world. The world is partially redeemed; the soul partially compromises. This is the rare third option.

## Conditions for the Novel as Form

The novel becomes the necessary form when:
- The world has lost its sense of immanent meaning
- The individual is the carrier of value (no transcendent authority)
- Time is felt as duration rather than as eternity
- Irony becomes the only stance commensurate with the situation

## Irony as the Novelist's Stance

The novelist cannot affirm the world (the world refuses to be epic) and cannot affirm the soul (the soul fails to remake the world). Irony is the formal name for the simultaneous holding of both.

## Evaluative Implications

A novel can be analyzed by:
- Its type (abstract-idealist hero, disillusioned hero, or Bildungsroman synthesis)
- The relation between hero and world (too narrow soul / too wide soul / mutual adjustment)
- The presence and quality of irony as formal stance
- Whether it acknowledges the loss of immanent meaning or pretends to it
- Its handling of time (epic eternity vs. lived duration)

## Significance

Lukács provides a philosophical-historical rubric for the novel that is sharper than craft manuals: he asks not "is it well-made?" but "is it appropriate to the world it confronts?" A novel can be technically accomplished and metaphysically dishonest.
