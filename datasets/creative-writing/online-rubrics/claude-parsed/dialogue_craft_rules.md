# Dialogue — Craft Rules

## Functions Dialogue Must Serve (at least one per line)
- Move the story forward
- Reveal character (voice, attitude, hidden self)
- Convey information
- Create or release tension
- Establish relationship dynamics

## Style Rules
- **Each character sounds different** — distinct vocabulary, rhythm, speech patterns. If two characters sound identical, consider conflating them.
- **Cut conversational filler** — no "um," "uh," "I mean," excessive greetings. Enter phone calls *after* "Hello."
- **Short sentences** — people rarely make speeches in real life
- **Real isn't realistic** — transcribed real speech sounds wrong on the page; suggest, don't transcribe
- **Use accent/dialect sparingly** — phonetic spelling alienates readers
- **Subtext over text** — characters often don't say what they mean

## Dialogue Tag Rules
- Use "said" almost always — invisible tag
- Avoid adverbs on tags ("she said softly" → "she whispered")
- Use action beats instead of tags when natural ("She set the cup down. 'I don't think so.'")
- Don't always tag — pacing benefits from un-tagged exchanges between two named speakers

## Formatting Rules
- New speaker = new paragraph
- Double quotation marks (American) or single (British)
- Punctuation inside quotes
- Em-dash for interruption; ellipsis for trailing-off

## Common Failure Modes
- "As you know, Bob" — characters telling each other things they both know
- Speeches on the writer's themes
- Politeness scaffolding (greetings/goodbyes that add nothing)
- All characters sound like the author
- Dialect via apostrophe-storm
- Tag adverb soup (sighed sadly, exclaimed loudly)
- Names overused in address ("Tom, would you, Tom, like…")

## Quality Indicators
- Dialogue is overheard (sounds like real people)
- Distinct voices identifiable without tags
- Tension/withholding present
- Subtext readable
- Action beats integrated with speech
- Pacing alternates exchanges and silence
