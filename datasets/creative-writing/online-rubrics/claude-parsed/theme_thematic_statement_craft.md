# Theme & Thematic Statement — Craft Principles

## Definitions
- **Theme topic** — single-word subject the story explores (love, hate, greed, fear, freedom)
- **Thematic statement** — a complete sentence stating the story's argument about the topic ("Greed isolates those who pursue it.")
- **Premise** (Egri) — the same idea, framed as a debatable proposition

## Where Theme Lives
- Implied, not stated
- Embodied in character arcs
- Tested through plot events
- Reinforced through symbol and motif

## Methods to Develop Theme
1. **Start with question** — "What does it cost to forgive?" Story is the question dramatized; theme is the answer.
2. **Mine character arcs** — what does the protagonist learn? That's likely the theme.
3. **Look at antagonist's argument** — the antagonist often embodies the counter-thesis
4. **Identify recurring imagery** — recurring motifs point to theme
5. **Find what you're scared to say** — your real theme is often something you almost don't want to acknowledge
6. **Discover during drafting** — many writers find theme only in revision

## Tests of Strong Theme
- Can you state the thematic argument in one sentence?
- Does every major scene engage the theme?
- Does the climax dramatize the thematic question?
- Does the ending resolve (or refuse to resolve) the theme?
- Is the theme *embodied* rather than *announced*?

## Anti-Patterns
- Author's notes / direct statement of theme
- Characters delivering speeches that say the theme
- Theme as moral lesson lectured at reader
- Theme that doesn't match the actual story events
- Multiple competing themes that fight for attention
- Vague themes that mean nothing ("love is good")

## Major Universal Theme Categories
- Identity / self-discovery
- Love and loss
- Coming of age
- Power and corruption
- Justice and revenge
- Freedom vs. duty
- Tradition vs. change
- Mortality
- Faith and doubt
- Nature vs. civilization
- The individual vs. society
- Truth and deception
- Family bonds and conflict
