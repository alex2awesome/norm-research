# Opening Lines — Craft Criteria for First Sentences

## What a Strong Opening Line Should Do
A great opening sentence does at least one of three things:
1. **Introduce a protagonist** — voice, perspective, identity
2. **Tell us when and where** the story is happening
3. **Set the tone / signal genre**

It should also possess most craft elements of the story as a whole: distinctive voice, point-of-view, rudimentary plot promise, and some hint of characterization.

## Key Principles
### Create Friction / Intrigue
Effective openings often contain something strange, contradictory, or unexpected — friction that raises an immediate question.

### Establish Voice
Especially in first-person, the opening line is your first chance to show *who* the narrator is — not just what they say, but how.

### Specific Techniques
- **Contradiction/paradox** — opposing ideas in close proximity
- **Sensory immersion** — weather, lighting, atmosphere
- **In medias res** — drop into action mid-event
- **Provocative declaration** — a strong, thesis-like statement
- **Mystery / question** — implicit or explicit
- **Unusual fact** — a detail that demands explanation

## Examples Often Cited
- "It is a truth universally acknowledged…" (Austen, *Pride and Prejudice*) — promise of irony, establishes voice
- "Many years later, as he faced the firing squad…" (García Márquez) — temporal layering, immediate stakes
- "Call me Ishmael." (Melville) — voice in three words
- "It was a bright cold day in April, and the clocks were striking thirteen." (Orwell, *1984*) — wrongness in the world
- "All happy families are alike; each unhappy family is unhappy in its own way." (Tolstoy)

## Process Tip
Reconsider the opening once the final draft is complete. The right first sentence often only emerges *after* the rest of the story has revealed itself.

## Common Failure Modes
- Weather report only (Leonard's rule)
- Generic "It was a quiet morning…"
- Backstory dump
- Character waking up / looking in mirror
- Dictionary definition of a word
