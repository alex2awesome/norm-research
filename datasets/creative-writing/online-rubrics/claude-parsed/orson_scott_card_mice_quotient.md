# Orson Scott Card — Characters & Viewpoint / MICE Quotient

From *Characters and Viewpoint* (Writer's Digest, 1988) and *How to Write Science Fiction and Fantasy*.

## The MICE Quotient

Every story is dominated by one of four story-types. The **type determines the structure**: where it begins, where it ends, what counts as resolution.

### M — Milieu
The story is fundamentally about a place / world / setting. Begins when the protagonist enters the milieu; ends when they leave it. (Gulliver's Travels; Le Guin's *The Dispossessed*.) Resolution = exit/return.

### I — Idea
The story is fundamentally about a question — a mystery, a discovery, a puzzle. Begins when the question is posed; ends when it is answered. (Detective stories, "what is the secret?" stories.) Resolution = answer.

### C — Character
The story is fundamentally about a transformation of identity, role, relationship. Begins when the character grows dissatisfied with their current self; ends when they have a new self or accept the old one. Resolution = self-knowledge / change.

### E — Event
The story is fundamentally about something disrupting the world's order. Begins with the disruption (or its first symptom); ends when order is restored or replaced. Resolution = new equilibrium.

## Nesting Codes

Sub-threads can be nested as MICE codes within a larger MICE thread. Each opened thread must be closed in **last-in-first-out** order, like brackets:

```
M{ I{ C{ ...resolve C...} ...resolve I...} ...resolve M...}
```

Unclosed threads = unsatisfying ending.

## Try/Fail Cycles

Within each MICE thread, the protagonist tries to resolve the question/problem and fails (or partially succeeds with new complication). 2–3 try/fail cycles tighten plot tension.

## Point of View Choices (Card's framework)

- **First person** — limited but intimate
- **Third person limited** — single character's consciousness in third person
- **Third person omniscient** — narrator above all characters
- **Cinematic** — external only, no interiority
- **Multiple viewpoint** — alternating limited POVs

Each carries a different contract with the reader about what can and cannot be revealed.

## Character Construction

- **What the character wants**
- **What they need** (often differs from what they want)
- **What they fear**
- **What stands in their way**
- **What they will pay** to achieve their goal

## Evaluative Criteria

A story can be diagnosed by:
- Identifying its dominant MICE letter
- Checking whether opening and closing align with that type
- Verifying nesting order is preserved
- Counting try/fail cycles
- Auditing whether POV honors its contract
