# H. Porter Abbott — The Cambridge Introduction to Narrative (2002, rev.)

Standard undergraduate textbook in narrative theory. Provides taxonomic vocabulary for evaluating fiction.

## Core Distinction

- **Story**: the sequence of events as the reader reconstructs them
- **Narrative discourse**: the actual telling — order, framing, voice, focalization

This is fundamental: every narrative has a story (chronological events) and a discourse (how those events are presented).

## Constituent vs. Supplementary Events

- **Constituent events**: those that must occur for the story to work; remove them and the story collapses
- **Supplementary events**: those that add thematic, atmospheric, or characterological weight but could be removed without breaking the plot

A great narrative uses supplementary events generously without losing constituent integrity.

## Universality of Narrative

Narrative is not confined to literature, film, or theater — it is a fundamental human cognitive activity. We narrate to make sense of experience. The same narrative principles operate in dinner-table anecdotes, court testimony, news reporting, and novels.

## Narrative Time

Three temporal dimensions:
- **Story time** — the chronological order of events
- **Discourse time** — the order in which they are narrated
- **Reading time** — the time spent reading

Mismatches among these create flashback, foreshadowing, suspense, surprise.

## Narrative Gaps

Every narrative has gaps — what is left unsaid that the reader must fill. Two kinds:
- **Constituent gaps** — withholding what is needed for the story (mystery, suspense)
- **Supplementary gaps** — leaving out detail that the reader fills with imagination

A craft of fiction is the art of strategic gap placement.

## Conflict and Closure

Narratives proceed via conflict — opposition between characters, between desires, between facts and beliefs. Closure resolves (or refuses to resolve) the conflict. Open endings are choices, not failures.

## Themes and Motifs

- **Theme**: the work's general intellectual or moral content
- **Motif**: a recurring image, phrase, or event that builds the theme

Themes emerge from motifs; motifs concretize themes.

## Truth and Fiction

Fiction can express truths that propositional discourse cannot — emotional truth, structural truth, moral truth. The truth-claim of fiction is different from but not weaker than that of nonfiction.

## Adaptation

When narrative crosses media (novel → film), what survives is the **story**; what changes is the **discourse**. The constituent/supplementary distinction predicts which adaptations succeed.

## Evaluative Criteria

A narrative can be analyzed on:
- Clarity of story vs. richness of discourse
- Skillful use of supplementary events
- Strategic gap placement
- Coherence of themes and motifs
- Truth-claim made by the fictional contract
- Treatment of conflict and choice of closure
