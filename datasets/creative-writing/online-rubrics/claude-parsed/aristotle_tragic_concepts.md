# Aristotle's Tragic Concepts — Recognition, Reversal, Hamartia, Hubris

## The Four Key Concepts

### Anagnorisis (Recognition)
"A change from ignorance to knowledge, disclosing either a close relationship or enmity, on the part of the people marked out for good or bad fortune." (Poetics)
- The character realizes truth previously hidden
- Often a moment of self-recognition
- Most powerful when combined with peripeteia
- Modern equivalents: the "twist," the epiphany

### Peripeteia (Reversal)
"A change from one state of affairs to its opposite." Sudden shift in fortune. Rich king → beggar. Hero → outcast. Innocent → guilty in eyes of community. The reversal must arise from causal chain (necessity/probability).

### Hamartia (Tragic Error / Flaw)
"The protagonist's error that leads to a chain of actions which culminate in a reversal of events from felicity to disaster." Often translated as "tragic flaw" but more accurately a *miss* (target metaphor). Could be:
- A moral failing (often, but not necessarily)
- A mistake in judgment under incomplete information
- A misunderstanding
- An act done in ignorance

### Hubris (Excessive Pride)
The most common species of hamartia. "Overbearing pride and self-assumed superiority." The hero's elevation above their station triggers divine/cosmic correction.

## Quality Criteria for Tragic / Serious Fiction
- The protagonist's downfall must arise from their own action (hamartia)
- The downfall should follow from probability/necessity
- Recognition should accompany or follow reversal
- The audience experiences pity (for the suffering) and fear (for themselves)
- This produces *catharsis* — purgation of these emotions

## The Aristotelian Tragic Hero (5 Traits)
1. **Noble birth or high stature** — fall is significant
2. **Hamartia** — flaw or error
3. **Reversal of fortune** — peripeteia
4. **Recognition** — anagnorisis
5. **Excessive suffering** — disproportionate to the error (key for catharsis)

## Modern Adaptations
- Death of a Salesman (Willy Loman, ordinary man as tragic hero)
- Breaking Bad (Walter White; perfect Aristotelian arc with hamartia, peripeteia, anagnorisis)
- Shakespeare's tragedies all map onto these concepts
- *The Godfather* trilogy

## Quality Tests
- Does the protagonist's error/flaw cause their fate?
- Is there a recognition moment?
- Is there a reversal of fortune?
- Do we feel pity AND fear?
- Is the suffering disproportionate (and thus cathartic)?
