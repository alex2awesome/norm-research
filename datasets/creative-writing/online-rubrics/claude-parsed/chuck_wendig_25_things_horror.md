# 25 Things About Writing Horror by Chuck Wendig

1. **Heart of Every Tale** — Every story contains horror fundamentally. It explores fear, tragedy, and whether characters overcome them — not just gore, but existential dread.
2. **Tragedy's Role** — Horror stems from character flaws and poor choices. "Tragedy is born through character flaws, through bad choices, through grave missteps."
3. **Literary Legacy** — Horror has ancient roots spanning from Beowulf to Greek mythology, establishing it as core to narrative tradition.
4. **Ghost Stories and Urban Legends** — These simple forms contain horror's essential elements: subversion, warning, and fear of unknowns.
5. **Fear of Darkness** — Humans are biologically programmed to fear the unknown and dark. The unknowable frightens more than the known.
6. **Clear Stakes** — Horror requires obvious consequences. Understanding what can be lost makes fear effective and visceral.
7. **Dread and Revulsion** — Horror operates through alternating tension: dread (anticipating bad things) and revulsion (witnessing them unfold).
8. **Three Levels** — Horror affects mind (intellectual puzzlement), heart (emotional terror), and gut (primal bodily response simultaneously).
9. **Disgust Factor** — Horror leverages our primal enjoyment of being grossed out, a fundamental and satisfying human response.
10. **Viscera Not Required** — Effective horror doesn't need graphic content; ghost stories prove horror works without blood or gore.
11. **Beloved Characters Making Mistakes** — Tension peaks when likable characters make poor decisions that endanger themselves, creating emotional investment.
12. **Horror and Humor Connection** — Both stimulate the same emotional response and use similar structures: setup, question, twist — but aim differently.
13. **Sex and Death Together** — Both taboo subjects exist in darkness. Sex represents life-affirmation against mortality, creating complex thematic resonance.
14. **Morbid Curiosity** — Humans possess an instinct to witness danger (slowing at accidents, watching forbidden videos), fueling horror's appeal.
15. **Genre's Current Struggle** — Horror once dominated bookstore shelves in the '80s-'90s but has diminished; some authors relabel books to different genres.
16. **Potential Resurgence** — Dark cultural moments may reignite horror popularity as readers seek both escape and reflection of anxieties.
17. **Nice Horror Writers** — Paradoxically, horror authors tend to be exceptionally kind, perhaps because writing dark fiction purges inner demons.
18. **Hope as Essential** — Good and evil define each other through contrast. Horror requires hope's presence to make darkness meaningful.
19. **Modern Fables** — Horror functions as cautionary tales conveying lessons about truth, consequence, and societal critique.
20. **Short Fiction's Power** — Brevity sustains horror's intense assault effectively; longer works struggle maintaining constant intensity without relief.
21. **New Monsters Needed** — Traditional monsters (vampires, zombies) remain relevant but new creatures reflecting current fears deserve exploration.
22. **Show, Don't Tell Fear** — Never explicitly tell readers to feel scared; instead, create atmospheric conditions allowing fear to emerge naturally.
23. **Broken Flashlight Approach** — Partial illumination, wavering light, and incomplete information heighten dread more effectively than clarity.
24. **Traditional Story Elements Matter** — Horror requires strong characters, compelling ideas, solid writing, and coherent plotting alongside scary content.
25. **Personal Horror** — Writers must engage authentically with their own fears. "Write about what scares you," as honest terror resonates most powerfully.
