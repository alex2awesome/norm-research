# Margaret Atwood — Negotiating with the Dead

Six essays from her 2000 Empson Lectures (Cambridge), published 2002.

## The Six Essays / Themes
1. **Orientation: Who do you think you are?** — Self-conception of "writer"
2. **Duplicity** — The writer as double; one self lives, other writes
3. **Dedication: The Great God Pen** — Vocation, calling, ambition
4. **Temptation: Prospero, the Wizard of Oz, Mephisto & Co.** — Art vs. commerce
5. **Communion: Nobody to Nobody** — The relationship between writer and reader
6. **Descent: Negotiating with the Dead** — All writing is in dialogue with the dead (predecessors, ancestors, mortality)

## Atwood's Key Claims

### Writing Is a Negotiation with the Dead
Every writer writes within and against tradition. Texts speak to texts. The writer is mediating between the dead (predecessors) and the living (readers).

### The Doubled Self
The "I" who lives and the "I" who writes are not identical. The writer-self is a negotiation, a partial fiction.

### Art vs. Commerce
The Mephistopheles theme — what writers risk by entering the marketplace; how to keep faith with the work.

### The Ideal Reader
Whom is the writer addressing? Often, an absent or constructed reader (the dead, the future, the imagined kindred spirit).

### The Female Writer's Particular Burden
Atwood addresses the additional pressures faced by women writers — especially historical (until recently, the writer-self was assumed male).

## Quality Implications
- Great writing engages tradition consciously, not naively
- The writer's voice is constructed, not transcribed
- Audience-awareness shapes tone but shouldn't compromise truth
- Writing requires confronting mortality / what's been lost / what survives
- The work has obligations beyond the moment of writing

## Notable Quotes
- "All writers learn from the dead."
- "A book is a heart that only beats in the chest of another."
- "The thing that's needed in the writer is the willingness to look."
