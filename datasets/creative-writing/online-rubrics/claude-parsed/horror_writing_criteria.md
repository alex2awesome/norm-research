# Horror Writing — Genre Quality Criteria

## Core Elements

### Atmosphere & Tone
The dominant tonal register; established from the first sentence through setting, word choice, sentence rhythm. Quality horror sustains unease across the work.

### Psychological Depth Over Surface Scares
Best horror exploits internal fears — guilt, paranoia, isolation, loss of identity, insanity — rather than relying on jump scares or gore. "The real can be scarier than the surreal."

### Relatable Characters & Settings
The contrast between ordinary settings (suburban home, summer camp, hospital) and shocking events amplifies fear. Characters must be sufficiently fleshed-out for readers to fear *for* them.

### Pacing — Tension Building & Release
Horror requires *lulls* (where dread builds) interrupted by intensity (the scene of revelation). Endless intensity exhausts; pure dread without payoff frustrates.

### Restraint — The Unseen
Implication is more frightening than description. Lovecraft's principle: "the oldest and strongest emotion of mankind is fear, and the oldest and strongest kind of fear is fear of the unknown." Withhold; let the reader's imagination work.

### Vulnerability
Protagonists must be *able* to lose. Invulnerable heroes can't sustain horror.

### Drawing on Personal Fears
Generic fears (clowns, etc.) feel rote; specific personal fears (your worst memory, your private shame) generate fresh horror.

### The Right Monster
The monster (literal or metaphorical) must embody something larger — the era's anxieties, the protagonist's repressed fear, social pressures.

## Subgenres
- **Cosmic horror** — the universe is indifferent or malevolent (Lovecraft, Ligotti)
- **Psychological horror** — the threat may be internal (Shirley Jackson, Henry James)
- **Gothic** — atmosphere of decay, isolation (Du Maurier, McCarthy)
- **Slasher** — embodied antagonist, body horror (King's *Misery*, *Halloween* novelizations)
- **Folk horror** — rural, rooted in local belief (*The Wicker Man*, Adam Nevill)
- **Body horror** — physical transformation/violation (Cronenberg, Carmen Maria Machado)
- **Quiet horror** — domestic, suggestive (Carmilla, Du Maurier)
- **Splatterpunk** — extreme, transgressive (Clive Barker)
- **Post-modern horror** — meta-aware (Mark Z. Danielewski's *House of Leaves*)

## Quality Tests
- Does the opening establish dread?
- Does atmosphere sustain across the work?
- Are scares earned by setup?
- Is the source of horror specific (not generic)?
- Does horror reflect a deeper anxiety or theme?
- Does the ending leave the right residue (relief, dread, ambiguity)?
- Does the reader fear *for* the character?
