# McKee's "Controlling Idea" / Moral Premise / Theme Statement

(Same concept under different names: Egri's "premise," McKee's "controlling idea," theology's "moral premise," screenwriting's "theme statement.")

## Definition
A single sentence that captures how and why life undergoes change from one condition at the beginning to another at the end of the story.

Form: **[Value Charge] + [Cause]**
- "Justice prevails because…" (positive value, redemption arc)
- "Love is destroyed by…" (negative value, tragic arc)
- "Wisdom emerges through…" (transformation arc)

## How to Discover It (McKee Method)
Look at your *climactic action* and ask: "As a result of this climactic action, what value, positively or negatively charged, is brought into the world of my protagonist?"

## Examples
- *Macbeth*: "Tyranny self-destructs through the very ambition that sustains it."
- *Star Wars*: "Good triumphs when humanity unites with technology in faith."
- *Casablanca*: "Love's truest expression is sacrificing the beloved to a higher cause."
- *Breaking Bad*: "Pride leads good men to monstrous acts that destroy what they sought to protect."

## Theme vs. Moral
- **Moral**: a specific lesson ("Slow and steady wins the race")
- **Theme**: bigger concept that emerges through repeated patterns
- **Controlling Idea**: the precise causal claim about value change in *this* story

## Quality Criteria
- States the story's value charge (positive or negative)
- Specifies the cause (what produces the value)
- Fits in one sentence
- Is *embodied* in plot, not announced
- Tested by climactic action
- Discoverable in retrospect by readers

## Why It Matters
McKee argues stories without a controlling idea become "slice of life" — events without meaning. The controlling idea is what differentiates story from anecdote.

## Quality Tests
- Can you state your story's controlling idea in one sentence?
- Does the climax dramatize that idea?
- Does each major scene engage the value at stake?
- Could the theme be embodied differently and lose meaning?
