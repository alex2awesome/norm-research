# Brandon Sanderson's BYU 2020 Creative Writing Lectures: Core Principles

## PLOT & STRUCTURE

**Promise, Progress, Payoff Framework**
- Stories make three types of promises: tone, character arc, and plot type.
- Sanderson's First Law: "Your ability to solve problems with magic in a satisfying way is directly proportional to how well the reader understands said magic."
- Progress requires showing steady movement toward an inevitable, exciting goal.
- Payoff can expand beyond initial promise or use substitution twists.

**Plot Archetypes vs. Structures**
- Archetypes (heist, master-apprentice, romance, information plot) target emotional experiences.
- Structures (Hero's Journey, Three-Act Format) organize narrative mechanics.
- Strip archetypes down to core elements; apply to different genres.

**Outlining Method**
- Backward outline: define desired ending, then create bullet points for necessary steps.
- Organize by character, setting, plot; rearrange chronologically during writing.
- Handles multiple nested plots by treating each as its own story with appropriate promises.

**Discovery Writing Technique**
- "Yes, but / No, and" method: characters attempt solutions, either succeeding with new complications or failing with escalation.
- Creates forward momentum and maintains rising stakes.

## CHARACTER & VIEWPOINT

**Character Arcs**
- Not all characters require arcs; iconic characters (James Bond, Sherlock) work through tone and plot.
- Show character desires and obstacles; sometimes use reverse approach (show what they *should* want).

**Viewpoint Types**
- First person: epistolary, flashback/classic, cinematic/immediate.
- Omniscient: present narrator, true omniscient, limited third person.
- Limited third: restricts to one character per scene but allows perspective shifts between scenes.

**Writing Groups**
- Workshoppers provide descriptive feedback, not prescriptive solutions.
- Workshopees listen without defending; give feedback time before implementing changes.

## WORLDBUILDING — Sanderson's Laws

- **Zeroeth Law:** Prioritize awesome concepts; build rules around them.
- **First Law:** Reader understanding of magical rules determines satisfaction of magical problem-solving.
- **Second Law:** "Flaws or limitations are more interesting than powers." Categories: flaws (fixable), limitations (permanent), costs (consequences).
- **Third Law:** Expand existing elements before adding new ones. Deep exploration of fewer concepts beats superficial treatment of many.

**Hard vs. Soft Magic**
- Hard magic: defined rules, predictable costs, problem-solving tool.
- Soft magic: mysterious, wonder-focused, unpredictable effects.
- Hybrid systems can create contrast and depth.

**Avoiding Info Dumps**
- Convey worldbuilding through character perspective and action.
- Avoid encyclopedia entries and "maid and butler" dialogue.
- Use pyramid of abstraction: ground readers in concrete details before abstract exposition.

**Worldbuilding Categories**
- Physical: geography, climate, fauna, flora, magic system, cosmology.
- Cultural: religion, government, economics, social hierarchy, fashion, language, taboos.

## PROSE & CRAFT

- "Grand skill of sci-fi/fantasy": transmitting worldbuilding engagingly.
- Show through character lens when possible; character revelation + world detail simultaneously.
- Concrete language establishes setting before abstract concepts.
- Consistent practice compounds skill development.
- 300–700 words/hour is average output; 100,000-word novel achievable with modest weekly commitment.

## PROFESSIONAL DEVELOPMENT
- Persistence matters; non-publication is not failure.
- Mentally demanding jobs hinder writing capacity; menial labor allows planning headspace.
- Set boundaries to protect writing time; involve family as guardians.
