# Creative Writing Workshop — Critique Guidelines

## Standard Workshop Critique Categories

When workshop members critique a fiction manuscript, they typically address:

### Plot
- Inciting incident clear?
- Rising action present?
- Climax earned?
- Pacing across the piece
- Stakes evident
- Causality vs. coincidence

### Characterization
- Protagonist 3D?
- Multiple characters distinguishable?
- Believable motivation?
- Growth/transformation arc?

### Setting
- Place rendered specifically?
- Sensory grounding?
- Setting affects events/character?
- Internal consistency?

### Logic of the Underlying Idea
- Premise consistent?
- World rules followed?
- Plot holes?

### Effectiveness of Opening
- Hook present?
- Establishes voice?
- Promises the right thing?
- Right entry point chosen?

### Effectiveness of Ending
- Earned?
- Resonant?
- Surprising-yet-inevitable?
- Resolves what was raised?

### Quality of Storytelling
- POV control
- Voice consistency
- Scene vs. summary balance
- Show vs. tell
- Dialogue authenticity
- Sentence-level prose

## Critique Etiquette (Workshop Norms)
1. **The critic's job is not to rewrite but to help the author realize their vision** — "your job is to help them create the piece the way they envision it"
2. **Balance** — say positive things alongside criticism; "writers often don't know what they're doing right"
3. **Specific, not vague** — point to specific lines/scenes
4. **Constructive, not just identifying problems** — suggest possibilities
5. **Author silence rule** (Iowa workshop tradition) — author often doesn't speak during their critique; absorbs feedback
6. **Process- and craft-focused** — not personal taste assertions

## Typical Workshop Format
- Manuscript distributed in advance (typically 20–30 pages)
- All readers prepare written feedback
- 15–45 minute discussion in class
- Author silent (or final-word at end)
- Instructor synthesizes/extends discussion
- Author revises based on feedback

## Implicit Criteria from the Workshop Model
A "good" workshop submission:
- Earns close attention
- Has clear craft choices to discuss
- Reveals authorial vision/intent
- Is technically competent enough to focus on higher-level concerns
- Is bold enough to risk failure
