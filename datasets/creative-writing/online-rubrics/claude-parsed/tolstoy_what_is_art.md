# Leo Tolstoy — What Is Art? (1897)

Tolstoy's late polemical aesthetic theory. Defines art by its **infectiousness** — the transmission of feeling from artist to recipient. Provides three criteria of artistic quality.

## Definition of Art

"To evoke in oneself a feeling one has once experienced, and having evoked it in oneself then by means of movements, lines, colors, sounds, or forms expressed in words, so to transmit that feeling that others may experience the same feeling — this is the activity of art."

Art = the **infection** of one mind by another with a feeling.

## The Three Conditions of Infectiousness

### 1. Individuality of Feeling
"The more individual the feeling transmitted the more strongly does it act on the receiver; the more individual the state of soul into which he is transferred the more pleasure does the receiver obtain."

Generic, formulaic, common feelings produce weak infection. Particular, distinctive, personal feelings produce strong infection. The cliché is the enemy of infection.

### 2. Clearness of Transmission
"The receiver... is the better satisfied the more clearly the feeling is transmitted."

Confused, garbled, occluded expression weakens infection. Lucidity is not opposed to depth — clarity carries the feeling intact.

### 3. Sincerity of the Artist
"The degree of infectiousness of art is increased most of all by the degree of sincerity in the artist. As soon as the spectator feels that the artist is infected by his own production, and writes, sings, or plays for himself and not merely to act on others, this mental condition of the artist infects the receiver."

Sincerity is the **most important**. The reader can sense whether the writer truly felt what they wrote, or merely simulated feeling for effect.

## Sincerity Subsumes the Others

Tolstoy notes that the three conditions can be summed up in **sincerity alone**: the genuinely sincere artist works from inner necessity, transmits a particular (individual) feeling, and naturally seeks clarity to make the feeling land.

## Tolstoy's Negative Criterion

A work that:
- Fails to transmit the artist's peculiar feeling (not individual)
- Is unintelligibly expressed (not clear)
- Did not proceed from inner need (not sincere)

…is "not a work of art."

This is a strong claim — much critically-praised work fails this test.

## Tolstoy's Moral-Religious Frame

For late Tolstoy, art's purpose is **moral**: to unite human beings in shared feeling. The good art is universal in its emotional accessibility while local in its specific feeling. The bad art divides (élitist, decadent, sensual without communion).

This produces some of his more controversial judgments (dismissing his own *War and Peace* and *Anna Karenina* as "bad art" for failing the universality test).

## Evaluative Criteria

A work of fiction can be assessed on:
- **Individuality**: does the feeling transmitted feel like nobody else's?
- **Clarity**: does it land cleanly, without occlusion?
- **Sincerity**: does the writer actually feel this, or perform feeling it?
- **Universality**: can the feeling cross persons (reader, era, culture)?
- **Inner necessity**: was this written from need, or for effect?

## Significance

Tolstoy gives us perhaps the simplest and the most demanding rubric: did the writer feel something genuinely, transmit it clearly enough, and individually enough, that the reader caught the same feeling? Most well-made stories fail this test. The few that pass are art.
