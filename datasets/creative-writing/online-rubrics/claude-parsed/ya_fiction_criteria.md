# Young Adult (YA) Fiction — Quality Criteria

## Core Definition
- **Audience**: 12–18
- **Protagonist**: usually 15–18, written from teen POV (no adult-looking-back)
- **Tone**: present-tense lived experience, not retrospective wisdom

## Essential Elements

### Authentic Teen Voice
The narrator must inhabit teen consciousness — vocabulary, concerns, slang, but without parodying. No "adult hindsight." The story is happening *now* to this teen.

### Coming-of-Age Themes
- Identity formation
- First love
- Family conflict
- Friendship and betrayal
- Authority and rebellion
- Mortality (often confronted for the first time)
- Sexual awakening
- Mental health

### Integrated Internal + External Conflict
External conflicts (school, family, antagonists) directly mirror/trigger internal struggles (identity, self-worth, belonging). Quality YA weaves them inseparably.

### Pacing & Compelling Story
"YA readers are some of your toughest readers." Story momentum matters; baggy MFA-style work doesn't fly. Sentence-level mastery less critical than narrative engine.

### Hopeful Endings
Not necessarily happy — but the journey must imply possibility. Most YA closes on a hopeful note showing the character has grown and will survive. (This distinguishes YA from "literary adult" which may end in despair.)

### Character Development
Personal growth is the spine. The character at the end has learned, changed, become more themselves.

## Subgenres
- **Contemporary YA** — realistic, present-day (John Green, Rainbow Rowell)
- **YA Fantasy** — Harry Potter–era genre (Sarah J. Maas, Leigh Bardugo)
- **YA Sci-fi/Dystopia** — *Hunger Games*, *Divergent*
- **YA Romance** — first-relationship stakes
- **YA Thriller / Horror** — Karen McManus, Adam Silvera
- **YA Issue Books** — addiction, abuse, mental health (Laurie Halse Anderson)
- **Verse novels** — Elizabeth Acevedo, Jason Reynolds

## Quality Tests
- Does the narrator sound 16, not 32?
- Are stakes appropriate to teen life (not trivialized, not over-amplified)?
- Does the protagonist drive the plot?
- Does the work avoid talking down or moralizing?
- Is hope present at the end (even if hard-won)?
- Does the story honor adolescence as serious life?

## Differences from Middle Grade & Adult
- **MG (8–12)**: protagonists 8–13; clearer moral; less romance/sex
- **YA (12–18)**: 15–18 protagonists; first-time experiences; emotional intensity
- **New Adult (18–25)**: college/first job; explicit content allowed
- **Adult**: any age protagonist; full range of content/themes/style
