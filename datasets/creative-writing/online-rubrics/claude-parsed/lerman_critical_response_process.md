# Liz Lerman — Critical Response Process (1990)

Workshop feedback method created by choreographer Liz Lerman; widely adopted in writing workshops, MFA programs, and arts education. Defines what kinds of feedback are admissible — and therefore what kinds of evaluation count as legitimate craft critique.

## The Four Steps

### Step 1: Statements of Meaning
Responders share what was "meaningful, evocative, interesting, exciting, and/or striking" in the work.

**Banned**: "it was good" / "I liked it." Too vague. Must be specific, observation-based.

### Step 2: Artist as Questioner
The maker asks the responders questions about the work. The maker controls what aspects of the work get critiqued. This shifts power away from the responder's agenda.

### Step 3: Neutral Questions
Responders ask questions about the work — but the questions must be **neutral**, not opinions disguised as questions.

- Allowed: "What was your intention with the last paragraph?"
- Banned: "Why did the last paragraph end so abruptly?" (an opinion in question form)

### Step 4: Permissioned Opinions
Responders may now offer opinions, **but only if the artist gives permission**. "I have an opinion about the ending — do you want to hear it?" The artist may say no.

## Why It Matters as a Rubric Source

CRP defines the **legitimate dimensions** of craft critique:
- Specific moments of impact
- The artist's intentions and unanswered questions
- Neutral inquiry into structural and craft choices
- Permissioned opinion on quality

These are themselves implicit criteria for evaluation — what counts as a critique-worthy element of a story.

## Underlying Assumptions

- The artist owns the work and the critique conversation
- Feedback should leave the maker "eager and motivated to get back to work"
- Critique is creative — not merely diagnostic
- Vague praise is as harmful as harsh criticism

## Evaluative Implications for Short Fiction

If CRP is applied to a story workshop, the dimensions of evaluation include:
- Identifiable moments of meaning / impact
- Coherence between artist intention and reader experience
- Craft choices visible enough to be neutrally questioned
- The work's invitation to dialogue (vs. closure)
