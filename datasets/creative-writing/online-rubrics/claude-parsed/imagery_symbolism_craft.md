# Imagery & Symbolism — Craft Principles

## Imagery
Vivid sensory language appealing to the senses. Concrete language that creates mental pictures.

### Types
- **Visual** — sight (color, shape, contrast, light)
- **Auditory** — sound (volume, pitch, rhythm, silence)
- **Olfactory** — smell (fragrance, odor, decay, freshness)
- **Gustatory** — taste (flavor, texture, temperature)
- **Tactile** — touch (texture, temperature, pressure)
- **Kinesthetic** — movement, body sensation
- **Organic** — internal sensation (hunger, fatigue, nausea)

### Quality Criteria for Imagery
- Concrete, not abstract
- Specific, not generic
- Earned, not decorative
- Linked to character POV
- Carries emotional weight
- Avoids cliché ("blood-red sky")

## Symbolism
Words/people/places/objects/ideas that represent something beyond their literal meaning.

### Types
- **Conventional** — culturally agreed (cross, dove, scales of justice)
- **Personal/contextual** — meaning emerges from the work itself
- **Archetypal** — universal/Jungian (water = rebirth, journey = change)
- **Allegorical** — sustained one-to-one symbol system

### Quality Criteria for Symbolism
- **Earned through repetition** — appears 2–3+ times in changing contexts
- **Layered meaning** — works literally and figuratively
- **Doesn't announce itself** — readers can engage on surface or deep level
- **Specific to story** — not generic "rose = love"
- **Functional** — shapes plot/character, doesn't just decorate
- **Doesn't fight the literal level** — concrete object first

## Common Failure Modes
- Heavy-handed symbols (HEROINE NAMED HOPE)
- Symbols that don't recur enough to register
- Symbols that have no narrative function
- "Look at my symbol" moments where craft strains
- Symbols that match nothing in character experience

## Image Systems / Motifs
- Repeated images that organize a work (water in *Beloved*, eyes in *The Great Gatsby*)
- Different from a single symbol — a network of related images
- Build resonance through accumulation

## Tests
- Could the symbol be removed without loss? If yes, cut it.
- Does the symbol accumulate meaning across the story?
- Is the symbol load-bearing or decorative?
- Does it work for readers who *don't* notice the symbolism?
