# Ploughshares — From the Slush Pile: Readers Speak

First-readers at Ploughshares (a major American literary magazine) describe what they look for and what kills a story before it reaches the editors.

## What Makes a Story Stand Out

### Language and Voice (Primary)
- Josh Garstka: "It's not the subject matter but the *way* it's written that stands out."
- Elizabeth Denison: "Almost always more taken with language than with plot."
- The sentence is the unit of judgment; voice precedes content.

### Emotional Impact
- Mark Hengstler: "The stories I pass on don't make me think; they make me feel."
- Stories that center on a character's sensory experience organized around longing — present from page one.

### Strong Pacing
- Eson Kim: "When a story is paced well, I forget that I'm turning the page."
- The reader stops noticing the act of reading.

### Vivid Setting
- Matt Socia: Spatial orientation must be immediate. "Reading stories without a good sense of space and location is very disorienting."
- Even a few precise details establish setting — not paragraphs of description.

### Connection to Characters
The reader needs to care what happens to them — early. Curiosity about events without care for the people fails.

## Common Reasons for Rejection

- **Clichés and stereotypes** that feel flat or predictable
- **Poor pacing** — endings that "peter out"
- **Lack of authenticity / voice**
- **Careless formatting** — typos, unproofread drafts
- **Track changes** or marginal notes left in submissions (negligence signal)
- **Over-elaborate cover letters** that summarize plots (suggests the story doesn't speak for itself)
- **Subject matter pitched as edgy** but written conventionally (gimmick over craft)

## What "Standing Out" Means

The slush pile is mostly competent. To rise above competence, a story must:
- Do something distinctive at the sentence level
- Claim attention from the first page and hold it
- Be different — not in subject, but in approach
- Feel necessary — like the writer had to write **this** story, **this** way

## Implication for Short-Story Rubric

A story can be evaluated on:
- Sentence-level craft (independent of plot)
- First-page hold
- Whether it provokes feeling, not just thought
- Spatial / sensory orientation
- Pacing that disappears
- Care signals (formatting, cover letter restraint)
- Necessity — does this story have to exist?
