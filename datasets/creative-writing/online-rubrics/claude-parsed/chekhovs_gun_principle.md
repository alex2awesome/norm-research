# Chekhov's Gun — The Necessity Principle

## The Rule
"If you say in the first act that there is a rifle hanging on the wall, in the second or third act it absolutely must go off. If it's not going to be fired, it shouldn't be hanging there." — Anton Chekhov

## What It Means
Every prominent element introduced in a story must serve a purpose. Irrelevant details that accumulate but never pay off violate an implicit promise to the reader.

## What Counts as a "Gun"
- A physical object (Chekhov's literal example)
- A character trait or personality quirk introduced
- A piece of backstory mentioned
- A line of dialogue with apparent significance
- A skill the protagonist mentions
- An unresolved problem or conflict
- A foreshadowed event

## Quality Criteria
- Every emphasized element pays off
- Foregrounding signals importance — the reader expects significance
- "Red herrings" must be intentional misdirection, not laziness
- Cut what doesn't pay off in revision

## The Inverse: Foreshadowing
For an event to feel earned at the climax, it should be planted earlier. The "gun" must have been hung on the wall.

## Common Failure Modes
- Loaded objects that never fire (broken promise)
- Skills mentioned in act 1 that aren't used at climax
- Backstory introduced that doesn't matter
- Coincidences that don't follow from setup
- Climaxes solved by previously unmentioned tools/abilities (deus ex machina)

## Tension with Realism
Real life has many irrelevant details; fiction is selective. Chekhov's gun acknowledges this — fiction is a designed system, not a transcript.

## Tension with the Iceberg Theory
Hemingway's iceberg suggests *more* unsaid than said. Chekhov's gun says: what *is* said must matter. Both compatible: the iceberg is what you know but don't show; the gun is what you show.

## Quality Tests
- Why is this detail in this story?
- What promise does it make?
- Is that promise kept?
- Could the story exist without this element? If yes, cut it.
