# Brian Richardson — Unnatural Narrative: Theory, History, and Practice (2015)

Founder of "unnatural narratology." Provides analytical tools for narratives that violate the conventions of mimetic realism — postmodern, experimental, antirealist fiction.

## Definition

An unnatural narrative is "one that contains significant antimimetic events, characters, settings, or frames." It violates the implicit contract of realism — that fiction depicts what could happen in the world as we know it.

## Three Modes (vs. Realist Fiction)

### 1. Mimetic
Realism. Aims to represent the world as we experience it.

### 2. Non-mimetic
Conventional fantasy or science fiction. Departs from realism but maintains internal consistency. Magic systems, faster-than-light travel — non-mimetic but rule-governed.

### 3. Anti-mimetic / Unnatural
**Actively violates** the conventions of realism without offering a substitute logic. Time runs backward (Amis's *Time's Arrow*); narrators speak from beyond death; characters address the reader; reality flickers without explanation.

## Unnatural Narrative Voices

Richardson catalogs voices that conventional narratology cannot accommodate:

### Second-Person Narration
"You" narration — addresses a reader/character/self ambiguity.

### "We" Narration
Collective first person — the chorus, the community, the family — without dissolving into individual voices.

### Multiperson Narration
Shifts among first/second/third person within a single text without clear motivation.

### Impossible Narrators
Speakers from beyond death; collective voices that should not be possible; objects narrating themselves.

### Multiple Contradictory Narrators
Two narrators of the same events whose accounts cannot be reconciled — no implied unreliable narrator above them.

## Antimimetic Events

- Acausal sequences
- Logical impossibilities
- Reversals of physical law
- Characters who know they are characters
- Narratives that pause to contradict themselves

## Antimimetic Frames

- Story-within-story collapses
- Narrator becoming character of own narration
- Endings that unwrite the story
- Multiple incompatible endings

## Evaluative Implications

Mimetic criteria (plausibility, consistency, character motivation) **do not apply** to genuinely unnatural narratives. To evaluate unnatural fiction, alternative criteria:

- **Productivity of the violation** — does the antimimetic move generate meaning?
- **Sustained logic of unnaturalness** — does the work establish what kind of unnatural it is?
- **Reader cognition** — does the reader engage productively with impossibility, or does the work merely confuse?
- **Aesthetic / intellectual yield** — what is gained that mimetic fiction cannot deliver?
- **Relation to convention** — every antimimetic move depends on the convention it violates being recognizable

## Significance

Richardson's framework expands the rubric. A piece of fiction that fails by mimetic standards may succeed brilliantly as antimimetic art — and vice versa. Evaluation must first identify the work's mode.
