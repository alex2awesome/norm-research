# Tzvetan Todorov — Narrative Theory (Equilibrium) and *The Fantastic* (1970)

Bulgarian-French structuralist whose narrative model and fantastic-genre theory are foundational.

## Five-Stage Narrative Cycle

1. **Equilibrium** — A stable situation; the world is in balance
2. **Disruption** — An event upsets the balance
3. **Recognition** — Characters become aware of the disruption
4. **Attempt to Repair** — Characters act to restore equilibrium
5. **New Equilibrium** — A new stable situation, transformed by the events

Critically: **the new equilibrium is not the same as the old one**. The story is the transformation — the difference between before and after.

## Implication for Story Structure

Every functional narrative passes through (or deliberately subverts) all five stages. A story that:
- Has no equilibrium → starts in chaos; reader cannot orient
- Has no disruption → no narrative pulse
- Has no recognition → characters fail to register the disruption (paralysis)
- Has no repair attempt → no agency
- Has no new equilibrium → no closure (open endings deliberately deny closure but acknowledge its absence)

## The Fantastic (Genre Theory)

A separate but related contribution. The fantastic exists in a **moment of hesitation**: when a character (and reader) encounters an apparently supernatural event and **cannot decide** whether it has a rational explanation or is genuinely supernatural.

Three genres on a spectrum:

### Uncanny
The seemingly supernatural event ends with a **rational explanation**. The hesitation is resolved toward the natural.

### Fantastic
The hesitation is **sustained throughout**. The reader leaves the work without resolution; both supernatural and natural readings remain possible.

### Marvellous
The supernatural is **accepted as real**. No hesitation; we are in fairy tale, fantasy, magical realism.

## Evaluative Criteria

A narrative can be analyzed by:
- Whether all five equilibrium stages are present
- Whether the new equilibrium genuinely differs from the old (story = transformation)
- Genre fit: uncanny / fantastic / marvellous
- For fantastic stories: whether hesitation is sustained without collapse

## Significance

Todorov gave structuralist narratology a clean five-part vocabulary — widely used in media studies, screenwriting, and high-school English curricula — and a precise definition of a fragile, important literary genre (the fantastic) that requires craft to sustain.
