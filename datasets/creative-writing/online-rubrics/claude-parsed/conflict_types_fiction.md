# Conflict — Types & Craft Principles

## The Foundational Distinction
- **External conflict** — protagonist vs. outside force
- **Internal conflict** — psychological struggle within the protagonist

## The Seven Types of Conflict

### External
1. **Person vs. Person** — Antagonist character (most common — Holmes vs. Moriarty)
2. **Person vs. Society** — Individual against social/cultural systems (1984, The Handmaid's Tale)
3. **Person vs. Nature** — Survival against natural forces (The Old Man and the Sea, Into the Wild)
4. **Person vs. Technology** — Against machines/systems they made (Frankenstein, 2001)
5. **Person vs. Supernatural** — Against gods, ghosts, fate (Job, The Odyssey, Greek tragedies)

### Internal
6. **Person vs. Self** — Internal psychological/moral struggle (Crime and Punishment, Hamlet)

### Hybrid (sometimes counted)
7. **Person vs. Fate / Destiny** — Against predetermined outcomes; can be external (gods) or internal (resignation)

## Quality Criteria for Conflict
- **Stakes are real** — protagonist could lose something important
- **Both sides have weight** — antagonist isn't a strawman
- **External + internal interplay** — the outer fight forces inner reckoning
- **Conflict drives plot** — events arise from conflict, not coincidence
- **Resolution earned** — outcome flows from character choice, not deus ex machina
- **Theme tested by conflict** — the story's argument is stress-tested

## The Best Stories Use Multiple Conflicts Together
A character might:
- Face an external antagonist (vs. person)
- That represents larger societal forces (vs. society)
- Triggering internal moral crisis (vs. self)
The interplay generates depth.

## Common Failure Modes
- Conflict introduced too late
- Antagonist too weak (no real opposition)
- All external, no inner stakes
- All inner, no dramatic events
- Conflict resolved by chance
- Conflict resolved by something off-page

## Donald Maass's Micro-Conflict Principle
Even within "quiet" scenes, there should be small tensions — between characters, within character, between desire and obligation, between past and present. Tension on every page.
