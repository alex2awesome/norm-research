# Wayne Booth — The Rhetoric of Fiction (1961)

A foundational work in narrative theory; introduced concepts now standard in fiction craft.

## Central Argument

All narrative is rhetoric. Authors persuade audiences through structural choices about what information to reveal and how to reveal it. There is no "neutral" telling — every choice (POV, ordering, voice) is rhetorically loaded.

## Implied Author

The "second self" of the author that readers construct from textual evidence — distinct from the flesh-and-blood writer. The implied author is "the sum of his own choices" within the work. A novel can have a generous implied author even if the real author is mean-spirited.

## Unreliable Narrator

Booth coined this term. A narrator is unreliable when their account diverges from the implied author's norms. Reliability is judged against the values the work as a whole endorses.

## Showing vs. Telling — A False Dichotomy

Booth famously **rejected** the modernist orthodoxy that "showing" is always superior to "telling." Both are necessary. Authors must select based on rhetorical purpose. Telling is sometimes the more honest, more efficient, and more powerful choice.

## Distance

Authors manipulate distance between reader and characters along multiple axes:
- **Moral**: how aligned reader values are with character values
- **Intellectual**: how much the reader knows vs. the character
- **Physical / Temporal**: nearness in scene
- **Emotional**: sympathy or detachment

## Three Types of Literary Interest

1. **Intellectual** — curiosity about facts, mysteries, truth
2. **Qualitative** — desire for pattern completion, aesthetic satisfaction
3. **Practical** — emotional investment in character outcomes

## Types of Narration

- **Dramatized vs. undramatized narrators**
- **Self-conscious vs. unself-conscious**
- **Privileged (omniscient) vs. limited**
- **Reliable vs. unreliable**
- **Observer vs. agent narrators**

## Evaluative Criteria

Booth opened the door to **ethical criticism**: a novel can be judged in part by the company it keeps the reader in — what kind of person it asks the reader to become while reading.
