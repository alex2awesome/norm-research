# Lajos Egri — The Art of Dramatic Writing

Foundational craft text (1946) for dramatists; widely used by fiction writers too.

## The Premise
Every story must have a premise — a one-sentence proposition the story proves.
- Example: "Stinginess leads to ruin." (Molière, *The Miser*)
- Example: "Great love defies even death." (*Romeo and Juliet*)
- Example: "Ruthless ambition leads to its own destruction." (*Macbeth*)

The premise contains the story's beginning (character with the trait), middle (collision with opposition), and end (consequence).

## Character Compulsion
Every protagonist needs a **compulsion** — an overriding trait, often negative, with the determination that the trait will prevail. Greed, snobbery, extravagance, rigid honor.

## Three-Dimensional Characters
Every character has three dimensions:
1. **Physiology** — appearance, health, heredity, age, sex, posture
2. **Sociology** — class, occupation, religion, family, race, politics, education
3. **Psychology** — morals, ambition, frustration, temperament, IQ, abilities, complexes

Flat characters miss one or more dimensions. Quality characters are fully fleshed across all three.

## Conflict Structure
A satisfying drama features two characters with opposing flaws bound together such that crisis becomes inevitable. The "orchestration" of opposing forces produces meaningful conflict.

## Quality Tests
- Is there a clear premise?
- Does every event prove or test the premise?
- Is the protagonist's compulsion strong enough to drive ruin (or salvation)?
- Are the antagonists worthy?
- Does the resolution fulfill the premise's promise?

## Three-Dimensional Test for a Character
Can you describe their physical, social, and psychological dimensions for at least three sentences each? If not, the character is incomplete.
