# Benjamin Percy — Thrill Me: Essays on Fiction

## Central Premise
"Thrill the reader." Percy challenges the literary/genre divide and argues that literary writers can — and should — learn from genre conventions.

## 15 Essays / Topics
- The title essay defends the kinds of books that make readers fall in love with fiction (SF, fantasy, mystery, horror)
- Engaging issues of plot, suspense, momentum, the speculative
- Character, setting, dialogue
- Creating urgency
- Avoiding backstory (Percy is anti-backstory-dump)
- Writing violence
- Modulation between scene and summary
- Filmmaking lessons applicable to fiction (Percy was a screenwriter for *Wolverine* and others)

## Key Craft Principles

### Urgency Above All
Every page must propel forward. If the reader can put the book down without curiosity to continue, you've failed.

### Lessons from Pop Genre Masters
Percy quotes *Jaws*, *Blood Meridian*, *The Girl with the Dragon Tattoo*, etc. — refusing the literary/commercial hierarchy.

### Cinematic Modulation
Pull lessons from screenwriting:
- Hard cuts between scenes
- Visual storytelling
- Rule-of-three setups
- Cliffhanger chapter endings
- Match-cuts

### Backstory: Less Is More
Backstory belongs in the writer's mind, not on the page. When backstory must appear, it should be:
- Brief
- Triggered by present action
- Carrying emotional weight
- In dialogue or action, not narration

### Violence Done Right
Violence must be earned, specific, consequential. Generic blood-and-guts is boring. Quality violence is character-revealing.

### Pacing
- Vary scene length
- Use chapter endings as cliffhangers
- Cut at the moment of maximum tension

## Quality Tests
- Does the reader want to keep going?
- Is there urgency on every page?
- Is backstory minimal and earned?
- Does the prose move?
- Are scenes ended at the right moment?
- Does this story care about the reader's experience?

## Why This Matters
Percy's stance — that literary fiction has much to learn from genre — speaks to *exactly* the situation in r/WritingPrompts: prompt-driven, often genre-forward stories competing for reader attention. Percy's "thrill me" mantra may be more predictive of upvotes than any literary-critical rubric.
