Sewanee Writers Conference 2025

Everything you've ever wanted to know about the Writer's Wonderland of the South.

Elizabeth Austin
Aug 11, 2025

The hardest part about being a writer, more than the myriad of rejections and bouts of interminable writers block and navigating an opaque and ever-changing industry, is the constant self-validation it requires to just keep going.

Most days I work in a vacuum, relying on continuous self-talk to keep my head above water both energetically and creatively. I spend a lot of time wondering: am I making this up? am I imagining that this is all something I can do because I want it so badly? And then I have to talk myself down: yes, this is something I’m skilled at, a career I’m capable of nurturing and growing.

One of the most effective ways I’ve found to combat this creeping self-doubt and bolster my own convictions is time spent in the company of other writers. It’s an instant balm for my soul to be immersed in a group of creative people who all share a passion and dedication for the work we do. It reminds me that what I do matters, and the goals I can envision for myself are all achievable if I keep showing up.

At the end of July, I spent two weeks in Tennessee attending the Sewanee Writers Conference. I came home with new friends, new perspectives, and a new sense of where I want to be with my writing and my career. I also came home with a renewed appreciation for all the work I’ve put in over the last few years.

Rather than a day-by-day recap (which would get boring fast), here's everything I can think to say about one of the most fulfilling experiences of my entire writing life:

Acceptance

Sewanee is competitive— in recent years, they’ve received around 1,800 applications annually for 140 available spots. I'd never applied before, but I watched writers get accepted and attend, and I aspired to one day be one of them.

I have to be intentional with the workshops and conferences I apply to. I can't just flit off to every writing residency that interests me— there is only so much childcare, pet care, and travel I can coordinate. I’ve turned down some wonderful opportunities this year because I don’t have the resources to attend.

One of the key factors in my decision to apply to Sewanee this year was Melissa Febos joining the nonfiction faculty. I've long wanted to work with her— I love her teaching style and approach to craft, so learning from her felt like a dream opportunity.

I applied for a scholarship since I don't yet qualify for a fellowship but couldn't afford to attend as a contributor. In my application, I focused on emphasizing the support attending the conference would provide me as a writer, and the specific benefits it would have to my current book project— the "what am I hoping to get out of this" factor.

Something to note: it was repeated throughout the conference that the team reading application materials changes every year, so writers who've applied before and didn’t get a spot should reapply. Not being accepted one year doesn't mean you won't be admitted another year. One writer attending this year applied four times, so if you apply and don’t get in, keep trying!

Schedule

The program uses an app for scheduling that isn't super intuitive, but I appreciated having it all on my phone. I could build my personal schedule out to see what I had going on each day, check who was reading during scholar and contributor sessions, see which special topics classes I'd been assigned, and track my meetings.

The events and offerings were spread across different app sections, so sometimes I had to hunt around. I made sure to write out my full schedule in my planner for an at-a-glance hard copy.

A sample daily schedule is available on the Sewanee site, but here's one of my actual days:

7:30am — wake up, get ready
8am — breakfast in the dining hall, workshop prep
9:30am - noon — workshop
12:30pm — LGBTQIA+ lunch in the dining hall
1:15pm — Special topics class: What to Expect When Working With A Publisher with Virginia Smith Younce, Vice President and Executive Editor at Penguin Press
2:30pm — walked the Abbo's Alley trail and read a book
3:45pm — Faculty lecture: Holly Goddard Jones, Lessons for the Would-Be Novelist
5pm — Scholar readings
6pm — pizza dinner downtown at Shenanigan's
7:30pm — Faculty Readings: Vanessa Hua and Amy Leach
8:30pm — karaoke at French House
11:30pm — back to the dorm, sleep

Days were busy, but other than workshops and 1:1 meetings, nothing was required, so there are plenty of opportunities to take breaks and decompress.

I tried to attend as much as I could while still maintaining some breathing room, and I found there was just enough going on that the conference felt full but not overwhelming.

Workshops

Everyone participates in a group workshop where they give and receive feedback. Workshops meet every other day in the morning for 2½ hours in larger groups with two faculty members (mine were Lacy M. Johnson and Melissa Febos.) The whole group workshops each writer's work, though half the class is assigned to one faculty and half to the other.

Every writer in my workshop was a badass on the page (and in real life) and I fell in love with each of them a little bit for the vulnerability and skill they brought to their work. It was incredible counting myself among them.

I felt fortunate to be in a workshop where each writer's session included a brief craft lesson that was broadly applicable. It was the best kind of teaching— rooted in specific work being workshopped, but structured so every writer in the room could benefit. I came home with so many notes— pages and pages filled in my journal— and a head full of craft insight I’ll be applying to my work for the rest of my career.

This is all a credit to Lacy and Melissa, who created what I found to be a nurturing educational environment where I felt both supported and encouraged to grow.

Each workshop also included two fellows— one per faculty member— who met with students for brief feedback sessions. My fellow was Tajja Isen, which was such a gift. Tajja offered me so much insight and feedback into my work, and also into the publishing industry and process. I’m so grateful to have had the opportunity to meet with and learn from her.

Overall, this structure is an excellent way for early-career writers to connect with writers who are a few steps ahead, but not so far down their career paths that their accomplishments and benchmarks feel out of reach. It behooves every writer to nurture relationships with people in all stages of writing careers.

Conferences

Everyone meets with their assigned faculty member for a 1:1 conference. Each instructor approaches this differently— some split meetings into two sessions, one pre- and one post-workshop. Melissa gave us an option: meet once— before or after. I elected to meet after being workshopped so I could talk through the feedback I’d received and ask any lingering questions.

Tip: I reserved the Ralston Listening Room for an hour following my conference. It's a wonderful space located inside the campus library, and it features the best publicly available sound system in the world. Students can reserve time and select from thousands of vinyls and CDs lining the walls. I sat for an hour as Billie Holiday streamed from the speakers, pored over my notes, and did some thinking. It worked perfectly for post-conference processing, and I'd recommend it for admin work too. It made cleaning out spam emails feel positively cinematic.


Lectures

All faculty and some fellows give lectures, and I was pleasantly surprised by how widely applicable many were. I attended nonfiction lectures, but also fiction, playwriting, and poetry sessions, and I took something valuable from each. Some addressed broader concerns of the writing life, while others focused on craft techniques that transcended genre boundaries.

Melissa Febos’s “Research as Divination” explored how she comes to fuller understandings of not just the topic she’s writing about, but also her own work and processes. As someone who can often get lost in research rabbit holes, it was helpful to hear how she approaches research from a logistical standpoint and how she structures her work around it to maintain focus.

R.O. Kwon gave a great lecture on revision, or “failing better,” and shared some of her own revision process, which begins with first drafts that she is always sure “are the worst thing anyone has ever written, ever.” Hearing R.O. Kwon acknowledge that her early drafts often feel lackluster gave me a great deal of hope for my own work and process.

Special Topics Classes

Everyone selects from a list of special topics classes prior to the conference and is assigned two or three. I attended three:

  • What to Expect When Working With a Publisher with Ginny Smith Younce, VP of Penguin Press

  • How Acquisitions Decisions Get Made with Millicent Bennett, Executive Editor at Harper

  • Crafting a Book Proposal with Ethan Bassoff and Margaret Riley King, both agents at WME

These classes offered practical, real-world knowledge that complemented the creative focus of workshops and lectures.

I heard through the grapevine that requests to join classes with extra open seats were welcome and accommodated as space allowed, though my schedule was quite full and I felt I was placed in the classes I’d most benefit from.

Meetings

Throughout the conference, attendees have opportunities to meet with people from across the industry, including agents, acquisition editors, novelists, theater professionals, and small press and lit journal editors. It was exciting to hear from people I’ve only ever seen onstage at the U.S. Book Show or listed on publishing house’s websites.

These meetings happen on the porch at Stirling’s, the on-campus coffee shop, which keeps them feeling comfortable instead of stiff or formal. Meeting with an agent on an oversized porch over coffee surrounded by trees and writers and cute dogs is much easier than talking across a desk in an intimidating office building.

I met with two literary agents from the same agency, which gave me an opportunity to ask broader questions about the industry but also specific questions and concerns I have for my book’s path to publication. I found it best to approach these meetings not as pitch opportunities, but rather information gathering— though inevitably my work and my current project did come up.

Going into these meetings with some questions prepared ahead of time, as well as a log line and elevator pitch for your project, is ideal.

I would have loved a chance to meet with an editor 1:1, though I was fortunate to get to speak with Millicent Bennett after her Acquisitions class, and she was gracious in offering insight and advice. I also connected with Sally Kim, who I’ve long admired.

Publishing is rarely, if ever, intuitive, and these conversations offered much-needed insight I couldn’t get anywhere else. They also offered opportunities to lay the foundation for longer term relationships, if ever my work ends up on one of their desks.

Readings

Everyone is given an opportunity to read their work. Faculty readings are grouped in twos with mixed genres. Staff and fellow readings happen in groups of four, scholars in groups of eight to ten, and contributors in groups of twelve to fourteen.

One of the great trials of the writing life is hearing work you want to read over and over, and then realizing it isn't out in the world yet. I came home still thinking about pieces other writers read, sections from brilliant soon-to-be books I can’t wait to one day see on my shelves.

One element of the readings I loved was that there are no introductions— no bios, no publication lists or accolades. While those things matter, by not prefacing each reading with a full bio, the focus was kept on the work we're all doing on the page. It kept the noise down and let the writing speak for itself. Consequently, I remember each writer not for their bylines or awards, but for what they read and shared.

Campus

Campus is big but walkable, and everything is accessible. It was lovely waking up, getting ready, and taking a walk through campus to get breakfast every morning.

This is specific to me, but at home my walks involve leashing the dog, waiting while one of the kids (usually Carolyn) decides to join, then waiting while Carolyn loads her cat into a backpack, and then stopping every five feet of the walk while Numa sniffs every plant growing in every sidewalk crack. Being able to get up and be out the door in fifteen minutes taking a walk by myself each morning felt luxurious.

The campus is gorgeous. It feels like a magic place, both because of the energy and because the buildings literally look like parts of a castle. There are shady courtyards surrounded by stone walkways perfect for relaxing and reading or reviewing workshop notes, and though there were over 160 writers on campus, it never felt crowded.

The library also has big tables perfect for research or co working, and oversized leather couches great for reading. I often wanted to spend time out of my dorm room without necessarily socializing, and knowing there were options was helpful.

It’s worth noting that I felt completely safe on campus, even during late-night walks.

Downtown

When you're on campus, it feels like there's nothing beyond the buildings, but downtown Sewanee is easily accessible by foot. It’s a tiny town great for afternoon trips. Some markets and shops line the main road, including a convenience store for snacks and basic essentials (Sewanee also provides several van trips to nearby Monteagle for things like groceries, CVS, and souvenirs.)

The main dining option in town is Shenanigan's, a casual restaurant with basics like burgers and pizza. There's also The Blue Chair Cafe and Tavern— the cafe is open for breakfast and lunch, and the tavern serves food through dinner. There are some nicer lunch restaurants scattered around, including James Beard Nominated Chef Julia Sullivan’s Judith (did not get to try but heard great things) and Lunch, an order-at-the-counter-seat-yourself morning-midday spot which was packed when a friend and I went, and absolutely delicious.

The University Bookstore stocks faculty and fellow books plus a broad selection across genres, and conference attendees get 10% off. I picked up a few books by local Sewanee writers and prior attendees, plus several faculty and fellow books I hadn't read.

A signing party at conference end had writers signing books at the bookstore. I didn’t go because I was feeling too drained for crowds, though I saw photos and it looked wonderful.

Staff

Every staff member at Sewanee is also a writer, which rounds out the feeling of connection and camaraderie throughout the two weeks on the mountain. I often forgot who was staff and who was an attendee— until I needed something, and then I’d find a staff member ready to provide an answer to whatever questions came up, from helping me find my workshop classroom to directions to French House.

Staff also provided daily conference announcements and updates both through the app and before readings. It’s clear the staff are the beating heart of Sewanee, with many of them returning year after year. I know that the wonderful experience I had is due to their dedication and hard work, and for that I’m so grateful.

Dorms

Some dorms have multiple private rooms in suites sharing two bathrooms. Others, like mine, have two rooms separated by one shared bathroom.

The beds are typical dorm beds— twin extra-long, basically steel springs wrapped in nylon. Bring a mattress pad! I put both mattresses together on the floor and used a king-size fitted sheet to hold them together.

I didn't have a single night of good sleep, but my bed at home is heavenly, so I expected dorm life would be a tough adjustment.

Since I drove down, I was able to bring a reading lamp (the dorms only have overhead fluorescents) and an extra blanket (dorms are air conditioned but the temperature is not adjustable.) There's free laundry, though it’s BYOD (bring your own detergent.)

Food

The cafeteria serves three meals daily. The food is fine— lots of options, including vegan and vegetarian offerings, plus an allergen-free station (though one woman I lunched with noted those offerings were hit or miss.)

The real benefit was not having to think, plan, or pay for food. The amount of mental space food occupies— especially for parents feeding multiple kids— is enormous. Remove that, and significant space opens up that I was thrilled to fill with creative pursuits.

Note: Dinner ends promptly at 7pm, so if you're used to later mealtimes, plan accordingly.

When the cafeteria gets redundant (it does), Stirling’s offers an on-campus option for breakfast and lunch dishes.

Outdoors and Exploring

Morning hikes are offered throughout the conference (I didn't participate but plan to if I return). There are also guided afternoon walks, including one of Abbo's Alley— a popular trail with great spots to read, journal, or take in scenery— and an evening ghost walk.

Lake Cheston is a popular swimming spot, though I only made it over once. I should have prioritized swimming more— one writer went daily, which I wish I'd done.

There was a “lawn games and goats” afternoon, featuring giant Jenga and visits from the University’s resident goats, and three opportunities for guided photo walks led by writer, photographer, and staff member Ananda Lima.

Socializing

Sewanee creates multiple opportunities for connection. There are lunches for BIPOC, LGBTQIA+, and AAPI writers, and an independently organized nonfiction dinner on the penultimate evening was lovely and made me wish it had happened sooner in the conference.

Conference receptions offered opportunities to dress up and hang out with the larger workshop group (one reception also included a chapbook fair) and organic socializing happened frequently. I loved how often someone would approach a writer sitting alone in the dining hall and ask, "Can I join you?" There was a lot of waving people over, making sure no one sat alone unless they wanted to.

There were lots of organized and themed events, including stand-up, a bonfire, karaoke, dancing, and trivia. French House was open every night, and it was easy enough to walk over, see who was out, and maybe play cards or a board game.

For drinkers, it's open bar after purchasing a required $35 mug. For non-drinkers, seltzer and soda flow freely, and I heard mocktail options were available though I did not have any myself.

I'll admit I didn't frequent French House enough. I felt disoriented being away from the kids, and I was focused on finishing my book proposal and preparing for workshops and meetings.

Additionally, it’s challenging navigating social gatherings without alcohol. This is a normal sobriety phase I'm working through, but being in Sewanee, knowing no one, and not drinking all at the same time initially felt a bit challenging.

Misc.

Literary Tarot readings: Writer Caroline Kim offered readings at Stirling's throughout the conference. I had mine done on the last day— Caroline is thoughtful and offers genuine insight into what the cards reveal.

Recovery meetings: There were no planned recovery meetings when I was at Sewanee, but there were several great conversations happening around designating a room for daily meetings. My guess is there will be a space available by next year’s workshop.

Bike rentals: I drove to Sewanee, but if I had flown, I would have rented a bike from Woody's downtown. Rates are reasonable, and biking would be a perfect way of getting to and from downtown and French House (though walking was great, too!)

So, was it worth it?

Absolutely, more than I can express.

Writing mostly in isolation, I’m constantly reminding myself that what I'm doing matters, that I'm skilled and experienced and this work is worth continuing. It's hard to overstate the difference being among writers who share my goals and passion makes. It reminds me this is all very real.

One of my best-laid plans was stopping to see writer and close friend Andrew Hahn on the drive home. I had a hours-long drive to reflect on everything I'd absorbed, and then I got to gush about it all over tapas while the excitement was fresh (to someone who appreciated the nuances!) It kept the magic going just a bit longer.

I came home to a shipwreck of dishes in my sink, a clogged shower drain, a mound of laundry, and two teenagers whose only priority was convincing me to go to the grocery store. It was a tough re-entry, after two weeks of nonstop creative input and engagement, but it’s also evidence of a full life that I am ultimately grateful for.

I’ve been able to retain some of the sparkle of my time at Sewanee— mainly, the knowledge that I belong in the rooms where I most want to be. I also take a lot of comfort in knowing that the community I crave is out there, and that I’m a part of it. Sewanee reminded that I am a writer, and that this dream I'm chasing isn't imaginary.

Each writer can only attend once in each capacity, so I’m out of the running for nonfiction until I qualify for a fellowship. The moment I’m able, I’ll be applying.

Thinking about applying, or have questions about my experience at Sewanee? Ask them below! I’m happy to share any insight.
