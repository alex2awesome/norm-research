The Caldecott Medal

 

In 1921, publisher Frederic G. Melcher proposed to the American Library Association meeting of the Children’s Librarian’s Section that a medal be given for the most distinguished children’s book of the year.  The proposal was approved and the Newbery Award became the first children’s book in the world, which recognizes an author’s contribution to American literature for children.  During the next several years, however, many people became concerned that the artists creating picture books were being overlooked, but were “as deserving of honor and encouragement”  (Peltola 2).  Consequentially, Melcher suggested a second medal to be awarded to the artist who had created the most distinguished picture book and that the award be named the Caldecott Medal, in honor of Randolph J. Caldecott, a nineteenth-century English illustrator.  In 1937, the Section for Library Work with children of ALA also accepted the proposal for this medal and was approved by the ALA Executive Board. 

Originally, the description of the terms for the award indicated that the Caldecott Medal be

“awarded to the artist of the most distinguished American Picture Book for Children published in the United States during the preceding year.  The award shall go to the artist, who must be a citizen or resident of the United States, whether or not he be the author of the text.  Members of the Newbery Medal Committee will serve as judges.  If a book of the year is nominated for both the Newbery and Caldecott Awards the committee shall decide under which heading it shall be voted upon, so that the same title shall not be considered on both ballots”(Peltola).  

 

In 1977, however, the Board of Directors of the Association for Library Service to Children (ALSC) rescinded the final part of the 1937 description and decided that any book published in the preceding year could be eligible for consideration for either award or both.  In 1978, separate committees were established to choose the Newbery and Caldecott Awards.

The “Terms and Criteria” for the Randolph Caldecott Medal help to explain and define the requirements of a “distinguished American picture book for children.”  The Terms and Criteria are included in Appendix A.

To submit a book for consideration for the Caldecott Award, one must review the terms and criteria for the award and send one copy of the work to the ALSC office.  Another copy of the work must be submitted to the award committee chair.  Additional copies may be sent to each of the fifteen committee members, but it is not required.  The deadline for submission is December 31 of the publication year.        

Each year, after a long process of nomination and election a committee is selected, consisting of 15 members, all children’s librarians, “who are chosen for their critical ability, leadership in the profession and service to children”  (Kellman).  Throughout the year, the committee members are expected to spend many hours reading hundreds of books and writing reviews and annotations, making sure that each and every submission is read and evaluated.  When the committee meets at mid-winter, hours of extensive discussion and numerous votes help to narrow down the hundreds of books.  The voting process ensures that a winner has majority support from the committee.  After selecting a winner, discussion continues to decide whether to select honor books for that year. 

From the beginning, medal awards committees could, and often did, cite other books as worthy of attention.  These books were referred to as “runners-up.”  In 1971 the term “runners-up” was changed to “honor books.”  The new terminology was made retroactive so that all former runners-up are now referred to as Caldecott Honor Books.  Silver facsimile seals with the designation were printed and could be placed on the honor books in similar fashion to the gold seals of the medals placed on the award winning books.  Books that have such distinction invariably show a marked increase in sales.

The actual medal for the Caldecott Medal was designed by René Paul Chambellan.  He used two scenes from Randolph Caldecott’s work for the medal designs.  On the face, along with the words “The Caldecott Medal,” he depicted John Gilpin’s ride (from Caldecott’s illustrations for William Cowper’s The Diverting History of John Gilpin).  On the other side is the pie containing four-and-twenty blackbirds being set before the king with the inscription, "Awarded annually by the Children's and School Librarians Sections of the American Library Association."  Each medal is struck in bronze and awarded to the annual winner.  Facsimile seals for Medal winners and Honor Books are printed and may be placed on the covers of each respective book.  The Caldecott Medal (gold) and Honor Book (silver) seals are sold by the Association for Library Service to Children, with all profits going to support its programs, including the Frederic G. Melcher Scholarship Fund. 

One criticism, however, about the Caldecott winners is that they are often not popular with children.  Some children look at the seal on the book and bypass it, thinking it is a “serious” book.  The response to this criticism in Immroth’s article states that the medal winners and honor books “have been chosen because they were distinguished in their year, because they set a standard.  Distinguished means marked by excellence, set above or apart from others.  A work may receive critical acclaim for excellence without achieving mass acclaim…While it is an important consideration that a winner be appealing to some children, criteria demand that distinguished qualities take precedence over popular qualities.”

In addition to placing a seal on Medal winning books and Honor Books, the Caldecott Medal has had further impact in the field of library and information science.  One has often heard the proverb, “A picture is worth a thousand words.”  The Caldecott Medal places much deserved focus on picture books that engage a child’s interest in reading a story that is combined with first-rate illustrations.  Teachers today are faced with the challenge of helping students select quality books that will spark interest.  In an article in Reading Teacher, Julia Chamberlain states, “The prestigious Caldecott Medal for excellence in illustration has already done part of this work by providing us with a list of noteworthy picture books.”  Many of the award books have great literary as well as artistic merit. 

Furthermore, the Caldecott Medal places importance on the genre of picture books and establishes children as a legitimate audience.  In ALA’s Authorization and Terms of the Caldecott Award, the definition of a “picture book for children” is described as “one that essentially provides the child with a visual experience.   A picture book has a collective unity of storyline, theme or concept, developed through the series of pictures of which the book is comprised.”  Thus, a picture book is not merely a book filled with illustrations, but a unified, well-developed, exceptionally illustrated book with a story line and underlying concept.  The definitions continue, stating that a “picture book for children…displays respect for children’s understandings, abilities, and appreciations.” 

The impact of the Caldecott Medal on librarians and libraries cannot be overlooked.  In her book, Hegel states that the award has also “helped many teachers, librarians, publishers, authors, illustrators, and even those not associated with the book business become more interested in children’s literature.”  In fact, Immroth also acknowledges that the Caldecott Medal selection process “focuses attention on the creation of new children’s books and inspires children’s librarians to sharpen their critical skills and take fresh enthusiasm in their work.” 

         In summary, the Caldecott Medal not only increases book sales of better quality books, but has also brought much needed attention to the genre of children’s picture books.  It established children as an important audience and set standards for excellence in illustration and storytelling.  The Caldecott Medal also inspires librarians toward professional development, keeping abreast of the trends and sharpening their critical skills.  The interest in children’s books generated by the Caldecott Medal award for the past 66 years is our (librarians’) legacy to encourage, enjoy and preserve (Immroth).

Evaluation of Secondary Sources

 

 

Chamberlain, Julia and Dorothy Leal.  “Caldecott Medal Books and Readibility Levels:  Not Just ‘Picture’ Books.” Reading Teacher 52 (1999): 898-902.

 

Although this article mainly focused on the need for measuring readability levels and providing suggestions for using Caldecott Medal books, it provided some background information of the award.  It did provide me with one concept for the impact of the award on library and information science. 

 

Fiore, Carole.  “Life on the Caldecott Committee.”  Wilson Library Bulletin 68 (1993): 43-45.

 

I found this article to be very helpful and interesting.  Fiore gave honest insight into the selection process of the Caldecott Medal award.  She candidly describes, in journal fashion, how she dedicated nearly two years of her life reading, evaluating, and discussing over 500 books, which finally led to the selection of one book to be named the Caldecott Medal winner. 

 

Hegel, Claudette.  Newbery and Caldecott Trivia and More for Everyday of the Year.  Englewood, CO:  Libraries Unlimited.  2003.

 

This book is actually an idea book for teachers to use in the classroom.  The foreword and introduction provided some background information, history of the award, and information about Randolph Caldecott, the award’s namesake.  Although I learned much about Caldecott, the person, I chose not to focus on the person, but on the award.  So, the information ended up “on the cutting room floor.” 

 

Immroth, Barbara Froling.  “The Newbery and Caldecott Awards.”  The Newbery and Caldecott Awards:  A Complete Listing.  Chicago:  American Library Association, 1990. 

 

I got a lot of information from this source.  I found information about the actual design and inscription of the medal.  Deeper insight is given into the selection and final voting process.  Understandably, this source was published by the American Library Association, the organization responsible for selecting the award.  A very authoritative and critical source.

 

Peltola, Bette.  “Newbery and Caldecott Awards:  Authorization and Terms.” The Newbery and Caldecott Awards:  A Guide to the Medal and Honor Books.  Chicago: American Library Association, 2003:  1-9.

 

Much like the source listed above, this one was very useful and credible.  Again, it was published by the American Library Association, the authority on the Caldecott Medal.  The official terms and criteria are plainly stated.  Here, Peltola gave insight into the significance of the awards and into the evaluation of children’s literature.

Appendix A

 

Terms and Criteria

Randolph Caldecott Medal


TERMS

1. The Medal shall be awarded annually to the artist of the most distinguished American picture book for children published in the United States during the preceding year. There are no limitations as to the character of the picture book except that the illustrations be original work. Honor Books may be named. These shall be books that are also truly distinguished.

2. The Award is restricted to artists who are citizens or residents of the United States. 

3. The committee in its deliberations is to consider only the books eligible for the award, as specified in the terms.

 

DEFINITIONS

1. A "picture book for children" as distinguished from other books with illustrations, is one that essentially provides the child with a visual experience. A picture book has a collective unity of story line, theme, or concept, developed through the series of pictures of which the book is comprised.

2. A "picture book for children" is one for which children are a potential audience. The book displays respect for children's understandings, abilities, and appreciations. Children are defined as persons of ages up to and including fourteen and picture books for this entire age range are to be considered. 

3. "Distinguished" is defined as 

marked by eminence and distinction: noted for significant achievement

marked by excellence in quality

marked by conspicuous excellence or eminence

individually distinct 

4. The artist is the illustrator or co-illustrators. The artist may be awarded the medal posthumously.

5. "Original Work" means that illustrations reprinted or compiled from other sources are not eligible. 

6. "American picture book in the United States" specifies that books originally published in other countries are not eligible. 

7. "Published . . . in the preceding year" means that the book has a publication date in that year, was available for purchase in that year, and has a copyright date no later than that year. A book might have a copyright date prior to the year under consideration but, for various reasons, was not published until the year under consideration. If a book is published prior to its year of copyright as stated in the book, it shall be considered in its year of copyright as stated in the book. The intent of the definition is that every
book be eligible for consideration, but that no book be considered in more than one year.

8. "Resident" specifies that author has established and maintained residence in the United States as distinct from being a casual or occasional visitor.

9.The term, "only the books eligible for the Award," specifies that the committee is not to consider the entire body of the work by an artist or whether the artist has previously won the award. The committee's decision is to be made following deliberation about books of the specified calendar year.

 

CRITERIA

1. In identifying a distinguished picture in a book for children, 

a. Committee members need to consider: 

Excellence of execution in the artistic technique employed;

Excellence of pictorial interpretation of story, theme, or concept; of appropriateness of style of illustration to the story, theme or concept; of delineation of plot, theme, characters, setting mood or information through the pictures. 

b. Committee members must consider excellence of presentation in recognition of a child audience. 

2. The only limitation to graphic form is that the form must be one which may be used in a picture book. The book must be a self-contained entity, not dependent on other media (i.e., sound or film equipment) for its enjoyment.

3. Each book is to be considered as a picture book. The committee is to make its decision primarily on the illustration, but other components of a book are to be considered especially when they make a book less effective as a children's picture book. Such other components might include the written text, the overall design of the book, etc.  

Note: The committee should keep in mind that the award is for distinguished illustrations in a picture book and for excellence of pictorial presentation for children. The award is not for didactic intent or for popularity.

Adopted by the ALSC Board, January 1978. Revised, Midwinter 1987.
