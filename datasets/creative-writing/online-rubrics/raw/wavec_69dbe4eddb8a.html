Beverly Cleary always saw herself as being a writer one day. Over the years, she kept a journal, in which she wrote dreams, frustrations, and thoughts. She finally began writing with the hopes of publishing in 1950. Cleary says in interviews that she would normally write in the morning while making bread. She did not write with the intention of teaching children anything. Her writing is simply for child enjoyment, so she puts plenty of humor in her books, writes on topics that she knows they will enjoy, and keeps it simple.   

Beverly Cleary believes that writing is improved through reading. Growing up, she loved reading, especially when her mother read aloud to her and her father. When children have written letters to her asking for tips about writing she has told them that if they just read a wide variety of books while growing up, when the time comes writing will come naturally. 
There are a few writing styles that are common to Beverly Cleay's books:

Ramona Quimby, Age 8 and "Thoughtshots"

There are many times in her novels that Cleary shares the thoughts of her characters through "thoughtshots", or passages that solely serve to express the inner most thoughts of characters. This technique allows readers to know feelings of characters without having to use dialogue. 

Excerpt from page 66 expressing a "thoughtshot" from Ramona Quimby, Age 8:
 
Ramona began to calm down and feel a little better.  Maybe Mrs. Kemp would let her wash her hair after school.  She could let Willa Jean pretend she was working in a beauty shop and not say anything about her Sustained Silent Reading.  One of these days Willa Jean was sure to catch on that she was just reading a book, and Ramona wanted to postpone that times as long as possible.

Dear Mr. Henshaw and Letter Form

Beverly Cleary was inspired to write this book when two boys asked her to write a book about a boy whose parents were divorce. She writes this book as a series of letter correspondences between a young boy and his favorite author. Just as the boy matures, his letters do too. 


Excerpt from page 37 illustrating the letter style used throughout Dr. Mr. Henshaw:                                                                  December 21 
Dear Mr. Henshaw,
          I got your postcard with the picture of the bears.  Maybe I'll do what you said and pretend my diary is a letter to somebody.  I suppose i could pretend to write to Dad, but I used to write to him and he never answered.  Maybe I'll pretend I am writing to you because when I answered all your questions, I got the habit of beginning, "Dear Mr. Henshaw."  Don't worry.  I won't sent it to you.
           Thanks for the tip.  I know you're busy.
                                          Your grateful friend,
                                               Leigh Botts

Henry Huggins and "Seeing through the Protagonist's point if view."

In the book, Henry Huggins, Beverly Cleary includes text breaks from the story that include other texts and reading materials that the protagonist see's or is participating in such as plays or reading a sign at the pet store.

Excerpt from page 32 demonstrating  "seeing through the Protagonists point of view" example from Henry Huggins:

One Friday when Henry went to the pet store he saw a sign that read:
