How to Write Flash Fiction

The Basics

The information below is intended as a general guide for writing flash fiction of 1,000 words or less. While everyone’s process is unique, the tips below are meant to be a good starting point for anyone just starting out writing short stories, as well as any writer looking to hone their craft further.

What is Flash Fiction?

Just like flash fiction’s shorter sibling microfiction, there are many different guidelines to define flash fiction. NYC Midnight, as well as many contemporary literary magazines, define flash fiction as a story of a thousand words or less. 

But although flash fiction keeps things brief, it’s more than a snapshot or gesture of a story. Each piece of complete flash fiction still has a polished narrative arc. The stories are self-contained. To feel whole, they don’t need more context or backstory than what’s already provided within the thousand words, and it’s not an excerpt from a longer piece. With limited space and a narrow scope, flash fiction still manages to capture a complete narrative arc.

How is Flash Fiction Different?

Flash fiction shares a lot of common ground with microfiction, longer short stories, and even novels. Like long-form fiction, thousand-word short stories…

  • …focus on a complete narrative arc. 

  • …contain a beginning, middle, and end. 

  • …have characters and a plot that balance each other out to compel readers forward. Flash fiction isn’t just plot or character; a good story balances the two, giving us plot points that a character we care about moves through (more on that later).

  • …thrives with any genre. Regardless of the genre assigned to you, you can write great flash fiction within those parameters. 

However, as flash fiction is substantially shorter, there are crucial key differences worth noting. 

For starters, novels and novellas encompass broader scopes. They typically have multiple threads, subplots, side characters, and settings. Long-form fiction relies on a complex narrative and has open-ended space to explore nuances and side stories that build out a rich tapestry.

Stories of a thousand words or less have no space for those additional threads and tendrils. In this style of story, less is more. A simple plot works in flash fiction more than a complex one. Unlike long-form fiction, flash fiction generally has just one narrative thread, and is a laser-focused story with a limited cast of characters - think a limit of three or four before the story gets muddy.

Like microfiction, brevity is extremely important. A streamlined plot with minimal backstory and exposition serves the form much better than a more complex plot does. But while it sticks to the one-arc format, you have plenty more space to explore. 

To get a better understanding of how these three forms (long-form, flash fiction, and microfiction) differ, let’s see a synopsis using the example of The Great Gatsby by F. Scott Fitzgerald, retold three ways:

NOVEL PLOT: 

Nick Carraway has just moved to New York and befriends his wealthy neighbor, Gatsby, who’s in love with Nick’s cousin, Daisy. When Daisy accidentally kills a woman, Gatsby lies to protect her, only to be murdered by the dead woman’s husband.

FLASH FICTION PLOT:

Gatsby has always loved Daisy from afar. When Daisy accidentally kills someone, he decides to take the blame.

MICROFICTION PLOT:

Gatsby watches a green light across the water, longing for Daisy.

In short, a novel is typically a longer and more complex story, flash fiction captures a slice of interactions and situations, and microfiction is a lone interaction or snapshot of time. 

In flash fiction, you can explore a broad time span - and, if you’re lucky, maybe even multiple plot twists.

Balancing Character and Plot

Good fiction of all lengths balances focus on the character and focus on the plot. To know how to balance these two, it helps to have a clear understanding of what they are in the first place.

What is Plot?

A plot is the structure and skeleton of a story. It’s a series of points that move us from the beginning through to the end. Perhaps the best way to explain it is that plot is when something changes for your characters. Spurred by an initial change, the plot begins when characters have to wade through a situation to reach a conclusion - a changed state. 

But it’s more than a series of disparate points in time. Plot points build on one another, inform one another, and grow based on what happened before and what is upcoming. A good plot in a piece of flash fiction is streamlined and focused, taking a character through a growth arc (for better or worse) that changes their life, mentality, or circumstances from the beginning and into the end.

For instance, a plot that doesn’t build on itself may have bullet points that look like this:

  • June goes to her ENG-101 class; she makes small talk with her classmates.

  • She then goes home, and as she’s walking back to her dorm, someone throws a watermelon out the window. It splatters on her clothes.

  • That night, she goes on a date and it’s terrible; she watches TV home alone, and it makes her happy.

Because none of those points build on one another, we aren’t as invested in why this date and June’s alone time at the end matter. Instead, if each point informed the next, the plot may look more like this:

  • June goes to her ENG-101 class; a classmate asks her out.

  • He gestures to his gym bag with a fresh change of clothes and mentions hitting the gym after they go out. Even though he annoys June, she can’t think of a good reason to say no, so she agrees.

  • After class, she and her classmate are walking to a bar off campus when someone throws a watermelon out a window, and it splats all over her clothes.

  • She asks for his spare shirt, but he just laughs at her instead. At the bar, he chews with his mouth open and isn’t a good listener.

  • Sticky with watermelon juice, June cuts the date short, heads home, and gets into PJs to watch TV by herself - happy to be alone.

When a plot builds on itself, we see why June does the things she does and, likewise, get invested in her as a person - which is how we build a compelling character. 

What defines character?

Your characters are the people (or animals or sentient objects) that interact with your plot. They take active roles in the story. Rather than the plot happening to them, think of it instead as them happening to the plot.

Often, beginner writers will lean heavily on plot, and the characters’ personalities will fall to the wayside. But despite space limitations, a thousand-word story has ample room for character development, especially when characters are seen as real and complete beings. As you draft, consider the kind of person your character is. And with each action you write them doing, ask how it aligns with the personality you’ve crafted - and how that character would respond to each new obstacle.

Physical description can never do the work that a crafted personality in context can. Rather than spending too much time on physical details, the character you build through action and reaction will jump to life on the page while staying firmly in the word count.

Limit your narrative POVs

Some writers have great success using multiple viewpoints in flash fiction, but it’s generally difficult to pull off. The divided POV can add some great energy to mysteries, romances, and thrillers, but be careful. Less space with one character means less time to craft a memorable personality. If you opt for multiple viewpoints, consider limiting it to two in a piece this short.  Dividing the perspective is something that a thousand words can do great with, so long as you’re judicious with your word choices.

Fewer characters

Limit named characters. Generally speaking, thousand-word flash fiction works best with as few characters as possible - especially named characters. If a character doesn’t have importance in the story - say, the guy in front of your MC (main character) at the coffee shop who nabs the last of your lead’s favorite pastry, adding to your MC’s already bad day but otherwise never appearing again - then he probably doesn’t need a name. Names can seem clarifying at first glance, but too many names dilute the focus, splitting your readers’ attention and diverting them to extraneous details. By only naming the people who have bearing on the plot, you’re keeping a tight grip on where your readers’ attention wanders.

Less is More

Writing short fiction isn’t a marathon. When writing something that excites us, it’s incredibly tempting to give into all the side quests and secondary characters that pop into our minds. And a thousand words may sound vast, but in reality, it’s only a few pages to work with.

Contain your story to just one single focus. A piece of only a thousand words is at its best with a limited scope. Instead of bogging the story down with too many things and details that will dilute the overall focus, fewer plot points will help create a compelling story.

Keep it simple

Even if the prompt you get triggers inspiration for an epic saga, for a thousand-word story, simplicity is your best bet. When you’re editing, look for new and complicated directions the story goes and ask yourself if it’s absolutely essential to the narrative arc. Usually, those side quests aren’t as important as the core story itself.

Shoving too many plot points into a thousand words may look more like a summary than a story - and although summaries give readers the gist, they don’t command readers’ attention. If you’ve written a draft that feels too complex and rushed - and you still haven’t answered the story’s driving question, look at your piece and ask what you can remove.

If it’s possible, don’t add to your story. Adding things is rarely the way to bring about solutions or a satisfying conclusion. Instead, while looking over your story (or your outline, or just the idea cooking in your head), ask yourself what you might subtract, even if it’s a painful option. The adage “kill your darlings” is as common as it is for a reason. Even if we love a segment, it may not have a place in your piece or contribute to the overall arc. To make cutting those segments easier, move them to a separate document so you can revisit them as standalone stories or parts of something longer later. 

By asking what you can subtract while still maintaining the core plot and character development, you’re taking a fresh look at potential redundancy. Whittle your story down. Choose one facet you believe you can do justice to in a thousand words and explore that in depth. Your story - and your readers - will thank you.

Skip subplots

A subplot is a side story that isn’t directly tied to the main plot. For instance, a story about two spies may include some budding flirtation as a subplot. Perhaps it comes up in their banter and pays off after the story’s climax: the action is over, the bad guy is defeated, and the two spies kiss. While this works in a novel or even longer short stories, subplots are tricky in stories of a thousand words or less. 

Subplots in longer pieces may be fun and satisfying, but they typically aren’t rewarding in a piece so short. If anything, a poorly fleshed-out subplot can feel like a cheap distraction that takes away from the story itself. If it’s rushed, the payoff isn’t very rewarding. If your spy story only has one or two lines of banter establishing the romance side plot, adding that kiss at the end won’t elicit any emotional reaction in your readers (even if you see the whole juicy saga clear as a movie in your head).

If you do choose to incorporate a subplot, make sure it never is at the expense of the key narrative arc. Pausing for banter to develop a relationship only works if the pacing of the main plot is still pulsing behind the scene. While some writers surely break this rule with great success, they most likely have been writing long enough to know exactly how to break it well. For those just starting out, you’re better off shaving your story down to the basics - the plot itself. 

Be careful with backstory

A thousand words may seem like a lot of space, but a novice writer’s biggest pitfall may just be giving too much backstory. Although a few nuggets of context are vital clues to establish a character’s skin in the game, beware of infodumping. Too much information arrests the plot without actually adding substance to your story.

For instance, if you’re writing a ghost story and your character Jen won a hot-dog-eating contest in her twenties, that may not be essential information to include if your story features her trying to escape from a haunted house. On the other hand, if Jen befriends the ghost with her incredible eating skills, then maybe Jen’s hot dog eating is a crucial piece of backstory to incorporate.  

Be judicious. Restrain your backstory elements only to what’s absolutely necessary. Focus on details that drive the story forward, emphasize the central plot, and build a rounded character or setting. Everything else is just fluff.

First Draft & Trimming

Most well-established writers agree: don’t self-edit as you’re writing your first draft. In your early draft, loosely adhere to the word count by keeping your side stories, cast of characters, and backstories to a minimum, but expend the bulk of your first-draft energy on getting the story down. As you write, let your characters and their plot surprise you, and don’t be afraid to move in strange or unanticipated directions.

But on the second pass, dive in with an eagle eye. Now’s the time to focus on getting your story within the word count. In addition to all the advice above about side characters and subplots, look at each plot point in your finished first draft and ask yourself these questions at every single turn:

  1. How does this scene serve the arc? 

  2. How does this scene push my characters to grow?

  3. How does this scene push the story forward?

  4. Does the setting challenge the characters? Does it spur them into action/reaction? How?

Anytime you come across a scene that doesn’t answer those questions to your satisfaction, explore what can be omitted, pared down, or reworked to fit the bill.

Keep relevant descriptions only

Better yet, infuse description into action. A paragraph establishing our understanding of a city may work in generating an image, but when you’ve only got a handful of words, lengthy descriptions do little to compel readers without plot or character-centric context. 

Setting details thrive when woven into action or character response. If you want to describe a city, do so by showing your characters walking through it, buying a beer, throwing out their recycling - anything that captures how they interact with the environment around them. 

Utilizing description in action, rather than employing it as a separate and distinct paragraph, can captivate your readers in the tight thousand-word limit.

Likewise, physical descriptions of characters are often secondary in importance to the power of action, reaction, and inner thoughts. These non-visual details shed more light on a character than any other detail could. If physical descriptions feel essential to your personal writing style, take the same approach as is used in setting descriptions above. Ask how physical descriptions of your characters can be interacted with - not just observed but actively participated with in the story.

Dialogue

In a short piece, breathing room is everything. Dialogue can give the story the appearance of length, giving readers a break from a dense block of action. It’s also a fantastic way to get to know your characters in far fewer words than a lengthy series of plot points. 

When writing dialogue, tailor your writing for every character. Each person, for the most part, speaks differently. Even people who grew up together have slight variations in their deliveries based on personal experiences, perspectives, and their relationships to themselves. Take the time to consider how your characters’ personalities would impact their way of speaking, and keep it consistent.

Dialogue tags

Because space is of the essence, it may be best to keep your dialogue tags to a minimum. Instead of

“I’m going to bed,” John said shrilly. He slammed the door on his way out. 

Or even

“I’m going to bed,” John shrieked. He slammed the door on his way out.

Consider something like

“I’m going to bed.” John slammed the door on his way out.

If it’s clear that John is the person speaking, especially because it’s followed with an action, then a dialogue tag is redundant. Likewise, if the dialogue does enough to convey his emotion, you don’t have to label exactly how he delivered his line.

Get in late and get out early

Dialogue is tricky. Often, writers include the build-up to a conversation. We show characters arriving, making small talk, and then getting into the meat. Likewise, the conversation often tends to go on a beat too long in early drafts. With flash fiction, being concise is key. Get in as late as you can, and get out as early as possible. Taking that approach to your dialogue will keep your readers focused and engaged, and keep your story well within the word count.

Formatting & Grammar

No matter how good your story is, plot point by plot point, if it’s formatted poorly or the grammar is iffy, readers will have a hard time appreciating a solid plot and compelling characters.

Every literary magazine and writing competition is different, and as you grow as a writer and submit across different places for consideration, triple and quadruple-check your work to make sure you’re doing whatever the literary magazine or competition’s unique guidelines call for. We (NYC Midnight) have a few formatting requirements that vary for each of the creative writing challenges, but for the most part, the spacing and margins are up to the writer.

That being said, some formatting choices commonly submitted to our challenges and accepted by literary institutions include one-inch margins on all sides, double spacing, and an automatic 0.5 indent for new paragraphs. The choice of formatting, however, is entirely up to you. Play around, and research what makes for an optimal reading experience - that way, judges can focus less on the act of reading and more on enjoying the world you’ve crafted.

Use free grammar tools

For your project to stand out among the rest, don’t let yourself fall into the trap of poor grammar. Spelling errors and egregious mistakes can take readers (especially judges) out of the story’s flow. Tools like the spell checker on your word processor are free, and they go a long way in professionalizing your piece.  

That being said, review all grammar suggestions with a keen eye. Grammar programs aren’t perfect, and they often misunderstand your sentence’s structure, suggesting mistakes that make your piece more chaotic. Look over each suggestion the grammar software offers and choose accordingly. Going this extra step to polish your story helps the judges focus on your arc and characters rather than errors that put a hitch in your piece’s flow.

As you read, read for pleasure, but also take notes. Jot down answers to these questions:

  1. What compelled you about it? Why?

  2. What didn’t compel you? Why?

  3. How many different scenes could you count?

  4. Settings?

  5. Characters?

  6. Were there any side plots?

  7. How did they structure their sentences? Their dialogue?

  8. What details show you the protagonist’s personality?

  9. In what ways do the characters surprise you? How about the plot?

  10. What’s one thing you notice that you’d like in your own writing style?

Answering these questions may take a little time, but understanding why you like the stories you like - and what works about fiction for you - can help you identify and hone those same traits in your own writing.

As you read more and more flash fiction, you may notice some stories deviating from the advice in this article. It’s true that rules are made to be broken, but knowing the rules first (and doing everything in your power to master them) is the best way to learn to break those rules with success.
