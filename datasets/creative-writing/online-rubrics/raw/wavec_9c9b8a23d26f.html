How to Write Microfiction

The Basics

The information below is intended as a general guideline for writing microfiction. Although every rule is made to be broken and writers have varied approaches to the craft, the advice below is a springboard for anyone getting started writing stories of three hundred words or less.

What Exactly is Microfiction?

While there are near-infinite ways to tell a story, for our challenges, microfiction is defined as a story of three hundred words or less. Think one double-spaced page or less - although dialogue-heavy stories may extend past a single page. Shorter microfiction - fifty to one-hundred-word stories - are much shorter than a page and are closer to a paragraph long.

Microfiction may be short, but like all fiction, microfiction pieces stand on their own with a clear beginning, middle, and end. It’s not an excerpt of a longer piece, but its own complete story. When a reader finishes a satisfying piece of microfiction, they’ll feel like they’ve glimpsed something finished, not needing any more context or backstory than what was given.

A Famous Example

Every successful writer has their own approach to telling a story, and many bend (or ignore) conventions about making good microfiction great. For people just starting out, however, there are several ironclad approaches to writing strong microfiction. 

One of the best ways to learn what works in microfiction is to look at solid examples. Perhaps the most famous micro piece is this six-word story penned by Ernest Hemingway:

For Sale: Baby shoes, never worn.

In a single sentence, Hemingway delivers. Rich in plot with only a handful of words, Hemingway’s story lays out three critical details to craft a narrative arc, with no words going to waste. While leaving room for compelling ambiguity, each clause delivers new - and critical - information. One interpretation of this story could be as follows:

  1. This is an ad, likely placed in the paper.

  2. There was a baby.

  3. The baby never needed to wear those shoes - and the seller needed money badly enough to sell such a sad memento.

There’s enough left out that readers can contemplate why the shoes would be sold and who is selling them, while enough concrete details were given to invest readers in the plot.

Although flowery language, backstory, and side characters are a blast to write, they generally don’t add to the narrative arc, often weighing down the word count limit. That’s what distinguishes microfiction from longer forms, like the short story or even flash fiction. Beautiful turns of phrase are right at home in novels and short stories, but with only a few hundred words, those poetic side quests only get you so far. Hemingway demonstrates that powerful plot makes a good story great - and in no medium is that distinction more important than microfiction.

Write Fewer Characters

Microfiction thrives when it’s kept simple. While it may be tempting to add a rich cast of characters a la Brandon Sanderson or Dune, a story this short has very limited space. Be mindful that naming more than two characters can be difficult for the reader to follow. Beyond that, readers are hard-pressed to keep track of every name dished out in a given paragraph, especially if names are similar. While there can be multiple characters in a piece that’s as short as fifty words, it can help to keep the names to a minimum. Keeping your character list as sparse as possible allows readers to get to know these characters more intimately. It’s a classic case of depth vs. breadth. Fewer characters (less breadth) means ample one-on-one time with your main character (more depth). More depth translates to invested readers who care what happens to the characters you craft.

Keep it streamlined. While writing your piece, only include characters essential to the central arc of the story - no matter how many side plots are cooking in your head.

Show, Don’t Tell

While this is a popular phrase thrown around writing circles, what does “show, don’t tell” actually mean?

Broad concepts like love, hate, loneliness, and war become meaningful to readers when narrow details rope them into the concept, giving it meaning that readers can feel. Get specific about what’s happening in your story. Show the characters concretely interacting with the world around them. Incorporating multiple senses is one way to ground readers in your story while anchoring the characters into a specific point in time and space. Too many broad concepts, and readers won’t be able to picture your character’s movements.

On its own, the word “loneliness” doesn’t capture as much as showing someone acting that loneliness out. Saying “El was lonelier than anyone in the world,” for example, doesn’t stir readers the same way that seeing El’s loneliness might.

Try showing what that loneliness looks like. Perhaps the character is an awkward conversationalist who stands at the edge of a party, too shy to talk to anyone. Maybe they have habits or routines at home that embody how accustomed to solitude the character has grown. Show readers the emotion - not just what it looks like, but what it smells, tastes, sounds, or feels like.

Although there are certainly cases where telling is better than showing, this is a good rule of thumb as you start out. With every emotion, ask how it influences the character’s way of interacting with the scene.

Verbs and Nouns, not Adjectives and Adverbs

While of course most stories need an adjective or adverb from time to time, more often than not there’s a stronger word that captures the essence without wasting word count. When you have such limited space to work with, it makes sense to opt for nouns and verbs that do the heavy lifting adjectives and adverbs are trying to do. 

In general, there are few adverb/verb combinations that you can’t replace with a good verb instead. Simple swaps, like “sprinted” instead of “ran swiftly” not only sound nice; they save you precious space, as well. Look at the following examples and see how the right verb captures the sentiment more:

sat lazily / slouched

ate quickly / wolfed, gobbled

walked confidently / strutted 

Adjectives have a bit more flexibility. Sometimes, they add the flavor a sentence needs. Still, they work best in doses. Too many in a row, and adjectives lose their impact. Readers’ minds begin to wander. When describing something, start with the single strongest noun you can. If it’s a book of spells, perhaps it’s a “tome” rather than a “big, heavy, old book.” Or if your story featured a “famous movie,” it could instead be called a “blockbuster.” A “noisy and spoiled kid” might just be a “brat.” 

Well-placed adjectives can spice up the story, but too many can overload it. If adjectives are too tempting to resist, test out limiting them to one per sentence. Remember, less is more. 

Writing Rich Characters

With so few words at your disposal, it can be tempting to pack a story with back-to-back action, rather than character elements. And sometimes, when we’re trying to show - not tell - we accidentally write a story that is only concise action after concise action. But “show, don’t tell” doesn’t mean showing the character doing a hundred different things back-to-back. 

To craft rich characters, we still need character elements. Just like real people, a short story’s characters should be so distinct from one another (and from other characters in other stories) that they’d never be confused with someone else if a reader bumped into them on the street.

A seemingly tall order for three hundred words or less, right?

The good news is, there are plenty of tricks to ensure you’ve got compelling characters packed with nuance that readers love to follow. 

KNOWING YOUR CHARACTER

Start by knowing your character. Who are they and how do they interact with the world? Write your thoughts down on a note card or a scrap of paper, and explore everything from how they handle conflict to the way they deliver sentences. Figuring these details out will serve as a strong reference guide for building a consistent character. Spending five or ten minutes doing this early in the drafting process will often generate ideas for a character’s personality that hooks readers.

Some stories thrive without dialogue, but if you plan on using it in your story, make sure it’s unique to your character. They don’t need seismic changes, but little subtleties make a character’s lines feel authentic. People all speak differently; even siblings have distinct ways of delivering sentences. Knowing whether your character is a run-on sentence kind of guy or someone who speaks in monosyllables, for instance, can make their voice ring true for readers - and set the stage for conflict if the character encounters someone highly incompatible.

MOTIVE AND MOVEMENT

Third, identify a motive. Characters compel us because they yearn for something - even if they’re just after a late-night chalupa. Knowing what the characters want out of a scene gives the story clear stakes while moving us forward with action. 

And when characters are acting out those wants, integrate that motive with concrete action, at least in key places. Many beginning writers choose thought or action. While that works in measures, marrying the two is one key way to propel the story forward while building character. A character interacting with their landscape and circumstances can capture their personality well while sticking within the confines of a word count. 

Example 1: I had never loved someone as much as I loved Tony. He was the light of my life. (All thought, no action)

Example 2: I was giving Tony roses. I wrapped them in paper and set them on the table. (All action, no thought)

Example 3: I gave Tony roses; his favorite. While wrapping them in paper, I hummed. I’d never loved someone so much before. (Action and thought) 


With memorable, distinct characters whose motives are clear, the plot will speak for itself.

Less is More

With fiction this short, trimming is everything. As you’re writing your first draft, have fun - don’t limit the creative flow. But as you edit, look at every single sentence and ask yourself: does this serve the arc? Meaning, does this contribute directly to the journey the characters are on, or is it not fundamental to the story? If it’s not essential, cut it and see what happens. 

It had been nine years, and Jon’s fingers still froze stiff every winter in this god-forsaken cabin. He lived out in the Colorado Rockies, in a cabin his uncle had built and abandoned. The family never settled on who owned it, and so far, nobody had come. Jon swore, lighting another match for the wood-burning stove. So much work to boil water for rice. He was making rice tonight for a simple dinner, like most nights. Sometimes, Jon imagined his life if he hadn’t shot that kid in the 7-11. Warmer, that was for sure. But without stealing that money, his daughter would’ve died. No, it was better he’d shot one kid so his own could live - even if he never saw her again. 

Something growled outside; the bear again. He grabbed his gun, fingers still stiff with cold, and waited.

If you’re unsure about a detail’s purpose, try removing it and then rereading the piece. If the story’s cohesive without it, then that detail probably wasn’t doing the plot favors. Take a look at the same story, but with some extra details and background trimmed:

It had been nine years, and Jon’s fingers still froze stiff every winter in this god-forsaken cabin. He lived out in the Colorado Rockies, in a cabin his uncle had built and abandoned. The family never settled on who owned it, and so far, nobody had come. Jon swore, lighting another match for the wood-burning stove. So much work to boil water for rice. He was making rice tonight for a simple dinner, like most nights. Sometimes, Jon imagined what his life if he hadn’t shot that kid in the 7-11. Warmer, that was for sure. But without that money he stole, his daughter would’ve died. No, it was better he shot one kid so his own could live - even if he never saw her again. 

Something growled outside; the bear again. He grabbed his gun, fingers still stiff with cold, and waited.

There isn’t enough space in microfiction to get into nitty-gritty histories or side plots, so keeping it simple and containing the details to things that craft a single, strong story or image can let the remaining juicy details define your story.

Trimming Words and Making Space

You’ve pared your adjectives and adverbs down to a minimum and are off to a great start! But with only three hundred (or one hundred, or even fifty) words to work with, there’s got to be more to cut down on to meet the word count. What’s a writer to do?

We’ve touched on extraneous information already. TL;DR: pare down your descriptions to the most powerful ones. Not only will it free up word count, but you’ll also let the descriptions that remain shine on their own, carrying more weight than if they were buried among other descriptive passages. Imagine describing a fast food restaurant the character is robbing at night:

The fryers were empty and cool, the only light in the joint the green, glowing exit sign and the lights from the parking lot, which was as empty as the store inside. Elle crept across the linoleum, the ‘90s kind that had seen better days, and leapt over the Formica counter to fiddle with the sticky-keyed register. 

Although there are plenty of details here, those two sentences are 57 words already, and all Elle has done is jump over the counter. Instead, condensing to only one or two of those details can set the scene while still giving us valuable room to write more. 

The glowing exit sign and the lights from the parking lot were the only things illuminating the joint. Creeping across the worn linoleum, Elle leapt over the counter to fiddle with the sticky-keyed register.

With 23 fewer words, the sentences that remain still capture the run-down fast food restaurant while providing ample room to explore Elle’s arc. 

OPT FOR THE ACTIVE VOICE

Passive voice is common, but it often adds to the word count without adding to the narrative voice. For instance, the sentence “It can be used to shield us from the sun” takes up far more words than “it can shield us from the sun” - or even, “it shields us from the sun.” Trimming four words when there are only a hundred total makes a world of difference.

TRIM PERCEPTION VERBS

Perception verbs are sneaky. At first glance, they work - they’re centering the experience around the main character, and readers get a clear image of what the character is doing. But although that’s true in theory, it’s more often the case that words like “think,” “feel,” “hear,” “see” - words that work with our senses and thoughts - put a filter over the story. Perception verbs hold readers at arms’ length. Take a look at the examples below:

“Lena saw the calendar sitting on the desk. When she opened it, she saw a date circled and noticed a series of strange symbols drawn below it.”

VS

“The calendar sat on the desk. Lena opened it to a circled date, a series of strange symbols drawn below it.”

Trimming perception verbs didn’t take away meaning. Instead, several words have been freed up from the word count, and - most importantly - the line has a greater sense of urgency overall. Rather than the protagonist standing between the readers and the calendar, the readers see the calendar directly, as if they are looking through the protagonist’s eyes themselves.

Formatting

In microfiction, it pays off to get creative with formatting. Spacing your passages gives the illusion of space without adding to the word count. With all the sentences crammed into one paragraph, a story might feel rushed, no matter how methodical or meditative the action is. Try breaking your piece up into more than one paragraph - within reason. Anything beyond double-spaced generally makes for a more difficult reading experience. 

Unless it’s a stylistic choice that serves the story in a very specific way, be sure to space your dialogue, too. Start a fresh paragraph each time a different character speaks. It helps ensure your piece is clear, as well as generous. 

“I’m going to the store,” Jerome said. “Want anything?” I shook my head and didn’t bother looking up from my book. “No, I’m okay.” Frowning, Jerome studied me. I never said no to free food. He sat down. “What’s wrong?”

VS

“I’m going to the store later, Jerome said. “Want anything?”

I shook my head and didn’t bother looking up from my book. “No, I’m okay.”

Frowning, Jerome studied me. I never said no to free food. 

He sat down. “What’s wrong?”


The pause between each line not only smooths out the reading experience; it also lets readers feel the space between their lines and sense the pause as Jerome waits for a response. Although you’re working with just a handful of words, the illusion of length in a piece of microfiction lets readers marinate in the world you’ve crafted that much longer.

But as you read each one, don’t just read them for pleasure (although that’s nice too). Take notes after you finish each piece, and answer these questions for yourself:

  1. What compelled you about it? Why?

  2. What didn’t compel you? Why?

  3. How many different scenes were there?

  4. Settings?

  5. Characters?

  6. How do they structure their sentences? Do they vary?

  7. What’s the character voice like? How can you tell?

  8. In what ways do the characters surprise you? How about the plot?

  9. What’s one thing you notice that you could incorporate into your writing style?

Taking the time to study other works of microfiction is one of the best ways to learn what works for you as a reader. And knowing what makes you tick as a reader is pivotal to telling great stories yourself.

While reading popular or award-winning microfiction, you may notice that several deviate from the guidelines laid out in this article. As with all artistic mediums, knowing the fundamentals - and mastering them as best you can - is how you learn to break the rules with success.
