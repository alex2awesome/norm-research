IB Language A: Language and Literature HL – What You’ll Be Assessed On

Your HL course is made up of four key components, each with its own focus and weight toward your final grade. Here's what you need to know to do your best in the class:

 

Paper 1: Guided Textual Analysis

Time: 2 hours 15 minutes

Weight: 35% of your final grade

Type: External assessment

Texts: Two previously unseen non-literary texts of different types

What you'll do: You’ll be given two non-literary passages (for example, ads, speeches, articles, etc.), each with a guiding question. Your task is to write a detailed analysis of both texts, showing how they communicate meaning through language, structure, and style.

Paper 2: Comparative Essay

Time: 1 hour 45 minutes

Weight: 25% of your final grade

Type: External assessment

Texts: Two literary works you’ve studied (not used in your individual oral or HL essay)

What you'll do: You’ll choose one of four general questions and write a comparative essay using two literary works from your course. This is your chance to show how themes, techniques, and ideas connect across texts.

HL Essay

Length: 1,200–1,500 words

Weight: 20% of your final grade

Type: External assessment

Texts: One literary or non-literary work you’ve studied (not used in your individual oral)

What you'll do: You’ll write a formal essay exploring a deeper idea or concept in one of your studied works. This is a chance to dive into a topic that really interests you and show off your analytical skills.

Individual Oral

Time: 15 minutes total (10-minute presentation + 5-minute Q&A)

Weight: 20% of your final grade

Type: Internal assessment

Texts: One literary work and one non-literary body of work (not used in your HL essay)

What you'll do: You’ll prepare a spoken response to a global issue of your choice, using one literary and one non-literary work to explore how that issue is presented through content and form. After your presentation, your teacher will ask you a few follow-up questions. See the Global Issues box below for more details.

 

Final Tip

Each component is designed to test different skills—from close reading and analysis to comparison, argumentation, and oral communication. Stay organized, plan ahead, and choose works and topics that genuinely interest you. And remember, your teacher is your best resource for this class!

 

Adapted from Language A: language and literature (first assessment 2026), Language A teacher support material

Learner Portfolio

What Is the Learner Portfolio?

Think of the Learner Portfolio as your personal space to explore, reflect, and grow throughout the IB Language and Literature course. It’s not just a folder of assignments—it’s your evolving record of ideas, discoveries, and connections. You’ll build it over two years, and while it’s not directly graded by the IB, it’s essential for preparing your assessments and showing your development as a thinker and communicator.

What’s the Purpose?

The portfolio helps you:

  • Track your learning journey and thought process
  • Reflect on texts and your responses to them
  • Make connections between texts, concepts, and real-world issues
  • Prepare for assessments like the Individual Oral, Paper 1, Paper 2, and HL Essay
  • Develop key skills like critical thinking, research, and self-management

What Goes in It?

Your portfolio should include a mix of formal and informal responses to the texts you study—both literary and non-literary. You can be creative, analytical, personal, or experimental. Here are some examples:

Reflections and Analysis

  • A journal entry exploring how The Handmaid’s Tale connects to gender roles in today’s society

  • A short essay analyzing the use of satire in a political cartoon

  • Notes on how your assumptions shaped your interpretation of a poem

Connections and Exploration

  • A mind map linking themes from different texts (e.g., identity, power, culture)

  • A comparison between a novel studied in class and one you read independently

  • Reflections on how a text relates to your CAS experience or TOK discussions

Creative and Experimental Work

  • A rewritten scene from a novel in a different genre (e.g., turning a tragedy into a comedy)

  • A podcast script discussing the impact of media language on public opinion

  • A visual collage representing the central concept of “representation” across texts

Preparation for Assessments

  • Annotated extracts you might use for your Individual Oral

  • Practice Paper 1 responses with feedback from your teacher

  • Brainstorming notes for your HL Essay topic

Self-Assessment and Feedback

  • Reflections on challenges you faced and how you overcame them

  • Peer feedback from group discussions

  • Your own evaluation of progress over time

Format and Freedom

You get to choose how your portfolio looks and works:

  • Digital or paper-based
  • Traditional or multimedia
  • Structured or freeform

Your teacher will guide you with templates, feedback, and class time to work on it—but the direction and creativity are up to you.

Final Requirement

At the end of the course, your portfolio must include a Works Studied Form listing all the texts you’ve engaged with and how they connect to your assessments.

Tips to Make It Meaningful

  • Start early and update regularly
  • Be honest and reflective—this is your space
  • Use it to experiment, question, and grow
  • Think of it as a conversation between you and the texts, the world, and yourself.

Adapted from IB Language and Literature guide (first assessment 2026), pp. 26-27 and IB Language A Teacher Support Material (first assessment 2021), pp. 8-11.

*Literary or Non-literary?

Sometimes it can be a little complicated deciding whether a text is literary or non-literary—especially with things like essays, memoirs, or other types of non-fiction. Here’s how to figure it out:

If the text... Then it's considered...
Is written by an author on the IB Prescribed Reading List or someone known for their literary work Literary
Has a clear artistic or aesthetic purpose (like using creative language, structure, or style to express ideas) Literary
Doesn’t meet either of those criteria Non-literary

So if a text fits either of the first two points, it’s treated as a literary work in this course—even if it’s non-fiction!

Global Issues

What is a Global Issue?

When preparing for your Individual Oral, one of the most important steps is choosing a global issue to explore. This isn’t just any topic—it needs to meet three key criteria to qualify as a global issue. Your chosen issue must:

  • Have significance on a wide scale – It affects many people or societies, not just a small group.
  • Be transnational – It crosses borders and is relevant in multiple countries or regions.
  • Impact everyday life – Even though it’s global, it should also be felt in local, personal contexts.

Think of it this way: a global issue is something that matters to the world and touches people’s lives in real, everyday ways.

Where Can You Find Global Issues?

There are different sources that you can turn to for inspiration when defining the global issue for your orals. One would be WSA’s subscription database, Gale in Context: Global Issues, which can be accessed from the WSA campus or offsite with a Google email login. Another is the Sustainable Development Goals  set by the United Nations’ Division for Sustainable Development Goals. The WSA Library has a guide explaining the Sustainable Development Goals with texts suggested for each goal. See: Global Issues Essay Guide.

To help you brainstorm, the IB provides five broad fields of inquiry. These are starting points—not final topics. Your job is to narrow down to a specific issue within one (or more) of these areas:

Field of Inquiry Examples of Possible Focus Areas
Culture, Identity and Community Race, gender, migration, nationalism, family, religion
Beliefs, Values and Education  Ethics, conflicting values, education systems, societal norms
Politics, Power and Justice Human rights, inequality, law, government structures, peace/conflict
Art, Creativity and Imagination Role of art in society, censorship, beauty, perception
Science, Technology and Environment Climate change, media influence, technological progress

Remember: don’t just pick a broad category like “gender” or “technology.” Instead, define a specific issue—for example, “the impact of gender norms on professional identity across cultures” or “how digital surveillance affects personal privacy globally.”

Making It Work for Your Oral

Once you’ve chosen your global issue, you’ll need to:

  • Select two texts (one literary and one non-literary) that clearly show this issue.
  • Choose extracts from each text that provide strong evidence of how the issue is represented.
  • Be ready to analyze how the authors use language, structure, and style to explore the issue. 

Your goal is to show how this issue is presented in both texts, and how it connects to the real world—locally and globally.
