In 1,000 Words or Fewer: Writing Great Flash Fiction

"...writing small does not mean thinking small."

Lou Orfanella
Mar 05, 2026

Welcome to our weekly column offering perspectives on lit mag publishing, with contributions from readers, writers and editors around the world.

In a literary landscape becoming increasingly more populated by sound bites and bullets the short short story has come a long way since Hemingway’s “For sale, baby shoes, never worn.” Whether he actually created that prototype or whether it is literary urban legend, audiences are hungry for nutritious stories in a fast-food world.

One might even venture back much farther, from Aesop’s Fables and The Arabian Nights to Biblical parables, for early examples of short short stories. Serialized works, from the novels of Charles Dickens to Armistead Maupin’s Tales of the City series are also possible to include in the dominion of flash fiction, depending upon which arbitrary word count to which one subscribes.

Flash fiction, aka sudden fiction, hint fiction, and short shorts, has proliferated in the ever-increasing electronic age where information is compressed and attention spans are shorter. Just look at any of the scores of publications in the periodical section of a bookstore or at your favorite newsstand. Articles are crammed with bullets and the pages are littered with sidebars. Whether or not it appeals to your artistic integrity, readers want their material delivered in short, easily digested chunks. Fortunately, easily digested does not have to mean without taste or value.

While the occasional vignette or slice-of-life piece will find its way into the arena of flash fiction, by and large the flash piece will generally follow the traditional short story format, just with fewer words. A traditional short story can run from a couple of thousand words to over twenty-thousand words which begins to inch it toward the length of a novella. Flash fiction will likely clock in at five hundred to one thousand words although lengths are open to debate and personal preference. Hint fiction and micro-fiction can be a mere twenty-five to one hundred words and should perhaps be considered a separate genre from flash fiction.

The beginning-middle-end structure remains the framework for a satisfying story, even those presented with an economy of words. Like a poem, a flash fiction relies on every word being essential. There is no room for multiple plot lines, minor characters, superfluous descriptive passages, or dialogue that does not drive the storyline or give readers a deeper insight into your characters.

Step 1: The Idea.

It is important that you not consider brevity a signal to use a “small” idea for your story. Don’t fall into the trap of saving what you consider to be your “big” or “important” stories for the Great American Novel or the Cannes Film Festival screenplay that you know you will write eventually. Most story ideas boil down to the same handful of conflicts and universal truths anyway, so if your idea is a sound one, writing a flash fiction will allow you to delve into it more deeply, see what themes it contains, and refine it for use in other projects later on. William Carlos Williams’ short observational poems are no less inspirational and no less anthologized and revered than Walt Whitman’s epic Song of Myself.

Step 2: The Opening.

Like any good piece of writing your opening must grab your readers by the throat and not let go. In flash fiction particularly, there is no room for unnecessary setting up. Drop your readers right into the scene. This might be a setting, a conversation, or an action, but we must want to go along with the characters for the rest of the ride. In her flash fiction “Objects in the Mirror are Closer Than They Appear” in the anthology New Sudden Fiction, Joyce Carol Oates begins,

First time you drive past my house Tuesday after school my big-boob spike-haired cousin Gwendolyn Barnstead is sitting with me smoking on the front steps, says Man! You are the handsomest man she has ever seen in actual life.

Are these girls looking for trouble? Will trouble find them? Something is brewing and we want to find out what it is.

Step 3: Development.

Selection of tense and point of view can be essential in flash fiction since some elements of development are more implicit than explicit given the economy of words necessary in the genre. Past tense can allow some events to unfold much more quickly than if they are happening in present tense narration. A third-person point of view could allow for more character development with fewer words than would be necessary with a first-person voice that is unable to access the minds of other characters. A benefit of flash fiction is that you can expediently rewrite your piece in a different tense or a different narrative voice and look at them side by side. This might be a daunting task when writing a novel but should be part of your regular flash fiction writing process.

Your middle has the same job in flash fiction as it does in a three-hundred-page novel. This is where you set up and develop the conflict facing your protagonist. In his section of the Gotham Writers’ Workshop’s Writing Fiction, David Harris Ebenbach says,

The middle section is, most importantly, where the protagonist’s path toward his or her goal is blocked again and again by increasingly daunting obstacles. And where the forces arrayed against him or her become ever more powerful.

In flash this can be implied without the exposition that could require several pages. In my story “Dead on Arrival” a modern mail order bride finds upon her arrival that her intended is dead. She uses his open computer files to support a lavish lifestyle which is implied, but we don’t have the luxury of enjoying it with her in great detail lest our word count begins to move us out of the realm of flash fiction. Still, it is hopefully enough for readers to share her shock and disillusionment when the computer crashes and his password-protected accounts are lost to her. One plot and one conflict should be your rule of thumb.

Step 4: The Payoff.

A successful flash fiction will have a kick-butt ending for your readers. The ironic twists of O. Henry and the often-heartbreaking climax resolutions of episodes of Rod Serling’s Twilight Zone are fine templates for the types of endings that make flash fiction work. Anyone who has ever seen the 1959 “Time Enough at Last” episode of Serling’s classic anthology series is forever haunted by the image of Henry Bemis, the henpecked bookworm who as the sole survivor of a nuclear blast finally has the time to do all of the reading his nagging wife deprived him of, as he cries, “It’s not fair!” upon breaking his eyeglasses after making stacks of books to last the rest of his life.

A good way to start writing flash fiction is to look for an ironic twist as your starting point and then work towards it. My story “Eternally Yours” is about a couple who decide to get each other a gift that will be a symbol of their love and connect them eternally. The woman had converted to Judaism for their upcoming wedding and her gift to him is a tattoo on her side with his name, a heart, and the word “forever.” He realizes immediately that due to the ancient laws of Judaism regarding tattoos, she cannot be buried in a Jewish cemetery thus thwarting their plans for an eternity together. My starting point was the Jewish tattoo law and the story stemmed from that.

A successful flash fiction will have a kick-butt ending for your readers.

Step 5: Marketing.

Traditional-length short stories still dominate printed literary magazines and journals, but online publications offer a seemingly endless stream of opportunities for placing flash fictions. A web search for “flash fiction publishers” or “online flash fiction magazines” will conjure over four million hits. Sifting out redundancies and irrelevant items still leaves a multitude of opportunities for novice and seasoned flash fiction-writers.

When I was preparing my first flash fiction collection for publication, I submitted a few of the stories to online journals which I had read for compatibility and by the time March Street Press put out my book, In a Flash: Twenty-One Short Short Stories, I had placed stories in The Scruffy Dog Review, Coffee Cramp, Bent Pin Quarterly, and Wild Violet. Marketing your work pre- and post-publication at open mics, libraries, and bookstores can help cultivate new readers and an awareness of flash fiction in a way that will surprise and entertain audiences. After four collections and numerous personal appearances, selections of my flash fictions were used as performance monologues by the theater group The Armchair Actors, giving me an entirely new venue for my flash fiction.

Remember, writing small does not mean thinking small. Fiction can come in all sizes and even the smallest portions should be satisfying.

Thanks Lou for this article - always glad to see a spotlight on flash! A note/ further information for new writers - I’ve written flash fiction and have been a flash editor and reader for journals. There are great examples by the great Kathy Fish (among others!) and in the Best Small fiction anthologies.
