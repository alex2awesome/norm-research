Writing Reviews: Scribophile

July 13, 2020May 19, 2020 blargablarga10 Comments

I’m going to be straight with you guys.  I don’t like being openly and unabashedly negative, or bought-and-sold positive, which I think you can probably figure out from my ongoing review of Breath of the Wild.  So I want to put a sort of disclaimer up here, that I thought long and hard about doing this review of Scribophile, but in my research, I went looking for other reviews, and it’s difficult to find ones that aren’t sponsored.  This needs to exist.  Because Scribophile is just lying to folks, over and over.  And it’s not acceptable.  So it’ll get pretty obvious pretty fast that they ain’t paying me.  But what I worry, is that readers won’t think my experience is generalizable or common, so I probably should have left more stuff up on my Scrib account so I could link to it.  But, if you really wanted, go out on the internet, and look at the one-star reviews.

The reason I put this disclaimer at the top, is because Scribophile is like…  unethical and evil to the point that I’m still coming to terms with it.  Like I called the fucking cops.  That’s not an exaggeration.  And it can be difficult, from the outside looking in, to hear about experiences like this, and not think the person is exaggerating.  I’m not.  I have no idea how this company is still allowed to operate; I think that they’re actively breaking the law.  But I’m not a lawyer, and they are still here, so I guess this behavior is legal, somehow.

So let’s get into it.  I’ll try to go fairly chronologically and move forward, documenting my experience.

So I decided to try Scribophile because I ‘won’ a discount by writing more than the required number of words on the Nanowritemo site.  It wound up being like $45 for a year-long membership, I think?  So I went to the site and read over their description, and to explain why I thought this was a good fit for me at the point I’m currently at, I’m going to take a little bit and talk about the writing process very, very briefly.  I might write an entire series of posts about that, because it is complex, and difficult, and time consuming.  Many people never get a book to print because it’s not really something to just play around with, you have to be serious about it and dedicate a lot of time to it.  There are several steps involved.

First, you have to do the research for your work, then you have to draft it, then you have to self edit it in at least three stages.  All that you can do by yourself, to get something that should be at least readable; something you wouldn’t be embarrassed to have people read.  This is the first or ‘alpha’ stage.  But that’s about as far as you can take it on your own.  After that you have to farm it out to somebody.  So for the beta stage, you have to find people in your target audience and ask for real, honest feedback.  You need their real subjective opinion, about everything.  These are the people that you would be selling this book to.  If they don’t like it, if they wouldn’t buy it, you need to know that, and you need to know why.

The beta stage can go wrong for new authors, in a couple of ways.  The first and most obvious is hubris.  The author can think that they’ve created art, they’ve created the best thing ever written, and the people THEY THEMSELVES asked for help are just haters.  This is a dick mindset, but it’s so, so easy to fall into.  You would not believe how easy it is to be completely unable to internalize this feedback, that you need to internalize, in order to grow.  This happened to me.  I think it happens to a lot of people starting out.  But you have to eventually get over it, stop being a whole bag of dicks, and realized that you asked for help because you need help, and I like to call this the, “Bitch, you ain’t -insert rich and famous author name-” realization.  It’s what we in the Bible Belt call the ‘Come to Jesus’ moment, and it’s important.

Other people can go the other way, and listen to everything their beta readers say, which is literally impossible to work with.  Because for a good beta stage, you need at least two rounds of ten readers each in your target audience with an edit in between based on the first round, and as we all know, if you ask 20 people something subjective like, “What do you like or dislike about this book; would you buy it and why or why not?”, you’ll get 20 different answers.  So this group who internalizes all the feedback has contradictory feedback, and gets stuck in an eternal loop of trying to please everybody; an impossible task, and like…  I’ve seen folks break down in tears.

This is the stage that hurts the most, if you’re emotionally attached to your work, and let’s be real- a lot of folks are emotionally attached to your work.  Because the goal of this round is to get something that OTHER PEOPLE think is worth reading.  It’s to build a target audience.  This is NOT an edit or proofread.  They’re not giving you any objective advice, everything will be subjective.  It’s based on whether or not other people like your work and would be willing to read it.  If you’re just writing for yourself; there’s no reason to get published, because you already read it.  You have to have a pretty good idea that other people will give a shit about your manuscript.

Then you do another edit based on that feedback.  Did people find your MC really unlikable and want them to fail?  Fix that shit, people hate characters they hate and won’t read about them.  Did people hate your setting because they didn’t know where the fuck they were at?  Fix it; readers need worldbuilding or they’re not gonna be grounded and they’ll get bored and you’ll get 1 star on Goodreads.  Was your romance subplot not compelling at all or worse, abuse masquerading as romance?  Bet your ass you better fix it.

Now, again, it’s important to get a consensus, not to try and please everybody, because the beta stage is for subjective questions.  If you have 19 people who were on board with the romance and totally ship it, but then you get one reader and the love interest reminds her of her ex girlfriend and for that sole reason she keeps giving feedback that she wants the character to die in a fire- maybe throw that one suggestion out?  Make a call.

Only when you have a revised manuscript with a real core target audience that you know exists, who enjoy the work and are willing to read it, are you ready to move on to the next stage, which is, you guessed it, more feedback and more editing based on that feedback.  Now at this stage, if you’re rich as fuck, you can hire a developmental or story editor.  I hear that they’re great.  But they wanted $300.  If you don’t have $300, you do the next best thing- a series of critique partners!  This is where you both agree to do developmental/story edits for each other, as folks who aren’t professionals, but you’re swapping work instead of paying for it, so you’re basically crowdsourcing your edit.

So the critique is objective issues with the writing itself.  Unlike beta readers, who are giving you subjective opinions, this is shit that is objectively right or wrong, will or will not fly.  Are there plot holes?  Did you fuck up your timeline (Hint: If there’s time travel, you probably fucked the timeline right up, but also like people will mess up calendar dates and whatnot)?  Does your book have a magic system, and if so, does it have internal consistency or did you pull it right outta your ass?  Did you do your research, or did you get basic shit like how heat works wrong?  For the research thing, if you have stuff in your book that you’re not an expert on, and you get a critique partner who is, you have been blessed and you need to be thankful as hell.  Appreciate that.  Do you know how rare that is?

This is going to ensure that your book makes sense, and there’s not going to be youtube videos picking it apart, and also that you don’t go out here looking a fool and shit who doesn’t know their own timeline, or their own magic system, or their own historical setting.  This is, once again, feedback that you need, the big difference is that it’s not, “Would you read this?” but “Did I do this right?”  The goal here for the critique partner is not to help you write a book people like, but to help you write the book you thought you wrote the first time.

You do this in this order, Stage 2, then Stage 3, for obvious reasons- if you’re more concerned with things like historical accuracy, fine tuning magic systems, and fixing obscure plot holes that beta readers didn’t notice BEFORE you see if people even connect with the plot or characters, then you just did a whole bunch of work for shit you might have to cut or rewrite completely.  Like imagine you set your romance book during slavery and it was about an enslaved person falling in love with their master, and you did all this research on the 1700s, and then you got that beta feedback, and EVERY BIT OF IT was, “I love romance, but this setting is offensive and creepy and horrible.  If it’s set here and this relationship is set up like this, not only will I not read it, I will call out your racism and you’ll never sell a copy.”  You gotta change your whole setting, or you’re a dick.  So if you did Stage 3 first, you wasted all your damn time.  That’s an extreme example, but let’s not pretend shit like that don’t happen.

The critique stage is what Scribophile caters to.  So on it’s face, this looks like a great system.  As long as the critique partner knows how to write it doesn’t matter if they’re part of your target audience or not, it doesn’t matter if they’re reading your genre or not.  They should, theoretically, be easier to find and work with, because you don’t have to be so specific.  It’s easier to answer objective questions and spot objective problems, than subjective ones, because of that ability to widen the circle.  And you’ll know, going in, that the people you’re getting feedback from have themselves already been through the first two stages.  They have a manuscript that they’ve drafted, self edited, researched for, and gotten beta feedback on, edited again, and polished, ready for critique.

So the way Scribophile works, is again, a good system as described to you, the customer.  You have to go read other Scrib users work, leave feedback (the feedback guidelines I’ve laid out here are also laid out on Scrib itself, there are terms of service and instructions), and for each critique you leave, you earn points.  You then spend those points to post your own work, which Scribophile guarantees will receive at least three critiques before it moves out of the queue.  Generally, you’re expected to post one chapter or short story at a time, so this will be a time consuming process if you’re posting a long fantasy series, as I was.  Scribophile is, on the terms of service when you sign up, very clear and very willing to sell you on the idea that yes, this is a lot of work on your part, but you’ll be getting lengthy, quality critiques from the other users.  On paper, this is a perfect system.

There are also groups that you can join within the site, for users in certain genres, with certain target audiences, where you can network with people even more closely aligned with your goals.  I joined many of these, and will discuss my experience as we get a little further along in this review.

So, now we know what Scribophile is supposed to be, what it is billed as,what it sells itself as.  And, going by that, it would seem as if I would have an extremely positive experience.  How could it possibly go wrong?

As I already said, you have to post critiques before you can post your work; I believe I wrote three critiques, all opening chapters of books I intended to continue critiquing through until the end- and I was perplexed by what happened next.  I did nothing except follow the guidelines, yet I started receiving messages through the private messaging system available on the site.  These messages said things like, “This is the best critique I’ve ever gotten, thank you so much, here’s how your feedback has helped me!”  From three different and completely unrelated users.  At the time, I did not register this as a red flag, because I didn’t read these as, “The other critiques were bad,” I read it as, “These three users are really nice.  I will continue to read their work.”  I thought they were using, “This is the best critique I’ve ever gotten,” as a turn-of-phrase, not as a statement of fact.

Then I joined several groups, as I talked about before, one of which was dedicated to developing good characterization for fantasy novels.  It was a place where users could workshop their characters, their backstories, development, etc.  Part of this group was a role playing aspect, similar to a story-go-round or Dungeons and Dragons, wherein different users could play out the characters they had developed in different scenarios, which would theoretically help them fully develop all aspects of their characters for their books.  I read a few of these threads, and fun fact about me: I love me some Dungeons and Dragons, so I went to the ‘I would like to play’ thread and listed a character.

I was contacted by another user, and set up a thread to play this game.  Then I got my second red flag.  This user wrote child pornography, exclusively, and wanted me to role play the statutory rape of a teenage girl.  Now…  I’m a human person, and this was a public forum, so I contacted the group leader and let them know what was going on, how horrified I was, and they informed me that I was not the first person to report this user to them for pedophilia.  They were more than willing to delete the thread for the obvious ethical and legal reasons, and warned me to have no further contact with that user.  I didn’t understand why the user was still in the group, but the group leaders are volunteer users, not employees of Scribophile.  They can’t really do anything about individual users, but I assumed that she, the group leader, was contacting people who could.  I feel, because of what happened later, that this was not an assumption I should have made.

BUT, I thought that this was one bad apple, and had met two other people in this group who were very nice, reasonable, normal people.  The sort of people you would want to network with and become critique partners with.  So I stuck it out, hung around, and I want to give credit where it’s due.  This group leader deleted that thread instantly when I asked her to, was as horrified as I was, and was very kind.  She seemed to be a good person.  I’m not saying that Scribophile is a cesspool of exclusively evil people.  It’s not.  But there seems to be no way to weed out the evil like there is on say, Critique Circle or Twitter, or even AO3.  I would soon learn how deep this went.

I kept giving critiques and was eventually able to post my own work, so I posted several chapters of a fantasy series I’m working on.  This took about three weeks, I want to say.

I realized instantly that there were people who were using Scribophile in a way that was again the rules; I got critiques that were not critiques at all.  I got a few beta reads (from people who were not in my target audience and didn’t even know the target audience, indicating that they had not read the provided author’s notes, and therefore would have been useless to me even as beta reads), and one thing that read as if it had been copy/pasted from another site and had nothing to do with and made no reference to the work I had posted.  So I took to the forums to see if anyone else was having this issue.  It turned out that this was a common issue discussed on the forums; there were a plethora of users who had not read the rules and had no desire to abide by them.  They were not there to help other authors, they were there to leave anything to get the algorithm to register it as a critique and had learned what words to use to do so.  There were even websites that had been set up with copy/pastable text to achieve this goal.  The desire of these users was that they would earn the points without having to do the work of helping others, and would then be able to post their own work and get real help.  Because people are assholes.

I get that there are going to be assholes everywhere, and this does seem like something you would just have to deal with, but it’s bullshit.  Fortunately, there was the ability to report these critiques and get them to where they didn’t count toward the guaranteed three, but apparently the system for doing this was done by people, Scribophile employees, and thus was slow and overly prone to human error.  So that was annoying, but still not a dealbreaker.

The deal broke for me when I received a critique so disturbing and illegal, I scoured the internet searching for the owners of Scribophile, to find some contact information, because I was going to call them.  I thought it was something they would want to know about so that they could pursue legal action.  A user who said he was the leader of a group left a ‘critique’ that was actually a subjective beta read about how he disliked the work, because it didn’t have enough descriptive depictions of minority and disabled children being graphically burned alive for the gall to have been born non-white and non-able-bodied.  He got extremely descriptive.  I, of course, reported the critique.  I won’t go into what it actually said, but it was not appropriate for any platform.  It was obviously something that needed to be dealt with.  This user took great pleasure in these sorts of atrocities and called them funny.  He seemed to think it would add humor and enjoyment to the story, and openly valued and promoted a variety of hate crimes.  Of course, this is not only immoral, like the child porn, but illegal here in the United States.

I managed to find the contact information for Scribophile, and contacted the person who claimed to run the site, to let them know what was going on.  I had already reported the ‘critique’, yet it was allowed to remain on my page, my platform, for the world to see.  I got a response saying that unlike any other online platform, Scribophile was not moderated or policed for content in any way.  That things like the extreme hate speech or child pornography that I had experienced were not only tolerated, but were-let’s be real-because of this lack of oversight, encouraged.  It was one of the few places on the internet that could happen, so it was going to happen.  In the individual groups, the group leaders could use their discretion to delete threads, but the work you posted was considered public, and you had no power over what people put under it.  They believe that they are not legally responsible for this, because the platform is a hosting site for user generated content, and the content is made by the user, so the user, not the platform, is legally responsible for it.

I’m not a lawyer.  I don’t know if that’s true or not.  It feels like it shouldn’t be, because I know that other platforms that provide hosting space for user generated content don’t operate as if it’s true.  Though they may not be perfect, platforms such as Youtube or here on WordPress where this site is hosted will remove illegal content.

So I called the police.  I made a report, then I went back to Scribophile and deleted everything I had posted, hoping that by deleting my story I would delete comments attached to it, and contacted Scribophile to let them know that I wanted my money back, or at least a refund on the half a year I wasn’t going to use, because obviously this platform was not for me.  They refused.  I wound up having to put a stop through the bank, which cost me a $35 fee, so I wound up getting very little back.

There was a way for me to know, going in, that I would have had this negative experience, but not an easy way.  When you search for reviews, the ones that show up are mostly positive, and the ones that have one-star do not adequately express the situation.  What most of the one-star reviews say is that the user tried to speak out against Scribophile in some way, and had their account deleted, and were denied a refund.  I had no reason to think that this was over valid complaints about immoral, unethical, and illegal content being forced upon them, but rather personal problems with management.  That just wasn’t clear in any of the reviews.

The reason that I felt compelled to write this lengthy review was because recently I’ve been working on another manuscript, and I’m at the beta stage.  I was trying to find beta readers, and came upon a website for a beta reader who looked promising.  But when I contacted her, it turned out to be a bot- selling Scribophile memberships.  I tweeted about this the day it happened, and in that tweet I promised to write a blog post about Scribophile, because I think that they need to be talked about, more than one can gather from the one-star reviews.

If you’re the kind of person who has infinite patience and an infinite capacity to suffer fools, evil, and bullshit, without a desire for any kind of oversight in communities you engage with- Scribophile might be helpful for you.  If you’re willing to go through all that and network with the good people you can find, this might be a good resource.  But you can do that on Twitter or Critique Circle for free.  So I don’t know why you would choose the shittiest platform possible to do it on, but I do think it can be done.

I actually shouldn’t say “no oversight”, because some of the reviews I read did say that they made posts on this topic on the public forum and they were hastily deleted along with their entire account, and again, they were given no refund.

Also, and this is neither here nor there, but I suppose it does bear mentioning, even though it’s nothing in the grand scheme of things; the site itself is laid out really poorly from a web design perspective, and I don’t understand what they’re charging for if they’re not paying moderators or web developers.  Like I can’t figure out where that money’s going.  It seems like the sort of thing that could easily be ad supported.

Like just as an example, there’s a ‘good critiquer’ section.  When your posts are listed on the main page, they’re listed chronologically with the newest appearing first, but if you critique well, your work is moved even farther down, under the fold, where it would get less exposure.  Why?  Why are you punished for abiding by the site rules and guidelines?  I brought this up on the forum, because there’s a section about web design, and no one who worked there seemed to know what the hell I was talking about, seemed to know anything about layout and exposure or retention rates, or even know what those terms meant.  A user literally, while arguing that I was wrong, asked me what ‘the fold’ and ‘viewing area’ and other basic design terms meant.  If you have people paying $60 or whatever for memberships, I really feel like you could hire a designer who knew basic shit.  Again, that’s not anywhere near the reason I quit, I quit because of the evil, but the incompetence wasn’t…  great.  It didn’t help.

When I brought this up, that my engagement numbers were being cut by more than half because I was being moved to such an undesirable location from a web design perspective, again, being punished for following the rules, I had other users tell me that I didn’t want engagement from the main page, because of that whole fishing for points thing; that there was considerably more fishing for points than actual critiques that came in through the main page, so I should NEVER try to get engagement from the main page, and should instead go to the groups and network.  And this is likely sound advice for navigating Scribophile as a site, but it shouldn’t be.  Like it’s not cool to have a website that runs like that.  I don’t think that’s a controversial thing to say; that your website should, at bare minimum, do what it says it’s supposed to do.  And Scribophile says that I should, as a user, be able to generate at least three real critiques by just being an active user within their point system.

Again, I’m not sure that would have made me quit, because there were people in the groups that I did enjoy networking with, but it’s not good.  Like we can’t sit here and pretend that that’s acceptable.

So the conclusion is that not only did I have a negative experience with this platform, you couldn’t pay me enough to recommend it to new writers.  And that makes me wonder how much they are paying for sponsored content and these sale bots and positive reviews and all that.  Because the negative reviews show me that my experience is generalizable.  You have to be able to look past the mountains of shit to be able to get a good experience, and I wonder what their retention rates are.  I wonder how many people who pay for a year stay for a year.  I would imagine that they’re rather low, that most people would leave after about half that, like I did, but that’s anecdotal.  I don’t have numbers on that.

If you want a similar experience without having to call the goddamn cops, I might do a review for Critique Circle.  Critique Circle is a similar site, but it’s guidelines are slightly different, and in my experience it seems as if it actually gives at least one fifth of a fuck about the ethics of running such a service, which is my main problem with Scribophile.  And if I remember right, it’s free.  I might be wrong about that, but I think it might be free.

Also, just the #writingcommunity on twitter is a great place to network, similar to how you would with Scrib groups, but free and with oversight so you can report hate speech and child porn and whatnot and unlike Scrib you can get it off your feed instantly.  You can block a user and not have to see their bullshit every time you log on.

So yeah.  I did not have a good experience with Scribophile, and I could never recommend it in good conscious.  Your mileage may vary, but you’re driving a rusted pickup that’s rattling like a motherfucker and you know it’s gonna explode at some point.

Addendum: I realized upon review that I didn’t state the reason Scribophile gave for disallowing authors any ability to curate their own pages when they post their work.  Because on most platforms, you can delete comments.  This should not be a major thing; it’s not just for hate speech and whatnot, some comments just aren’t helpful, and you paid money: you’re not here to dick around, you’re here to get the feedback that’s useful to you, so you should be able to curate it.

Apparently, Scribophile is so poorly designed that deleting a comment from the critique section also deletes the points the user who left the critique generated.

For some reason.

So because of this massive design flaw, there’s no way to curate the feedback in a meaningful way; no way to delete unhelpful feedback that you’re not going to use (aside from reporting it, which in many cases leaves it on your feed when you log in to look at your critiques) and no way to organize the critiques that I could find, because apparently the staff is afraid that such a system would lead to abuse, because they consider it abusive to delete comments you don’t find useful, because it’s cheating that user out of points.  Again, people are paying for this service.  Why can’t they hire a programmer to write some better backdoor code so this simple thing like determining which critiques you can see on your own feed wouldn’t be a major issue so you wouldn’t have to log in and read Nazi language you don’t want to see?
