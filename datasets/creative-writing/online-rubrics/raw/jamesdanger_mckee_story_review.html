STORY: SUBSTANCE, STRUCTURE, STYLE AND THE PRINCIPLES OF SCREENWRITING by Robert McKee

Posted on April 23, 2017 by Daniel James Hunt

 

PART I: THE WRITER AND THE ART OF STORY

Story is about:

  • Principles not rules
  • Eternal, universal forms, not formulas
  • Archetypes not stereotypes
  • Thoroughness not shortcuts
  • The realities, not the mysteries of writing
  • Mastering the art, not second guessing the marketplace
  • Respect not, disdain for the audience
  • Originality not duplication

Ch. 1 “The Story Problem”

  • The 1st Act should build to a sudden climax then spinoff into a superb weave of plot and subplot then drive into the 2nd Act climax that is so moving that the movie seems like it’s over. The goal must be a great story well told.
  • The mark of a master is to select only a few moments but still give us a lifetime.
  • Story values are the universal qualities of the human experience that may shift from a positive to a negative or vice versa from one moment to the next achieved through conflict.
  • Look at each scene you write and ask; what value is at stake in my character’s life right now?

PART II: THE ELEMENTS OF STORY

Ch. 2-3 “The Structure of Spectrum” & “Structure & Setting”

  • Beats build scenes, scenes build sequences.
  • A movie must have a beginning, middle and end just not necessarily in that order – Godard
  • Classical Design, Archplot – a story built around an active protagonist who struggles against primarily external forces of antagonism to pursue his or her desire through continuous time within a consistent and causally connected fictional reality, to a closed ending of absolute irreversible change
  • Archplot: Causality, closed ending, linear time, external conflict, single protagonist, consistent reality, active protagonist, examples: The Seven Samurai, The Seventh Seal, The Hustler, 2001: A Space Odyssey, Big
  • Minimalism, Miniplot: Open ending, internal conflict, multi-protagonists, passive protagonist. Less various than archplot and mostly international Examples: Tender Mercies, A River Runs Through It, Wild Strawberries, The Sacrifice, La Passion De Jeanne D’Arc
  • Anti-Structure, Antiplot: Coincidence, nonlinear time, inconsistent realities. Predominately European post WWII. Examples: 8½, Persona, Monty Python and the Holy Grail, Stanger Than Paradise, Wayne’s World
  • Causality – interconnectedness of reality
  • Coincidence – disconnectedness of existence
  • Each tale you create for the audience is you saying, “I believe life is like this.”
  • Write only what you believe.
  • Difference for the sake of difference is empty.
  • Setting is 4 dimensional: Period, duration, location, and level of conflict
  • A story must obey its own internal laws of probability.
  • Comedy rule: nobody really gets hurt.

PART III: THE PRINCIPLES OF STORY DESIGN

Ch. 7: “The Substance of Story”

The Protagonist

  • Is a willful character
  • Has a conscious desire
  • May also have a self-contradictory, unconscious desire
  • Has the capacities to pursue the object of desire convincingly.
  • Must have at least a chance to attain their desire.
  • A protagonist who is literally hopeless, who hasn’t even the minimal capacity to achieve their desire cannot interest the audience.
  • A story must build to a final action beyond which the audience cannot imagine another.
  • Must be empathetic, may or may not be sympathetic.

Audience

  • Audiences disassociate themselves from hypocrites.
  • Humanity is fundamentally conservative as is all of nature; nothing expends more energy than needed or takes any action unless it must.
  • Human nature is just an aspect of universal nature.
  • The difference between story and life is in story we cut out the minutiae of daily existence where humans take actions expecting a certain reaction and more or less get what they expected.

Character

  • The closest circle of antagonism for a character is always himself.
  • The three levels of conflict; extrapersonal, personal and inner.
  • We all walk the Earth thinking, or at least hoping, we understand ourselves, our intimates, society and the world.
  • What does the character stand to lose if they don’t achieve their desire? If this question cannot be answered in a compelling way, the story is misconceived at its core.
  • What’s true of you will be true of every character you write.
  • The measure of the value of a character’s desire is in direct proportion to the risk they are willing to take to achieve it; the greater the value, the greater the risk.
  • Write from the inside out; find your way to the center of a character and experience it from their P.O.V.
  • Create emotional truth.
  • The only source of emotional truth is the writer.
  • Many of the greatest screenwriters were also actors.
  • If a scene is emotionally meaningful to us, we can trust that it’ll be meaningful to the audience.
  • Give the characters an objective for each scene.
  • “If I were the character in these scenes, what would I do?”
  • To progress the scene the writer moves out of the character’s P.O.V. and looks objectively at the action they have just created. This action anticipates a certain reaction from the character’s world, but that reaction must not occur. Instead the writer asks, “What is the opposite of that anticipated reaction?”
  • “The spirit of creation is the spirit of contradiction – the breakthrough of appearances toward an unknown reality.” – Jean Cocteau
  • Fine writing emphasizes reactions.

The Gap

  • Story is born in that place where the subjective and objective realms touch.
  • Rather than evoking cooperation from their world, the character’s action provokes forces of antagonism that open up the gap between his subjective expectation and the objective result, between what they thought would happen when they took their action and what in fact does happen between their sense of probability and true necessity.
  • Action/reaction/gap – renewed action/surprising reaction/gap builds a great scene.
  • A pointless pace killer is any scene in which reactions lack insight and imagination, forcing expectation to equal result.
  • Words are not the writer’s tool they are a medium to their creativity.
  • The substance of a story is the gap that splits open between what a human being expects to happen when he takes an action and what really does happen; the rift between expectation and result, probability and necessity.
  • To build a scene we constantly break open these breaches in reality.

Ch. 8 “The Inciting Incident”

  • Cast design: nothing in a work of art is there by accident.
  • The 1st principle of cast design is polarization. Between the various roles devise a network of contrasting or contradictory attitudes.
  • If 2 characters share the same attitude and react in kind to whatever occurs, you must either collapse the two into one, or expel one from the story.
  • Story is a ritual surrounding a metaphor for life.
  • “For the purposes of [story] a convincing impossibility is preferable to an unconvincing possibility” – Aristotle
  • Authenticity has nothing to do with actuality. A story set in a world that could never exist could be absolutely authentic.
  • Originality lies in the struggle for authenticity, not eccentricity. A personal style, in other words, cannot be achieved self-consciously.
  • The inciting incident radically upsets the balance of forces in the protagonist’s life.
  • The protagonist must react to the inciting incident even if it is a refusal.
  • The energy of a protagonist’s desire forms the critical element of design known as the spine of the story.
  • If the protagonist has an unconscious desire than this becomes the spine of the story. An unconscious desire is always more powerful and durable, with roots reaching to the protagonist’s innermost self, this allows the writer to create a far more complex character who may repeatedly change his conscious desire.
  • Every story can be called a quest.
  • The inciting incident creates an image of the climax in the mind of the audience. The writer has to create that image.
  • The audience intuitively knows when something is missing. A lifetime of story-telling has taught the audience to anticipate that the forces of antagonism provoked at the inciting incident will build to the limit of human experience, and that the telling cannot end until the protagonist is in some sense face to face with these forces at their most powerful.
  • The instant the audience has a sufficient understanding of character and world to react fully, execute your inciting incident.
  • What is the worst possible thing that can happen to my protagonist? How can that turn out to be the best possible thing that happened to him/her?
  • What is the best possible thing that can happen to my protagonist? How can that turn out to be the worst possible thing that happened to him/her?
  • The impact of the inciting incident creates our opportunity to reach the limits of life. It’s a kind of explosion. No matter how subtle or direct, it must upset the status quo of the protagonist and jolt his life from its existing pattern, so that chaos invades the character’s universe. Out of this upheaval, you must find, a climax, a resolution, for better or worse, that rearranges this universe into new order.

Ch. 9 “Act Design”

Progressive Complications

  • A story must not retreat to actions of lesser quality or magnitude, but move progressively forward to a final action beyond which the audience cannot imagine another.
  • Nothing moves forward in a story unless it is through conflict.
  • Conflict is to storytelling what sound is to music
  • Story needs to hold our uninterrupted concentration, then carry us through time without the awareness of the passage of time.
  • The quality of conflict changes from level to level; the quantity of conflict is constant.
  • If the depth and breadth of conflict in the inner life and the greater world don’t move you then let this: death.
  • If we’re to live a life of satisfaction we need to engage life’s forces of antagonism before death arrives.
  • Life is about the ultimate questions of finding love and self-worth, of bringing serenity to inner chaos, of the titanic social inequities everywhere around us, of time running out.
  • Have conflict on all three levels: inner, personal, and extra personal.
  • Do not proliferate characters, do not multiply locations.
  • Acts create a major reversal in characters’ lives, greater than any sequence.
  • If an audience is willing to give you hours of their lives then they’re entitled to a story that has the power to reach the boundaries of experience.
  • Three major reversals are the necessary minimum for a full length work of narrative art to reach the end of the line.
  • The first act should be about 25% of the telling and the last act should be the shortest.
  • A three act story requires 4 memorable scenes; Inciting incident, Act I, II, & III climaxes.
  • If every scene screams to be heard, we go deaf.
  • The only reason to delay the entrance into the central plot is if the audience needs more time to fully understand the protagonist. If this is necessary than a subplot is also necessary. Ex. Rocky, Inciting Incident is at 33 minutes, the Adrian-Rocky love story starts at 5 minutes.
  • Repetitiousness is the enemy of act rhythm.
  • You cannot set up an up ending with an up ending or vice versa. The ultimate and penultimate climaxes must be in contrast to one another.
  • A subplot may be used to contradict the Controlling Idea of the central plot and thus enrich the film with irony.
  • A subplot may be used to resonate the Controlling Idea of the central plot and enrich the film with variations on a theme.
  • The principal of thematic contradiction and variation is the genesis of multiplot films. An idea holds the film together. Ex. Parenthood plays on the idea that in the game of parenthood you cannot win. All the sets of parents in the story lose, just in varied fashions.
  • Diner resonates with the idea that men cannot communicate with women. Fenwick cannot bring himself to speak to women, Boogie talks nonstop to women (only to bed them), Eddie won’t marry his fiancée until she can pass a football trivia test. Billy faces his emotional issues with the woman he loves, he lets his guard down and talks honestly with her. Once able to communicate with a woman, he leaves his friends – a resolution that contradicts all others to add a layer of irony.
  • A subplot may be used to complicate the central plot.
  • To deemphasize a subplot, some of its crucial moments – Inciting Incident, act climaxes, Crisis, Resolution – may be kept off screen.
  • If a subplot runs parallel to the central plot then it will split the telling down the middle and destroy the story.
  • Screenwriting is making the mental
  • Create visual correlatives for inner conflict.
  • Faced with irreconcilable choices, such as pace versus empathy, the wise writer redesigns the story to preserve what’s vital. You’re free to break or bend convention under one circumstance: to put something more important in its place.

Ch. 10 “Scene Design”

Turning Points

  • A scene is a story in miniature.
  • In theory there’s no limit to the size or location of a scene, it may be infinitesimal.
  • No matter locations or length, a scene is unified around desire, action, conflict and change.
  • A scene causes change in a minor but significant way.
  • Each scene needs to be a minor, moderate, or major turning point.
  • The effects of turning points are four-fold: surprise, increased curiosity, insight, and new direction.
  • The effect of a beautifully turned moment is that the filmgoer experiences a rush of knowledge as if they themselves did it. It essence, they did. Insight is their reward for paying attention.
  • Self-expression occurs in the flood of insight that pours out of a turning point.
  • Insights must be shaped into Setups and Payoffs.
  • Setups must be handled with great care. They must be planted in such a way that when the audience first sees them, they have one meaning, but with a rush of insight they take on a second more important meaning. It’s possible, in fact, that a single setup may have meanings hidden to a third or fourth level.
  • If Setups are too subtle the audience will miss the point; if they’re too obvious they’ll see the Payoff coming a mile away.
  • The understanding of how we create a response from the audience comes with the realization that there are only 2 emotions – pleasure or pain.
  • Story must create dynamic alternations from positive to negative emotions in order to obey the law of diminishing returns – the more often we experience something the less impact it has on us.
  • The choice between good and evil or right and wrong is no choice at all. Human nature dictates that we will always choose the “good” or the “right” as we perceive the “good” or “right.”
  • Good/evil right/wrong choices are dramatically obvious and trivial.
  • True choice is dilemma – two irreconcilable goods or the lesser of two evils.
  • Meaningful decisions are triangular.
  • By adding the third corner we are given over 20 variations.
  • An original work poses choices between unique but irreconcilable desires: it may be between two persons, a person and a lifestyle, two lifestyles, two ideals, two aspects of the innermost self – between any conflicting desires at any level of conflict, real or imagined, the writer may devise. But the principle is universal: choice must not be doubt but dilemma, not between right/wrong or good/evil, between either positive desires or negative desires of equal weight and value.

Ch. 11 “Scene Analysis”

Text and Subtext

  • If the scene is what the scene is about, then you’re in deep shit.
  • Actors are not marionettes to mime gestures and mouth words. There artists who create from the subtext.
  • It’s virtually impossible for anyone to fully express what’s really going on inside of them. We never fully express the truth, for in fact we rarely know it.
  • The most passionate moments must conceal an even deeper level. Ex. Chinatown: Evelyn Mulwray cries out “She’s my sister and my daughter. My father and I…” but what she doesn’t say is, “Please help me.”
  • Star Wars: When Darth Vader offers Luke Skywalker the chance to join him in running the universe, bringing “order to things,” Luke’s reaction is to attempt suicide. Not a logical response, but one that makes perfect sense, for both Luke and the audience read Darth Vader’s subtext: Behind “bring order to things” is the unspoken implication “…and enslave billions.” Wen Luke attempts to kill himself, we read a heroic subtext: “I’ll die before I’d join your evil enterprise.”
  • Characters can do anything imaginable, but with their inability to express the full truth at any given moment the writer must layer through subtext.
  • Subtext is present even when the character is alone. We wear masks to hide ourselves from ourselves.

Scene Analysis in 5 Steps

  1. Define conflict – who drives the scene? Motivates it? Makes it happen? Could be any force or even an inanimate object or act of nature. Then ask what does the driver of the scene
  2. Note Opening Value – identify what value is at stake in the scene and whether it’s positive or negative at the beginning.
  3. Break the scene into beats – look carefully at the scene’s opening action on two levels: what is the character doing outwardly? What he seems to be doing, and, more important, look beneath the surface and see what he is actually doing; Subtextural Action. The phrases that express the action in the subtext do not describe character activity in literal terms; they go deeper to describe the character’s essential action with emotive connotations.
  4. Note Closing Value and compare it with Opening Value – Analyze the scene and notice if the value is charged positive or negative. If it is the same as the Opening Value than the scene is a nonevent. If nothing changes then nothing happened.
  5. Survey the beats and locate the Turning Point – well designed scenes will have an arc and a purpose. Within the arc locate the moment when the major gap opens between expectation and result, thus turning the scene to its end changed value. This is the Turning Point. Ill-written scenes may lack conflict because desires are not opposed, may be because their Turning Points come too early or too late, or lacking credibility because dialogue and action are “on the nose.” But an analysis of a problematic scene that tests beats against scene objectives, altering behavior to fit desire or desire to fit behavior, will lead to a rewrite that brings the scene to life.

Ch. 12 “Composition”

  • Composition means the linking and ordering of scenes.

Unity and Variety

  • A story, even when expressing chaos, must be unified.
  • Because of the Inciting Incident the Climax had to happen.
  • Unity is critical but not sufficient. Within this unity, we must induce as much variety as possible. Casablanca, for example, is not only one of the most loved films of all time, it’s also one of the most various. It’s a brilliant Love Story, but more than half the film is Political Drama. Its excellent action sequences are counter pointed by urbane comedy. And it’s the next thing to a Musical with over a dozen tunes strategically placed throughout.

Pacing

  • If you slowly turn the screw adding a bit more tension throughout then the audience will be worn out before the Climax.
  • Alternation between tension and relaxation is the rhythm of life.

Rhythm and Tempo

  • Rhythm is set by the length of scenes. How long are we in the same place?
  • A typical 2 hour feature has 40-60 scenes meaning, on average, a scene is 2½ minutes long.
  • Scene length is a reaction to the nature of cinema and the audience’s insatiable hunger for expressive moments.
  • Tempo is the level of activity within a scene through dialogue, action or a combination. Ex. Lovers pillow talking to each other; low tempo. Courtroom argument; high tempo.
  • In a well told story the progression of scenes and sequences accelerates pace.
  • Climactic doesn’t mean short and explosive; it means profound change.
  • Cliché or not, we must control rhythm and tempo. If the screenwriter doesn’t control it the director will, if the director doesn’t the editor will.
  • The screenwriter must master the aesthetics of motion pictures and create a screenplay that prepares the way for the artists who follow.

Expressing Progression

There are four primary techniques to express progression:

  1. Social Progression – widen the impact of character actions into society. Ex. Lonestar, Men in Black.
  2. Personal Progression – drive actions deeply into the intimate relationships and inner lives of the characters. Ex. Ordinary People, Chinatown.
  3. Symbolic Ascension – build the symbolic charge of the story’s imagery from the particular to the universal, the specific to the archetypal. Ex. The Deer Hunter, The Terminator.
  4. Ironic Ascension – turn progression on irony. Ex. Othello, Ruthless People.

Principle of Transition

  • A story without a sense of progression tends to stumble from scene to scene.
  • As we design cycles of rising action we must also create smooth transitions between them. Therefore we need a third element that links the tail of scene A with the head of scene B.
  • To find this link look at either what the scenes have in common or what they have in opposition.
  • Examples:
  1. A Characterization – Cut from a bratty child to a childish adult (common) Cut from an awkward protagonist to an elegant antagonist (opposition)
  2. An Action – From the foreplay of lovemaking to savoring the afterglow (common) From chatter to cold silence (opposition)
  3. An Object – Greenhouse interior to woodland exterior (common) Congo to the Antarctica (opposition)
  4. A Word – a phrase repeated from scene to scene (common) compliment to curse (opposition)
  5. A Quality of Light – Shadows at dawn to shade at sunset (common) red to blue (opposite)
  6. A Sound – waves lapping a shore to rise and fall of a sleeper’s breath (common) silk caressing skin to the grinding of gears (opposition)
  7. An Idea – a child’s birth to an overture (common) a painter’s empty canvas to an old man dying (opposition)

Ch. 13 “Crisis, Climax, Resolution”

Crisis

  • Crisis is the ultimate decision.
  • The Chinese ideogram for crisis is in two terms: danger/opportunity
  • The crisis must be true dilemma – a choice between two irreconcilable goods, the lesser of two evils, or the two at once that places the protagonist under the maximum pressure of their lives.
  • The protagonist may get what they want but it won’t be in the way they expected.

Climax

  • Meaning produces emotion.
  • Meaning – a revolution in values from positive to negative or negative to positive with or without irony; a value swing at maximum charge that’s absolute and irreversible. The meaning of that change moves the heart of the audience.
  • The action that creates this change must be pure, clear, and self-evident, requiring no explanations. Dialogue or narration to spell it out is boring and redundant.
  • This action must be appropriate to the needs of the story.
  • The writer hints at the ending starting at the beginning of the film.
  • An ending must be both inevitable and unexpected.
  • A great ending should leave the audience with a single image that sums up and concentrates all meaning and emotion.

Resolution

  • A resolution may be needed to complete a subplot, however if done wrong this can be awkward.
  • A Resolution may be needed to show the spread of climactic effects. Some of the supporting roles may be impacted by the climax, so the audience needs a resolution for them as well ex. The satirical title roll in Animal House.
  • All films need a resolution.

PART IV: THE WRITER AT WORK

Ch. 14 “The Principle of Antagonism”

  • The principle of antagonism is the most important and least understood precept in story design.
  • A protagonist and his story can only be as intellectually fascinating and emotionally compelling as the forces of antagonism make them.
  • When we see the protagonist and understand their sources of conflict we should see clearly that they are an underdog. They should have a chance to achieve their object of desire, but only a chance.
  • Our subject is life, not arithmetic.
  • In life two negatives don’t make a positive. In English double negatives are grammatically incorrect, but the Italians uses double, sometimes triple negatives so that a statement feels like its meaning. In anguish an Italian might say, “Non ho niente mia!” (I don’t have nothing never!)
  • Negation of the Negation – a compound negative in which a life situation turns not just quantitatively but qualitatively worse.
  • The Negation of the Negation applies not only to the tragic but the comic as well. The comic world is a chaotic, wild place where actions must go to the limit. If not the laughs fall flat.
  • All other factors of talent, craft, and knowledge being equal, greatness is found in the writer’s treatment of the negative side.
  • When a story is weak the inevitable cause is that the antagonism is weak.
  • Build the negative side to create a chain reaction that pays off honestly and naturally on the positive dimensions.
  • Generally, progressions run from the positive to the contrary in act one, to the contradictory in later acts, and finally to the Negation of the Negation in the last act, either ending tragically or going back to the positive with a profound difference. Big, on the other hand, leaps to the Negation of the Negation, then illuminates all degrees of immaturity. Casablanca is even more radical. It opens at the Negation of the Negation with Rick living in fascist tyranny, suffering self-hatred and self-deception, then works to a positive climax for all three values.
  • Anything is possible, but the end of the line must be reached.

Ch. 15 “Exposition”

  • Exposition means facts.
  • Show don’t tell; dramatize exposition
  • Dramatized exposition serves two purposes: further the immediate conflict and convey necessary information to the audience.
  • Two characters don’t tell each other things that they both already know.
  • If the information is needed then the writer must create a motivation for the characters to say it in a way that is greater than the facts.
  • Convert exposition to ammunition – let them use what they know as ammo in their struggle to get what they want.
  • Show don’t tell doesn’t mean have the camera pan across a bunch of expository photos of the characters.
  • Parse out exposition bit by bit through the story.
  • Don’t write “California Scenes”
  • California scenes – scenes in which two characters who hardly know each other sit down and immediately begin an intimate discussion of the deep, dark secrets of their lives (a conversation that would only happen in California).
  • When it comes to exposition save the best for last.
  • Anytime you write about one character telling another character something they both should already know, ask yourself is it exposition or ammunition?
  • You can turn a scene only one of two ways: an action or a revelation
  • Dramatize flashbacks. Rather than flashing back to flat scenes in the past, interpolate a mini drama into the story with its Own Inciting Incident, Progressions, and Turning Points.
  • The test of voice-over narration is this: Ask yourself, “If I was to strip the voice-over out of my screenplay, would the story still be well told? If the answer is yes… keep it in.
  • Oliver Stone’s JFK is a skillful design of exposition.

Ch. 16 “Problems and Solutions”

The Problem of Interest

  • Curiosity is the intellectual need to answer questions and close open patterns.
  • Concern is the emotional need for the positive values of life.
  • Human nature is instinctively repelled by what it perceives as negative, while drawn powerfully toward positive.
  • The reason we search for the center of good is that we believe that we are good or righteous and want to identify with the positive. Deep down, however, we know that we are flawed.
  • The center of good should be found in the protagonist. The center of good doesn’t imply “niceness.”
  • Curiosity and concern create three possible was to connect the audience to the story: Mystery, suspense, and dramatic irony.
  • Mystery – the audience knows less than the characters.
  • Arouse the audience’s curiosity about past events, tease with hints of the truth, then keep it in the dark with “red herrings,” so that they suspect or believe false facts while the real facts remain hidden.
  • Red herring etymology: as peasant poachers of deer and grouse made off with their booty through medieval forests, they would drag a fish (a red herring) across the trail to confuse the lord of the manor’s bloodhounds.
  • This technique is only used in murder mysteries.
  • Murder mysteries have two subgenres: Open and Closed.
  • The open mystery is the Colombo form in which the audience sees the murder committed and knows who did it. The story becomes a “How will he catch him?”
  • The closed mystery is the Agatha Christie form in which the murder was committed off screen. The story becomes a “Who done it?”
  • These two designs may be mixed or satirized. Chinatown starts closed but turns open at the Act II climax. Usual Suspects parodies the closed. It starts as a “Who done it?” but becomes a “Nobody done it.”
  • Suspense – the audience and characters have the same information.
  • In suspense the curiosity is not about the facts but rather the outcome
  • Dramatic Irony – the audience knows more than the characters.
  • Creates interests primarily through concern alone.
  • The audience is given God-like knowledge, the story usually begins with the ending.
  • We feel for the character because we know they are headed toward disaster.
  • Placing the audience in Dramatic Irony doesn’t remove curiosity it simply switches the emotional experience. The audience now wants to know how and why the character(s) do what the audience already knows they have done.
  • Reward the filmgoer for their concentration with honest, insightful answers to their questions. No dirty tricks, no Cheap Surprise, no False Mystery.

The Problem of Surprise

  • If what the audience expects to happen happens, or worse, if it happens the way the audience expects it to happen, they will be unhappy. We must surprise them.
  • Two kinds of surprises: True and Cheap.
  • True surprise comes from a sudden revelation of the Gap between expectation and result. This surprise is “true” because it’s followed by a rush of insight, the revelation of a truth hidden beneath the surface of the fictional world.
  • Cheap surprise takes advantage of the audience’s vulnerability.
  • In certain genres – Horror, Fantasy, Thriller – cheap surprise is a convention and part of the fun.

The Problem of Coincidence

  • Story creates meaning, therefore coincidence should be regarded as an enemy of the writer; for coincidence is random and meaningless. Yet coincidence is a part of life.
  • The trick is not to avoid coincidence, but to dramatize how it may enter life meaninglessly, but in time gain meaning, how the anti-logic of randomness becomes the logic of life-as-lived.
  • Bring coincidence in early to allow time to build meaning out of it.
  • Example of bad coincidence: Eric seeks his estranged lover, Laura, but he finds out she’s moved. After searching in vain he goes to a bar. On the stool next to him sits the realtor who sold Laura her new house. He gives Eric the new address. Eric thanks him and leaves. (Not that this coincidence couldn’t happen, but it’s pointless)
  • On the other hand what if the realtor can’t remember the address but remembers her car. Eric and the realtor go out and search for Laura, they find the car at her house. Both men go up to her door. Still angry with Eric, Laura invites them in and flirts with the realtor in an attempt to annoy her ex-lover. What was meaningless good luck bow becomes a force of antagonism to Eric’s desire. This triangle could build meaningfully through the rest of the story.
  • Rule of thumb: do not use coincidence past the mid-point of the telling. Rather, put the story more and more into the hands of the characters.
  • Never use coincidence to turn an ending. This is deus ex machine, the writer’s greatest sin.
  • Deus ex machine is a Latin phrase taken from the classical theaters of Greece and Rome, meaning “god from machine.” From 500 B.C. to 500 A.D. theater flourished throughout the Mediterranean. Over those centuries hundreds of playwrights wrote for these stages but only seven have been remembered, the rest mercifully forgotten, due primarily to their propensity to use deus ex machine to get out of story problems. Aristotle complained about this practice: “Why can’t these writers come up with endings that work? Mother fucker.”
  • In these amphitheaters at the far end of a horseshoe-shaped stage was a high wall. At the bottom were doors or arches for entrances or exits. But actors who portrayed gods would be lowered down to the stage from the top of the wall standing on a platform attached to a rope and pulley. This “god from machine” device was the visual analogy of the deities coming down from Mount Olympus and going back up to Mount Olympus.
  • If a playwright ran out of creativity for a true Climax then he would crank down a god ex. Apollo or Athena, who would come down and settle everything.
  • Nowadays writers don’t use a god coming down to get their ending but they use an act of god if they can’t think of anything better. Ex. The hurricane that saves the lovers in The Hurricane or the T-Rex that kills the velociraptors in Jurassic Park.
  • Deus ex machine not only erases all meaning, it insults the audience.
  • Each of us knows we must choose and act, for better or worse, to determine the meaning of our lives. No one and nothing coincidental will come along to take that responsibility from us, regardless of the injustice and chaos that may surround us.
  • Deus ex machine is insulting because it is a lie.
  • The one exception is antistructure films that substitute coincidence for causality.
  • Stranger in Paradise and After Hours begin by coincidence, progress by coincidence, and end on coincidence. When coincidence rules story it creates a new and rather significant meaning: Life is absurd.

The Problem of Comedy

  • The dramatist admires humanity and creates works that say, in essence: under the worst of circumstances the human spirit is magnificent.
  • Comedy points out that under the best of circumstances human beings will find a way to screw things up.
  • Comedy is at heart an angry, antisocial art. To solve the problem of weak comedy, therefore the writer first asks: what am I angry about? Then finds that aspect of society that angers his blood and goes on an assault.
  • Comedy allows more coincidence than drama and may even allow a deus ex machina ending, if two things are done: first, the audience is made to feel that the comic protagonist has suffered enormously. Second, that he never despairs, never loses hope. Under these conditions the audience may think: “Oh hell, just give it to him.”

The Problem of Adaptation

  • We have three media for telling story: prose (novel, novella, short story) theater (legit, musical, opera, mime, ballet), and screen (film, television, internet).
  • Each medium tells complex stories by bringing characters into simultaneous conflict on all three levels of life; however, each has a distinctive power and innate beauty at one of those levels.
  • Prose dramatizes inner conflict the best.
  • Theater commands the dramatization of personal conflict. A great play is almost 80% pure dialogue.
  • Screen or cinema dramatizes extra-personal conflict the best.
  • The purer the novel, the purer the play, the worse the film.
  • Literary purity means a telling located exclusively at the level of inner conflict, employing linguistic complexities to incite, advance, and climax story with relative independence of personal, social, and environmental forces: James Joyce’s
  • Purity of theater means a telling located exclusively at the level of personal conflict, employing the spoken word in poetic excess to incite, advance, and climax story with relative independence of inner, social, and environmental forces: T.S. Eliot’s The Cocktail Party
  • To adapt first read the source material without taking notes. You must be able to feel the spirit of the story.
  • After you understand the story write a sentence or two about each event that occurs. Do not add the sociology or psychology.
  • Read through the story and decide if it is well-told.
  • Be willing to reinvent.
  • Reorder the story chronologically regardless of how the novel or play tells it and create an outline.
  • Feel free to cut scenes and add new ones as necessary.
  • Express mental into physical. Don’t fill character’s mouths with self-explanatory dialogue but find visuals to express inner conflict.
  • Seek a design that expresses the spirit of the original yet stays within the rhythms of a film and ignore the risk that critics may say “But it’s not like the novel.”
  • Chuck Palahiuahnk preferred the film Fight Club to his own book.
  • To learn adaptation study the work of Ruth Prawler Jhabvala.
  • Read Quartet, A Room With a View and The Bostonians. Create an outline from each novel, then scene by scene compare your work to Jhabvala.
  • She and director James Ivory restricted themselves to the social novelists – Jean Rhys, E.M. Forster, Henry James – knowing that the primary conflicts will be extra-personal and camera attractive. No Proust, no Joyce, no Kakfa.
  • Write in a purely dramatic and visual way, to show a natural world of natural human being behavior, to express the complexity of life without telling.

The Problem of Melodrama

  • Nothing human beings do in and of itself is melodramatic; human beings are capable of anything.
  • Melodrama is not the result of overexpression, but of under motivation; not writing too big, but writing with too little desire.
  • A scene is melodramatic if the motivation doesn’t match the action.

The Problem of Holes

  • Cowardly writers try to kick sand over a hole and hope the audience won’t notice. Other writers face the problem manfully. They expose the hole to the audience, then deny it’s a hole.

 

 

Ch. 17 “Character”

The Mind Worm

  • Mind Worm – suppose a character had the power to burrow into the brain and come to know an individual completely – dreams, fears, strength, weakness. Suppose that this Mind Worm could then create a specific happening geared to the unique nature of that person that would trigger a one-of-a-kind adventure, a quest that would force them to use themselves to the limit. Whether a tragedy or fulfillment, this quest would reveal their humanity absolutely.
  • The writer is a Mind Worm.
  • We burrow deep into a character to discover their aspects and potential then create an event geared to their unique nature – the Inciting Incident.
  • We design the Inciting Incident to fit the character, the precise invitation needed to send them on a quest that reaches the limit of their existence.

Characters Are Not Human Beings

  • A character is a work of art, a metaphor for human nature.
  • We relate to characters as if they’re real but they are superior to reality.
  • Character design begins with two primary aspects: Characterization and True Character.
  • Characterization is the sum of all the observable qualities, a combo that makes the character unique: physical appearance coupled with mannerisms, style of speech and gesture, sexuality, age, IQ, occupation, personality, attitudes, values, where they live, how they live.
  • True Character waits behind the mask: Who are they despite what we see? Loyal or disloyal? Honest or a liar? Loving or cruel? Courageous or cowardly? Generous or selfish? Willful or weak?
  • The key to true character is desire.
  • What does the character want? Now? Soon? Overall? Conscious or subconsciously?
  • Behind desire is motivation. Why do they want what they want?
  • The more the writer nails motivation to specific causes, the more he diminishes the character in the audience’s mind. Rather, think through a solid understanding of motive, but at the same time leave a shroud of mystery around the whys, a touch of the irrational perhaps, room for the audience to use its own life experience to enhance your character in its imagination.
  • A character is the choices he or she makes.
  • What other characters say about a character is a hint.
  • What a character says about themselves may or may not be true.
  • Characters with self-explanatory dialogue are phony. We know who a character is when we see their actions. In Casablanca Rick says, “I stick my neck out for nobody.” The audience thinks, “Well not yet Rick, not yet.” The audience knows Rick better than he knows himself.

Character Dimension

  • Dimension is the least understood concept in character.
  • Decorating a protagonist with quirks doesn’t open his character and draw empathy.
  • Fine characters, arguably, are marked by one dominant trait, i.e. Macbeth’s ambition is frequently cited. His ambition supposedly is what makes him great. This theory is incorrect. If he were merely ambitious there’d be no play. His greatness as a character stems from the contradiction of his ambitiousness vs. his guilt. This inner contradiction springs his passion and complexity.
  • Dimension means contradiction: either within deep character (guilt-ridden ambition) or between characterization and deep character (a charming thief.)
  • Contradictions must be consistent.
  • The protagonist must be the most dimensional character or else the audience loses balance and the story suffers. Ex. Blade Runner, Rick Deckard (Harrison Ford) is supposed to be the protagonist but audiences were drawn to the greater contrast and complexities of Roy Batty (Rutger Hauer) and diminished what should’ve been a huge successful film into a cult classic.

Cast Design

  • In essence the protagonist creates the rest of the cast.
  • Imagine a protagonist who at times is optimistic then morose, compassionate then cruel, fearless then fearful. This character will need an ensemble of characters present in order to be able to dramatize these contradictions, and make them credible and consistent.
  • Don’t cause false anticipation by making bit parts more interesting than necessary.

The Comic Character

  • The comic character is marked by a blind obsession.
  • The first step to solving the problem of a character who should be funny but isn’t is to find the character’s mania.
  • Sociopaths are the most charming people we meet; they’re sympathetic listeners who seem so deeply concerned about our problems all the while leading us into hell.
  • No one thinks they’re bad. Villains and antagonists are just people who are struggling to make it through life with the cards they’ve been dealt.

3 Tips for Writing Characters on the Screen

  1. Leave room for the actor – an actor brings a character to life from the subtext.
  2. Fall in love with all your characters – we often see films with a cast of excellent characters… except for one, who is awful. We soon realize it is because the writer hates this character and trivializes and insults them at every opportunity. Embrace all your creations. Only writers who love their characters find true moments.
  3. Character is self-knowledge – Everything I learned about human nature I learned from me – Chekov. Despite the obvious distinctions of age, sex, race, and culture, the truth is we are all far more alike than we are different. We are all human. That’s why you ask yourself “If I was this character, what would I do?” the honest answer is always correct. You would do the human thing.

Ch. 18 “The Text”

Dialogue

  • Dialogue is not conversation. Eavesdrop on any conversation in a coffee shop and you’ll soon realize you would never put that slush on screen.
  • When two friends stop and talk about the weather, they’re not really talking about the weather; they’re stopping their lives for a moment and confirming that they are indeed friends or “keeping the channel open.”
  • The text is not the subtext.
  • Screen dialogue must say the maximum using the minimum possible words.
  • Dialogue must have direction and purpose. Each exchange in dialogue must turn the beats of the scene. Each exchange executes a step in design that builds and arcs the scene around its Turning Point.
  • All this precision and dialogue still needs to sound like normal talk, using an informal natural vocabulary, with contractions, slang, profanity and even misuse of words or phrases as necessary.
  • Speak as common people do, but think as wise men do. – Aristotle.
  • The aesthetics of film are 80% visual 20% auditory.
  • Dialogue doesn’t require complete sentences.
  • Never write anything that calls attention to itself being dialogue.

The Suspense Sentence

  • Trim speeches to lift the scenes energy.
  • Excellent film dialogue tends to shape itself into the periodic sentence: “If you didn’t want me to do it, why did you give me the…” Look? Gun? Kiss?

The Silent Screenplay

  • The best advice for writing screen dialogue is don’t do it. If you can create a visual expression instead; do that.
  • Asks of every scene; how can I express this with as little dialogue as possible?
  • Write for the eye.
  • The audience will be hungry for dialogue.
  • How would you write a waiter seducing a customer scene? Here’s what Bergman gave us: The waiter walks to the table and accidentally on purpose drops a napkin to the floor. As he slowly bends to pick it up, he slowly sniffs and smells Anna from head to crotch to foot. She, in reaction, draws a long slow, almost delirious breath. CUT TO: They’re in a hotel room. Purely visual without a single line of dialogue.
  • When the screenplay has been written and the dialogue has been added, we’re ready to shoot. – Alfred Hitchcock.

Description

  • A screenplay waits for the camera.
  • 90% of all verbal expression has no filmic equivalent.
  • “He’s been sitting there for a long time.” is un-filmable; perhaps he stomps out his tenth cigarette or checks his watch anxiously.
  • Whether we jump to or flash back to, it’s still a We write in the present tense.
  • Write vividly bad ex. “The carpenter hammers a big nail.” Good ex. “The carpenter hammers a spike.” Avoid generic names attached to adjectives “nail,” and “big.” Seek out the specific word you are looking for.
  • Same goes for verbs. Bad ex. “He starts to slowly move across the floor.” Good ex. “He pads across the room.” “He [ambles, strolls, moseys, saunters, drags himself, staggers, waddles, minces, trudges, teeters, waltzes, glides, lumbers, tiptoes, creeps, slouches, shuffles, waddles, lurches, gropes, hobbles] across the room.
  • Eliminate “is” and “are” throughout.
  • Onscreen nothing is in a state of being; story life is an unending flux of change.
  • “There is,” “They are,” “It is,” “He/She is” are the weakest possible ways into any English sentence.
  • “There is a big house on a hill above a small town.” vs. “A mansion guards the headlands above the village.”
  • Paint a picture inside the readers head; incept them and then get them to do things for you.
  • Eliminate “we see,” or “we hear,” “we” doesn’t exist.
  • “We see” injects an image of the crew watching through the lens and shatters the reader’s vision of the film.
  • Delete all transitions.

Image systems

  • We react to every image symbolically.
  • The first step to turning in a well-told story into a poetic work is to exclude 90% of reality.
  • If a director wants to add a vase to a shot, an hour long conversation will be had about it. Because it isn’t just a vase, it’s a highly charged, symbolic object resonating throughout the film.
  • Image System – a strategy of motifs, a category of imagery embedded in the film that repeats in sight and sound from beginning to end with persistence and great variation, but with equally great subtlety, as a subliminal communication to increase the depth and complexity of aesthetic emotion.
  • Category means a subject drawn from the physical world that is broad enough to contain sufficient variety. For example, a dimension of nature – animals, the seasons, light and dark – or a dimension of human culture-buildings, machines, art.
  • A films poetics must be handled with virtual invisibility and go consciously unrecognized.
  • Casablanca uses three Image Systems. Its primary motifs create a sense of imprisonment as the city of Casablanca becomes a virtual penitentiary. The beacon from the airport moves through the city like a prison spotlight, the window blinds, room dividers, stair railings and even potted plants all cast shadows that look like bars of prison cells.
  • The second system Casablanca uses is a mini United Nations. Casablanca homes Americans (black and white), Germans, French, Asians, Arabs, Africans etc.
  • Rick represents America and Casablanca represents the rest of the world. Rick, like America in 1941, wants to remain neutral, but is congratulated once he enters the fight against tyranny.
  • The third system is linking and separating. Rick and Ilsa are constantly linked while separated at the same time. Ilsa and Lazlo are seen separated through a series of images as well. Which creates a sense that even though these two are together they belong apart.
  • Chinatown uses a “blind seeing” or false seeing as an Image System – windows, rearview mirrors, eyeglasses, broken spectacles, cameras, binoculars, eyes themselves.
  • Hitchcock’s thrillers combine images of religion and sexuality; John Ford’s westerns counterpoint wilderness with civilization.
  • An Image System must be subliminal.

 

Title

  • To title means to name.
  • Moment to Moment is the placeholder title McKee uses until he figures out the real title.
  • The best titles often name two or all elements at once.
  • Jaws names a character, set the story in the wilds, and gives us the main theme, man against nature, in the Action/Adventure genre.

Ch. 19 “A Writer’s Method”

  • Successful writers start by creating a story’s step outline.
  • Using one or two sentences the writer simply and clearly describes what happens in each scene, how it builds and how it turns. Ex. “He enters expecting to find her at home, but instead discovers her note saying she’s left for good.”
  • The successful writer wants to destroy his own work.
  • Trash everything that is less than your best.
  • The writer doesn’t show his step outline to people because it’s his
  • If a story can’t work in 10 minutes it can’t work in 110 minutes. It won’t get better just because it got bigger.

Treatment

  • The treatment is the next step after the step-outline.
  • Expand each scene from 1-2 sentences to a paragraph or more. Double spaced, present tense, and moment by moment description.
  • In treatment the writer indicates what characters talk about; “he wants her to do this, but she refuses,” for example – but never writes dialogue. Instead he creates the subtext, the true thoughts and feelings underneath what is said and done. We may think we know what our characters are thinking and feeling, but we don’t know shit about shit until we write it down.
  • The treatment of a typical screenplay should produce 60, 80, 90 or more double spaced pages,
  • In the 1930’s to the 1950’s treatments were often 200-300 pages long.
  • The 10-15 page “treatments” of today are not really treatments they’re more like outlines.
  • In the treatment is where you find out that things might not work the way you planned them to in the step outline.
  • You must rework the treatment until every moment lives vividly, in text and subtext, then you have my permission to die… I mean write the screenplay.

Screenplay

  • Now you transfer treatment description to screen description and add dialogue. Dialogue written at this stage is often the best dialogue.
  • When you allow your characters to speak, scenes you thought would work in the treatment now want to work in a different way.
  • A fault can rarely be figured out with a change of dialogue. You have to go back into the treatment and rework the setups and perhaps go beyond the faulty scene and redo the payoff.
  • The wise writer puts off writing dialogue as long as possible. The premature writing of dialogue chokes creativity.
  • If you type dialogue before you understand what happens then you fall in love with your own words.

 

 

FADE OUT

High above the forest floor, a millipede strolled along the branch of a tree, her thousand pairs of legs swinging in an easy gait. From the tree top, song birds looked own, fascinated by the synchronization of the millipede’s stride. “That’s an amazing talent,” chirped the songbirds. “You have more limbs than we can count. How do you do it?” And for the first time in her life the millipede thought about this. “Yes,” she wondered, “how do I do what I do?” As she turned to look back, her bristling legs suddenly ran into one another and tangled like vines of ivy. The songbirds laughed as the millipede, in a panic of confusion, twisted herself into a knot and fell to the earth below.

         On the forest floor, the millipede, realizing that only her pride was hurt, slowly, carefully, limb by limb, unraveled herself. With patience and hard work, she studied and flexed and tested her appendages, until she was able to stand and walk. What was once instinct became knowledge. She realized she didn’t have to move at her old, slow, rote pace, she could amble, strut, prance, even run and jump. Then, as never before, she listened to the symphony of the songbirds and let music touch her heart. Now in perfect command of thousands of talented legs, she gathered courage and, with a style of her own, danced and danced a dazzling dance that astonished all the creatures of her world, while she killed those mother fuckin’ songbirds.

Now go forth and create, you beautiful son-of-bitch!
