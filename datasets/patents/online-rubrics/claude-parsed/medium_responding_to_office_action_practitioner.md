# Responding to Your First Office Action: A Practitioner's Guide

## Read the Rejection Three Times Before Doing Anything

The first time, read it for emotional reaction. The second time, read it ignoring the examiner's tone and just identifying which claims are rejected under which sections (101, 102, 103, 112). The third time, read it with the cited references open in another window. Most "bad" office actions are actually defensible once you read what the references actually disclose.

## Map Every Element to Every Reference

For a 103 rejection, build a literal table: rows are claim elements, columns are references, cells are the passages cited. You will discover (a) elements where the cited passage does not in fact teach what the examiner says it teaches, and (b) elements where there is no citation at all. Both are arguments.

## Argument vs. Amendment - The Trade-Off

Arguing without amending preserves claim scope but creates more file-history estoppel if the examiner is not persuaded and you amend later. Amending without arguing concedes the rejection. The strongest responses do both: a narrow amendment plus a clear argument that explains why the amended language is not taught in the references.

## Avoid "Disparaging the Reference"

Do not write "the examiner clearly misunderstands the reference" or "this rejection has no basis." Write "the cited passage at column 4, lines 13-22, discloses X but is silent as to Y." Tone matters; arguments that read as personal attacks invite a final rejection.

## Use Claim Charts in the Response

Even though they are not required, including a one-page claim chart for the amended independent claim makes the examiner's job easier and dramatically increases your odds of allowance.

## Know When to Call the Examiner

A 15-minute interview after a first office action is usually free, often results in agreed-upon language, and saves a round of correspondence. If your amendment is non-trivial, call before filing the response.

## Watch Your After-Final Window

Once a final rejection issues, your options narrow. You can file an RCE (expensive, restarts examination), an after-final response, an AFCP 2.0 request, or a pre-appeal brief. Pick before you draft.
