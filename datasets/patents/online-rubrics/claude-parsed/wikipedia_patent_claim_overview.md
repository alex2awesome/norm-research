# Wikipedia: Patent Claim

## Definition

In a patent or patent application, the claims define, in technical terms, the extent of the protection conferred by a patent or the protection sought in a patent application. Claims are sentences that define the invention. The claims play the central role in patent litigation: they are the metes and bounds of the patentee's exclusive right.

## Structure of a Claim

A claim is generally a single sentence with three parts:

1. **Preamble**: Introduces the type of subject matter being claimed (e.g., "A method of," "A system for").
2. **Transitional phrase**: Connects preamble to body. "Comprising" (open), "consisting of" (closed), "consisting essentially of" (semi-closed).
3. **Body**: The list of elements/limitations of the invention.

## Independent vs. Dependent Claims

- **Independent claim**: Stands alone; defines the broadest scope sought.
- **Dependent claim**: References an earlier claim and adds further limitations. Dependent claims inherit all limitations of their parent.

A typical patent has 1-4 independent claims and many dependent claims, organized in a hierarchy.

## Categories of Claims

- **Product claims** (apparatus, machine, composition of matter, manufacture): Cover physical things.
- **Method/process claims**: Cover series of steps.
- **Use claims**: Cover use of a known thing for a new purpose (more common in EPO than USPTO).
- **Means-plus-function claims** (US): Recite a function performed by undisclosed structure; construed under 35 USC 112(f) as covering corresponding structure in the spec plus equivalents.
- **Beauregard claims** (US, software): Recite a computer-readable medium storing instructions.
- **Markush claims** (chemistry): Recite a closed group of alternatives.
- **Jepson claims**: Recite known elements in the preamble and improvement in the body; rare in US practice due to admission of preamble as prior art.
- **Swiss-type claims** (formerly EPO, second medical use): Now generally disused.

## Claim Construction

Claims are interpreted by courts during litigation. In the US, claim terms get their ordinary meaning to a person of ordinary skill in the art at the time of the invention, in light of the specification and prosecution history (Phillips v. AWH, Fed. Cir. 2005, en banc).

## Doctrine of Equivalents

A claim can be infringed not only literally but also by the doctrine of equivalents: an accused product that performs substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result (Graver Tank). The doctrine is limited by prosecution history estoppel and the all-elements rule.

## Claim Indefiniteness

Under 35 USC 112(b), a claim must "particularly point out and distinctly claim" the invention. Nautilus v. Biosig (2014) established that a claim is indefinite if its scope is not reasonably certain to a POSITA in light of the spec and prosecution history.

## Claim Differentiation

A doctrine that presumes different claims have different scope: a limitation in a dependent claim is presumed not to limit the independent claim from which it depends. Used as an interpretive aid by courts.
