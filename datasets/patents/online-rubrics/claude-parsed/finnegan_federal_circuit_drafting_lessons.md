# Finnegan Federal Circuit IP Blog: Drafting Lessons from Recent Decisions

## Lesson 1 - SciMed and "The Invention Is" Statements

In SciMed Life Systems v. Advanced Cardiovascular Systems (Fed. Cir. 2001), the spec described a coaxial design as "the invention." The Federal Circuit held that a separate claim drawn to a dual-lumen design was nonetheless construed to require the coaxial structure. **Drafting lesson**: Refer to "embodiments" or "examples"; never to "the invention."

## Lesson 2 - Phillips and the Spec as Dictionary

Phillips v. AWH (Fed. Cir. 2005, en banc) reaffirmed that claim terms get their ordinary meaning to a POSITA in light of the spec. **Drafting lesson**: Define unusual terms explicitly in the spec. Do not rely on dictionaries to do the defining for you.

## Lesson 3 - Williamson and Functional Language

Williamson v. Citrix (Fed. Cir. 2015, en banc) lowered the bar for invoking 35 USC 112(f) - functional language without sufficient structural support triggers means-plus-function construction even without the word "means." **Drafting lesson**: Use structural language ("processor," "circuit," "memory") rather than nonce words ("module," "mechanism," "unit") for non-means claims.

## Lesson 4 - Aristocrat and Algorithmic Disclosure

Aristocrat Technologies v. International Game Technology (Fed. Cir. 2008) held that for a means-plus-function claim invoking a software function, the specification must disclose an algorithm (flowchart, pseudocode, or prose) - not just "a general-purpose computer." **Drafting lesson**: For software patents, include algorithmic flowcharts in the spec.

## Lesson 5 - Nautilus and Definiteness

Nautilus v. Biosig (Sup. Ct. 2014) established the indefiniteness standard: claim scope must be "reasonably certain" to a POSITA in light of the spec and prosecution history. **Drafting lesson**: For each claim term that is not standard in the field, ensure the spec provides reasonable certainty about its scope.

## Lesson 6 - Akamai and Divided Infringement

Akamai v. Limelight (Fed. Cir. 2015, en banc) tightened the rules for direct infringement when multiple actors perform different steps of a method claim. **Drafting lesson**: Draft method claims so a single actor performs every step, or so that one actor directs/controls another.

## Lesson 7 - Nautilus, Williamson, and the Software Drafting Trap

Software patents have multiple ways to die: 101 (abstract idea), 112(b) (indefinite under Nautilus), 112(f) (means-plus-function under Williamson without algorithmic disclosure under Aristocrat). **Drafting lesson**: Address each separately. Concrete technical improvement (101), structural language (112(f)), clear definitions (112(b)), and algorithmic flowcharts (Aristocrat).

## Lesson 8 - Festo and Amendment Strategy

Festo Corp. v. Shoketsu Kinzoku Kogyo Kabushiki Co. (Fed. Cir. 2003, en banc) created a presumption that amending a claim element forecloses doctrine-of-equivalents reach for that element. **Drafting lesson**: Amend surgically and articulate the reason. Cancel cleanly if amendment cannot save a claim.

## Lesson 9 - In re Wertheim and Range Disclosure

For numerical ranges in claims, the spec must disclose the range or at least disclose representative endpoints. **Drafting lesson**: When you might want to claim "between 50 and 100," disclose multiple endpoints (e.g., 30, 50, 75, 100, 150) so any claimed range has support.

## Lesson 10 - In re Kao and Inherency

If a claimed feature is inherent in a prior art process or composition, the prior art anticipates even without expressly disclosing the feature. **Drafting lesson**: When distinguishing prior art, do not rely on features that may be inherent in the cited references; distinguish on actively chosen, disclosed features.
