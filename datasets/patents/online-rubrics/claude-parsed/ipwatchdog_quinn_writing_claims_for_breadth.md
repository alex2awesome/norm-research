# IPWatchdog: Writing Patent Claims for Maximum Breadth

## Strip Out Adjectives

Most adjectives in claims are limiting and unnecessary. "A user-friendly graphical interface" - delete "user-friendly." "A high-performance processor" - delete "high-performance." Each adjective is something an accused product can lack.

## Functional Claiming Carefully

Reciting elements by what they do (rather than what they are) is broader - but invokes 112(f) risk in U.S. practice. Threading the needle: use functional language paired with one or more concrete structural recitations ("a sensor configured to detect X, the sensor comprising Y").

## Genus, Not Species

If your invention works with springs, do not claim "a coil spring." Claim "a biasing element" - and disclose, as alternatives, coil springs, leaf springs, elastomer pads, gas springs. Each species disclosure supports a genus claim.

## Avoid Numerical Limits Unless Necessary

"At least 50% by weight" is a hard limit; an accused product at 49.9% does not infringe. If the number is not load-bearing for novelty, leave it out. If the number is needed for distinguishing prior art, use ranges with multiple endpoints disclosed in the spec.

## Watch Out for "Configured To" vs. "For"

"A processor for performing X" may invoke 112(f) post-Williamson; "a processor configured to perform X" plus a clear algorithmic disclosure in the spec is safer. The choice has scope consequences.

## Avoid Describing the Environment as a Limitation

A claim to a widget that says "for use in an automobile" - is the automobile a limitation? Maybe. If you do not need the automobile to distinguish prior art, do not include it.

## Use Open Transitional Language

"Comprising" admits additional unrecited elements. "Including" is treated similarly. "Consisting of" closes the claim. Default to comprising.

## Do Not Be Afraid of a Long Spec to Support Short Claims

Disclosure costs little; claim breadth is determined at the time of drafting and frozen. A 60-page spec supporting a 4-line claim is a fine outcome.

## Beware Negative Limitations

"Wherein the device does not include X" must be supported in the spec by an explicit statement that X is absent. Santarus v. Par (Fed. Cir. 2012). Without spec support, the negative limitation is a 112(b) problem.

## The Genus-Species Trade-Off in Biotech

In biotech, broad genus claims face heightened written-description scrutiny (Amgen v. Sanofi, 2023, on enablement). The lesson generalizes: in unpredictable arts, breadth must be earned by extensive disclosure of representative species and a unifying structural feature.
