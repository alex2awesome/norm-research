# The Interpretation-Construction Distinction: Quality Implications

Chiang & Solum reframe claim-construction debates by distinguishing two activities courts actually perform: linguistic interpretation (what the words mean) and legal construction (what legal effect to give the meaning). The distinction has direct implications for patent drafting quality.

## The Two-Stage Framework

**Stage 1: Interpretation (Linguistic Meaning)**:
Determining what the claim words communicate as a matter of language. This is largely about ordinary or technical word meaning.

**Stage 2: Construction (Legal Effect)**:
Determining what legal scope the patent receives. This involves judgments about:
- The "real invention" the inventor disclosed
- Policy goals (notice, incentive, scope-disclosure proportionality)
- Doctrinal commitments (literal scope vs. equivalents, broadest reasonable construction during prosecution)

## The Authors' Central Claim

Most claim-construction disputes are NOT about linguistic ambiguity. They are about contested theories of construction—whether scope should track linguistic meaning or the real invention. Drafting cannot eliminate construction disputes through better word choice.

## Quality Implications

### Drafting-Quality Markers (Interpretation Stage)

Even though drafting cannot solve construction problems, it should solve interpretation problems:

**High-Quality Markers**:
- Defined claim terms (especially novel or coined terms)
- Consistent terminology across claims and specification
- Use of art-recognized terms with settled meaning
- Glossary or definition section in specification

**Quality Defects**:
- Undefined coined terms
- Inconsistent use of the same term across claims
- Multiple plausible linguistic interpretations
- Reliance on prosecution-history disclaimers to narrow ambiguous language

### Construction-Stage Implications

For construction disputes, drafting strategy should:

**Anchor the "Real Invention"**:
- Specification clearly identifies the technical contribution
- Working examples illustrate the invention's scope
- Background distinguishes the invention from prior approaches

**Pre-Empt Doctrinal Disputes**:
- Use of "comprising" vs. "consisting of" with clear intent
- Proper means-plus-function indication when desired
- Express references to embodiments as illustrative vs. limiting

### Specification-Claim Coordination

Chiang & Solum's framework implies quality patents have:

- Specification that supports the construer's "real invention" inquiry
- Claims that map cleanly onto the specification's disclosed embodiments
- No mismatch between specification scope and claim scope (creating construction conflicts)

## Operative Quality Test

For any patent claim:

**Interpretation Quality**:
1. Are claim terms defined or have settled meaning?
2. Is terminology consistent across claims and specification?
3. Are there obvious linguistic ambiguities (homographs, undefined coined terms)?

**Construction Quality**:
4. Does the specification clearly identify the "real invention"?
5. Are working examples representative of the claimed scope?
6. Is the relationship between embodiments and claims explicit?
7. Does the prosecution history support a single coherent construction theory?

A patent passing all seven tests has high construction-resistance. A patent passing only the interpretation tests but failing construction tests is vulnerable to Markman volatility.

## Implications for Examination

Examiners performing "broadest reasonable construction" during prosecution effectively construct the claims at maximum breadth. Quality applicants should:

- Anticipate BRC during prosecution
- Argue for narrower construction with specification support
- Document prosecution-history positions that bind future construction
- Avoid creating estoppels that conflict with desired construction

## The Critique of "Better Drafting" Solutions

Chiang & Solum challenge a common reform proposal: that better drafting will solve construction uncertainty. They argue:

- Drafting cannot eliminate disputes about construction theory
- Even perfectly drafted claims face uncertainty about the "real invention" inquiry
- Reform must address construction theory directly, not drafting practice alone

This implies that patent quality cannot be assessed solely by drafting clarity; the courts' theoretical commitments matter independently.

## Synthesis

The Chiang-Solum framework supports a two-tier quality analysis:

**Tier 1 — Linguistic Quality**: Are the claims linguistically clear?
**Tier 2 — Construction Quality**: Does the specification support a coherent construction theory?

Patents that pass both tiers are quality-aligned for predictable construction. Patents that pass only Tier 1 are linguistically clean but construction-vulnerable. Patents that fail Tier 1 are weakest.

## Operative Markers Derived

For any issued patent:

- Are claim terms defined either explicitly or by art-recognized usage?
- Does the specification identify a coherent "real invention"?
- Are working examples representative of the claimed scope?
- Has the prosecution history committed to a single construction theory?
- Are there construction-conflict markers (specification-claim mismatch, ambiguous embodiments)?

A patent satisfying all five markers has high construction-quality and is litigation-resistant in the Markman context.
