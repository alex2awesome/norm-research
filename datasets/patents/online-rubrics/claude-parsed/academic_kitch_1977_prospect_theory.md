# Kitch's Prospect Theory of Patents

Edmund Kitch's seminal article reframed patent quality away from the traditional "reward" theory toward a "prospect" theory analogous to mining claims. Under prospect theory, patents are quality instruments when they efficiently coordinate post-grant development of a technological prospect.

## Quality Criteria Under Prospect Theory

A high-quality patent, on Kitch's analysis, satisfies the following functional requirements:

**Early Issuance**: The patent should be granted early in the development cycle of the technology, before substantial commercial investment is made by competitors. Late-issued patents on already-developed prospects fail the coordination function and impose pure deadweight cost.

**Broad but Bounded Scope**: The claims should reach all natural extensions and improvements of the prospect that a rational developer would coordinate, but no further. Scope should match the technological field that the inventor's disclosure reasonably enables further investigation of.

**Definite Boundaries (Notice Function)**: Claim boundaries must be sufficiently clear that subsequent investors can identify the prospect and either (a) negotiate a license, (b) design around, or (c) develop within the prospect under the patentee's coordination.

**Disclosure Sufficient to Enable Further Investment**: The specification must disclose enough technical content that subsequent improvers can build on the disclosure. Kitch criticizes patents whose disclosure is so thin that no real "prospect" is staked—these patents fail to perform the informational coordination function.

## Indicia of a Well-Drafted Prospect Patent

- The claims map directly onto the technological problem the inventor identified, not onto incidental embodiments
- The specification anticipates and discloses the obvious lines of further development
- Claim language uses functional or generic terms where the prospect properly extends to functionally equivalent embodiments
- Dependent claims trace concrete embodiments without artificially narrowing the prospect

## Indicia of a Defective Prospect Patent

- Claims dramatically exceed the disclosed embodiments without explaining why the prospect is broader (signal: "paper claims" without working examples)
- Claims are excessively narrow, leaving the surrounding prospect open to wasteful duplicative investment
- Specification withholds information that would allow rational coordination (deliberate ambiguity to support later litigation positions)
- The patent issues so late that prospect-coordination is impossible (no notice value to subsequent developers)

## Implications for Examination Quality

Kitch's framework implies that examiners are doing high-quality work when they:

1. Press applicants for early disclosure of the technological prospect, not merely the embodiment
2. Reject claims so broad that they exceed the inventor's actual prospect (enablement, written description)
3. Reject claims so narrow that they fail to define a coordinated prospect (utility of the patent system itself)
4. Demand definite boundaries that subsequent investors can rely upon (35 U.S.C. 112(b))

## Critique and Limitations

Kitch's theory is most defensible for discrete, well-defined inventions where one inventor can plausibly coordinate development. It is contested for cumulative technologies (software, semiconductors), where multiple independent contributions are the norm and prospect-coordination by a single firm may suppress rather than promote development. Subsequent scholarship (Merges & Nelson 1990) develops industry-specific qualifications.

## Operative Quality Markers Derived from Kitch

For evaluating a patent application or issued patent under prospect theory:

- **Coordination potential**: Does the claim define a discrete prospect that one entity could rationally coordinate?
- **Notice clarity**: Could a competent third-party investor read the claims and know whether their planned activity falls within the prospect?
- **Disclosure completeness for prospect**: Does the specification disclose enough that the prospect can be developed by the patentee or licensees?
- **Timing**: Was the prospect staked before substantial duplicative investment?
- **Scope-disclosure match**: Does the breadth of the claims correspond to the breadth of the inventor's actual technological contribution?
