# Yale Survey: Industry Appropriability and Patent Quality

The 1987 Yale survey of 650 corporate R&D managers established the foundational empirical understanding of when and why firms use patents. The findings remain the canonical reference for industry-specific patent strategy.

## Methodology

Survey of R&D managers across 130+ industries on the effectiveness of various appropriability mechanisms:

- Patents (separated into product and process patents)
- Secrecy
- Lead time
- Sales and service efforts
- Manufacturing learning curves
- Complementary assets

Effectiveness rated on a 1-7 scale across thousands of innovation cases.

## Headline Findings

**Patents Are Generally NOT the Most Effective Mechanism**: Across most industries, lead time and complementary assets rank above patents.

**Pharmaceutical Exception**: Patents rated highly effective for both product (6.5/7) and process (4.9/7) innovations in pharmaceuticals. Pharmaceuticals are the canonical "patent-dependent" industry.

**Industry-Specific Patent Effectiveness**:
- Pharmaceuticals: Very high (6.5/7)
- Inorganic chemicals: High (5.2/7)
- Organic chemicals: High (5.6/7)
- Petroleum refining: Moderate (4.3/7)
- Steel mills: Low (~3.5/7)
- Pulp and paper: Low (~2.5/7)
- Semiconductors: Low for patents; high for lead time
- Computers: Low for patents; high for complementary assets

**Process vs. Product Patent Differential**: Process patents are systematically rated less effective than product patents (~1-2 points lower on average). Reverse-engineering of processes is harder than products, making secrecy more attractive.

## Reasons Patents Are Less Effective

The Yale survey identified specific reasons firms perceive patents as less effective:

1. **Easy Design-Around**: Competitors can invent around patent claims
2. **Disclosure Cost**: Patent specification reveals information competitors can use
3. **Invalidation Risk**: Patents often held invalid in litigation
4. **Enforcement Difficulty**: Detecting infringement is hard, especially for processes
5. **Time-Limited Protection**: 17-year term (now 20-year) is short for some innovations

## Quality Implications

### Quality Patents in High-Patent-Dependence Industries

For pharmaceutical and chemical industries where patents are critical:

**Quality Markers**:
- Robust composition-of-matter claims
- Method-of-treatment / method-of-use claims
- Manufacturing process claims with detailed conditions
- International family covering major markets
- Continuation strategy preserving commercial coverage

### Quality Patents in Low-Patent-Dependence Industries

For semiconductors, computers, machinery where patents are secondary:

**Quality Markers**:
- Standard-essential coverage where applicable
- Cross-license value
- Defensive portfolio depth (vs. offensive single-patent strength)
- Coverage of own products (not just competitor products)

### Process Patent Quality

Given the systematic process-patent weakness:

**Quality Markers** (when filing process patents):
- Detection-friendly process steps (observable in product)
- Conditions narrow enough to permit detection
- Disclosure tolerable given trade-secret alternative

**When to Skip Process Patents**:
- Process is inherently undetectable in product
- Trade-secret protection durable
- Competitor reverse-engineering cost high

## Operative Quality Test for Patent-Filing Decision

The Yale survey supports an industry-calibrated quality framework. Before drafting:

1. Is this a high-patent-dependence industry (pharma, chemical, biotech)?
2. Is this a product or process innovation?
3. What is the imitation cost / lead time differential?
4. Are complementary assets available as alternative protection?
5. Will patent disclosure compromise other appropriability mechanisms?

Patents that fail this calibration test are quality-deficient at the strategic level even if technically valid.

## Comparison to Carnegie Mellon Survey (CNW 2000)

Cohen, Nelson & Walsh (2000) replicated the Yale survey in 1994 and found:

- Patent effectiveness rankings broadly similar
- Secrecy use increased between 1983 and 1994
- Strategic patenting (blocking, cross-licensing) gained importance
- Pharmaceutical dominance of patent-dependent industries persisted

The robustness of these findings across two surveys, separated by a decade, validates the Yale framework as a stable description of industry patent practice.

## Synthesis

The Yale survey supports the foundational quality principle:

**Patent quality must be evaluated relative to industry-specific appropriability conditions**.

A patent that is "high quality" technically but mismatched to its industry's appropriability needs is, in practice, low quality strategically. A patent that is "average" technically but well-matched to industry strategy is, in practice, higher quality.

Industry calibration is the first quality test, before any technical patentability analysis.
