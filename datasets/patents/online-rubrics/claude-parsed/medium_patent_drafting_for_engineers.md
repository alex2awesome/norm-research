# Patent Drafting for Engineers: What Your Patent Attorney Wishes You Knew

## Bring an Invention Disclosure, Not a Marketing Doc

A useful invention disclosure has: (1) the technical problem in operational terms, (2) the closest known prior solution and why it falls short, (3) the new solution explained as if to a competent engineer who has not seen it, (4) at least one alternative way to implement the new solution, and (5) the result data if you have any. Marketing language ("disrupts," "revolutionary," "best-in-class") is filler.

## The Difference Between a Feature and an Invention

A feature is something a user notices. An invention is a non-obvious technical mechanism that achieves a feature. "Push notifications when the package arrives" is a feature. "A geofence-triggered notification scheduler that batches push events to reduce radio wakeups" is closer to an invention.

## Whiteboards Are Better Than Slides

When you meet with the attorney, sketch on a whiteboard. Slides force you to commit to one architecture; whiteboards let you say "or alternatively..." which is exactly what your attorney needs to write a broad spec.

## Identify the "Inventive Concept" in One Sentence

If you cannot finish the sentence "The new thing is that ____," you do not yet have an invention you can claim. The blank should be filled with a mechanism, not a goal.

## Variants Matter More Than the Best Mode

The single best version of your invention is what your competitors will design around. Your attorney needs to know the worse versions, the ugly hacks, the alternative materials. Each one becomes a fallback claim.

## Prior Art You Already Know

Tell your attorney every prior solution you considered. This is not an admission of unpatentability - it is the foundation for distinguishing your work. The duty of disclosure (Rule 56) requires you to disclose what you know is material.

## The "Why Did Nobody Do This Before" Question

If a colleague asks "why didn't anyone do this before," your answers are worth gold: a technical bias was misleading the field; an enabling component just became affordable; a counter-intuitive trade-off had to be accepted. Write these down. They become the heart of the 103 non-obviousness argument.

## Do Not Publish, Demo, or Sell Before Filing

In the U.S. you have a one-year grace period (35 USC 102(b)(1)) but most other countries have absolute novelty. A conference talk, a sales pitch with a non-NDA prospect, or a public demo on the same day as filing can kill your foreign rights.
