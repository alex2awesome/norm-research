# Patently-O: Why Specification Quality Matters More Than Ever

## The Spec Is Where Cases Are Won and Lost

Federal Circuit decisions over the last decade have repeatedly turned on what the specification did or did not say. Phillips v. AWH (2005, en banc) held that claim terms get their ordinary meaning to a person of ordinary skill in the art - in light of the specification. The spec is the dictionary.

## "The Invention" - Avoid Singular Framing

When the spec says "the present invention is X" or "the invention requires Y," courts may construe every claim as limited to X or Y. SciMed v. Advanced Cardiovascular (Fed. Cir. 2001) is the leading example: the spec said the invention was a coaxial design, and a separate claim was construed not to cover dual-lumen designs.

## Use "Embodiments" Not "The Invention"

Refer to "an embodiment," "one example," "in some implementations," "a non-limiting example." This frames the spec as a collection of optional examples rather than a definition of "the invention."

## Define Terms Affirmatively When Useful

You can be your own lexicographer: if you want a claim term to have a specific meaning, define it explicitly in the spec ("As used herein, the term 'X' means..."). Courts respect express definitions.

## Avoid Definition by Negative Implication

Do not write "in contrast to prior systems, which used Y, the present system uses X." This can be read as disclaiming Y from the claim scope. Describe what your system does without the negative comparison.

## Disclose Ranges, Not Single Values

If your prototype runs at 50 Hz, the spec should say "between approximately 30 Hz and 100 Hz, e.g., 50 Hz." Single values invite narrow construction; disclosed ranges support broader claims.

## Disclose Multiple Embodiments Across Use Cases

A spec that describes one product is implicitly limited to that product. A spec that describes the invention applied to three different fields with three different embodiments forces broader construction.

## Background Section - Less Is More

Long background sections that disparage prior art create estoppel and 103 admissions. Keep the background short, factual, and avoid characterizing prior art as having "drawbacks" or "deficiencies" you have solved - or be prepared to have those statements quoted back at you.
