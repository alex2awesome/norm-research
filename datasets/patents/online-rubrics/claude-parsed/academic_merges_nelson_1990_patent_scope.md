# Merges & Nelson: Industry-Specific Patent Scope Quality

Merges & Nelson developed the foundational framework distinguishing patent scope quality across four technological regimes. The article is the leading rejoinder to Kitch's prospect theory, arguing that scope-quality requirements are highly context-dependent.

## Three Doctrinal Levers Controlling Scope

**Enablement (35 U.S.C. 112(a))**: The specification must teach a person of ordinary skill to make and use all embodiments encompassed by the claims, without undue experimentation. Merges & Nelson argue examiners should rigorously enforce enablement against claims that "stretch beyond" disclosed ground, particularly in cumulative-technology fields.

**Doctrine of Equivalents**: Courts extend literal claim language to cover devices performing substantially the same function in substantially the same way to achieve substantially the same result. Pioneer patents historically receive broader equivalents protection than improvement patents.

**Reverse Doctrine of Equivalents**: A device that literally infringes may nonetheless escape liability when it represents a meritorious improvement substantially departing from the patented principle. Merges & Nelson urge robust use of this doctrine to preserve incentives for cumulative improvers.

## Industry-Specific Scope Quality Criteria

### Discrete Inventions (Pharmaceuticals, Mechanical Standalones)

For inventions that do not function as inputs to follow-on innovation:

- Broad scope is generally tolerable because there is little risk of blocking subsequent improvers
- Claim breadth should match the experimental difficulty of identifying the invention (justifying high reward)
- Particular value in claiming compounds by structural genus where the disclosed examples support generalization
- High-quality discrete-invention claims are clear in scope and supported by representative working examples

### Cumulative Technologies (Mechanical Systems with Many Components, Software)

For technologies built from many interrelated components developed by many actors:

- Pioneer-patent breadth becomes dangerous, risking single-firm bottlenecks
- Examiners should apply enablement strictly, rejecting genus claims that exceed the inventor's actual contribution
- Reverse doctrine of equivalents and narrowing claim construction are essential safety valves
- The Edison incandescent lamp case is the cautionary example: broad enforcement halted competitive improvement
- Scope-quality marker: do the claims permit independent improvement, or do they create a blocking bottleneck?

### Chemical Industries (Process Chemistry, Industrial Synthesis)

Chemical inventions present a hybrid pattern:

- Product patents on specific compounds are typically discrete; broad scope is acceptable when justified by structural support
- Process patents tend to be cumulative; narrow scope and strict enablement are appropriate
- Cross-licensing norms historically mitigate broad-patent harms in chemical sectors
- Quality markers: chemical product claims should disclose at least one synthesis route enabling all claimed embodiments; genus claims must be supported by representative species

### Science-Based Technologies (Biotechnology, Modern Software)

For fields where scientific discovery creates immediate practical applications:

- Highest scope-quality concerns; first-applier risk of "controlling a broad array of improvements"
- Strict enablement particularly for biotechnology genus claims (cf. Amgen v. Sanofi reasoning)
- Functional/result-oriented claiming should be heavily disfavored unless the specification teaches a structural-functional correlation
- Quality marker: does the disclosure enable the claimed genus across the full scope, or does it merely identify one species and claim the genus?

## Concrete Quality Markers for Patent Drafting & Examination

**Pioneer-Patent Discipline**: Patents claiming pioneer status (i.e., a substantial leap from the prior art) must support the breadth with proportionate disclosure. A pioneer patent backed by a single embodiment is a quality red flag.

**Genus-Species Proportionality**: Claims to a genus must be supported by enough species to make the generalization credible to a person of ordinary skill. The number of species, their structural diversity, and disclosed properties should justify the genus.

**Improvement-Friendliness**: High-quality cumulative-technology claims should permit reasonable design-around. Claims drafted to capture every possible improvement create scope-quality concerns.

**Cross-Industry Borrowing Caution**: Doctrines developed in chemical or pharmaceutical contexts (broad genus claiming, doctrine of equivalents reach) should not transfer reflexively to software or biotech without revisiting the cumulative-vs-discrete analysis.

## Critique of Excessive Examiner Deference to Broad Claims

Merges & Nelson criticize the practice of allowing very broad claims when examiners cannot definitively disprove enablement of every embodiment. They argue:

- The burden of proving enablement should rest on the applicant for genus claims
- Examiners should require representative examples spanning the claimed genus
- "Paper claims" untethered to working examples are presumptive scope-quality failures
- Reasonable doubt about enablement should resolve against the applicant for broad claims

## Operative Scope-Quality Test (Synthesizing Merges & Nelson)

Ask, for any claim:

1. What technological regime governs (discrete / cumulative / chemical / science-based)?
2. Does the disclosed embodiment-set support the claimed scope under that regime's standards?
3. Would enforcement of this claim foreclose reasonable independent improvement?
4. Are the claim's outer boundaries clear enough to permit design-around?
5. Is the breadth proportionate to the inventor's demonstrated contribution to the art?
