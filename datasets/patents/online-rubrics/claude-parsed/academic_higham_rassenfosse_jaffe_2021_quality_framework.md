# Toward a Systematic Patent Quality Framework

This paper is the most rigorous critique of standard patent quality measures. Higham, de Rassenfosse & Jaffe show that popular indicators are mutually inconsistent and propose a more disciplined framework.

## Core Empirical Critique

The authors test consistency among popular post-grant quality measures including:

- Forward citation counts
- Patent renewal/lifetime
- Litigation incidence
- Family size
- Opposition outcomes
- Patent office rejections in foreign jurisdictions

**Finding**: These measures are **substantially inconsistent**. Patents identified as "high quality" by one measure are often classified differently by another. The conventional wisdom that forward citations equal quality is too simplistic.

## Quality is Multi-Dimensional

The authors argue quality must be defined relative to a specific use:

**Technological Quality**: How much the patent advances the underlying technology (citation indicators may capture this)

**Legal Quality**: How likely the patent is to survive validity challenge (litigation outcomes capture this)

**Economic Quality**: How much commercial value the patent generates (renewals, family size capture this)

**Drafting Quality**: How well-drafted the patent document is (separate from validity or value)

These dimensions are imperfectly correlated. A patent can be technologically pioneering but legally weak (foundational software patents post-Alice). A patent can be legally strong but economically minor (well-drafted patent on a niche product).

## Pre-Grant vs. Post-Grant Indicators

A central methodological contribution: pre-grant indicators (available at issuance) are more useful for many purposes than post-grant indicators (which require years of waiting):

**Pre-Grant Indicators**:
- Number of claims
- Specification length
- Number of inventors
- Examination duration and rejection count
- Backward citations
- Family size at filing
- Drafting characteristics

**Post-Grant Indicators**:
- Forward citations
- Renewals
- Litigation
- Opposition outcomes
- Foreign-jurisdiction grant rates

The authors show pre-grant indicators predict subsequent post-grant outcomes with surprising accuracy, supporting their use for early quality assessment.

## Quality Implications

### Indicator-Specific Use Cases

The framework implies different indicators for different purposes:

**For licensing valuation**: Family size + renewals + technology field
**For litigation strategy**: Examination depth + claim clarity + scope
**For policy evaluation**: Composite multi-indicator scores
**For early-stage assessment**: Pre-grant indicators (claim count, specification depth, examination signals)

### Multi-Method Validation

The authors recommend any quality assessment use **multiple consistent indicators** rather than relying on one. A patent that scores high on multiple disparate indicators is more reliably high quality than one that scores extremely high on a single metric.

### Selection Effects in Each Indicator

**Forward citations**: Confounded by examiner citation practices, art-unit norms, time-to-citation lag
**Renewals**: Confounded by maintenance-fee timing, firm cash-flow, defensive holding
**Litigation**: Confounded by selection (only high-stakes patents litigated)
**Family size**: Confounded by firm international strategy independent of patent quality

## Operative Quality Markers Derived

**For Pre-Grant Quality Assessment**:
- Specification depth and number of working examples
- Number of independent claims
- Examination duration (longer = more substantive)
- Number of office actions before allowance
- Number of substantive (102/103/112) rejections in prosecution
- Examiner-cited prior art beyond applicant IDS

**For Post-Grant Quality Assessment**:
- Forward citations vs. cohort
- Renewal pattern through full term
- Family size, particularly triadic
- Opposition / IPR outcomes
- Foreign-jurisdiction grant patterns

**For Composite Quality**:
- Use 4+ indicators across both pre- and post-grant categories
- Require consistency: high-quality patents should score above median on most indicators
- Treat single-indicator outliers as suspect

## Critique of Single-Indicator Practice

Higham et al. specifically warn against:

- Citing forward-citation count alone as quality measure (high noise; multiple confounders)
- Treating renewal as proxy for quality without controlling for firm cash position
- Using litigation outcomes as quality measure without selection-effect correction
- Relying on family size alone (firm-strategy-driven)

## Policy and Methodological Implications

The framework supports:

- Skepticism of patent-quality scores reported by commercial data providers (PatentSight, Innography) that use opaque single-indicator-dominant methodologies
- Use of academic multi-indicator scores (OECD, Lanjouw-Schankerman) for policy work
- Pre-grant indicator development for real-time examination quality monitoring
- Distinct quality assessment for distinct decision contexts (licensing vs. litigation vs. R&D strategy)

## Operative Test

For any patent quality assessment:

1. Identify the decision context (licensing, litigation, valuation, R&D, policy)
2. Select indicators relevant to that context (technological / legal / economic / drafting quality)
3. Use 4+ indicators rather than 1
4. Verify cross-indicator consistency (high-quality patents should not be top-quartile on one and bottom-quartile on others)
5. Acknowledge measurement uncertainty; treat single-indicator results with appropriate skepticism

A patent that consistently scores high across multiple disparate indicators (citations + renewals + family + examination depth) is high quality with confidence. A patent with inconsistent scores requires further investigation before quality classification.
