# MPEP 2141: Examination Guidelines for Determining Obviousness Under 35 U.S.C. 103

## The Graham v. John Deere Factors

The Supreme Court established three foundational factual inquiries that examiners must address:

1. **Scope and Content of Prior Art**: Determining what prior art exists in the relevant field, including references from the same or different fields of endeavor that address related problems.

2. **Differences Between Prior Art and Claims**: Identifying what distinguishes the claimed invention from existing prior art disclosures.

3. **Level of Ordinary Skill**: Determining the education, experience, and problem-solving capability of a hypothetical person ordinarily skilled in the relevant art at the time of the invention.

### Secondary Considerations

Objective evidence may rebut obviousness, including: commercial success, long-felt but unsolved needs, failure of others, and unexpected results.

## KSR International v. Teleflex Framework

The Supreme Court rejected rigid application of the teaching-suggestion-motivation (TSM) test and established that obviousness may be found when:

- **Predictable results** occur from combining familiar elements using known methods
- **Familiar techniques** improve similar devices in foreseeable ways
- A **finite number of predictable solutions** exists ("obvious to try")
- **Design incentives or market forces** prompt variations across fields

The Court emphasized that persons of ordinary skill possess "ordinary creativity" and can "fit teachings of multiple patents together like puzzle pieces."

## Seven Rationales Supporting Obviousness

Examiners may rely on these approaches (per KSR and Federal Circuit precedent):

**(A) Predictable Combinations**: Combining known elements using established methods yielding expected results.

**(B) Simple Substitution**: Replacing one known element with another to achieve predictable outcomes.

**(C) Known Technique Application**: Applying a recognized technique to similar devices in comparable ways.

**(D) Ready-for-Improvement Application**: Using known techniques on devices already recognized as needing enhancement.

**(E) Obvious to Try**: Selecting from limited, identified, predictable alternatives with reasonable success expectations.

**(F) Cross-Field Variations**: Adapting solutions from one field to another when market forces or design considerations justify the transfer.

**(G) Prior Art Teaching/Suggestion**: Explicit or implicit motivation in references to modify or combine teachings.

## Analogous Art Requirement

A reference must be "analogous art" to support obviousness rejection. Two separate tests apply:

1. **Same Field of Endeavor**: The reference addresses the same general technological area, regardless of whether it solves an identical problem.

2. **Reasonably Pertinent to Problem**: The reference addresses the inventor's identified problem, even if from a different technological field. An inventor facing that problem would reasonably consult such references.

**No analogous art requirement exists for anticipation (35 U.S.C. 102) rejections.**

## Key Principles for Examiners

**Factual Findings Required**: Examiners must articulate explicit findings regarding prior art scope, differences, and skill level, supported by reasoned explanation—not conclusory statements.

**Whole Invention Analysis**: The claimed invention must be evaluated as a complete system, not reduced to isolated "gist" or "thrust" elements. Inherent properties disclosed in specifications count as part of "the whole."

**Hindsight Avoidance**: Analysis must reflect knowledge available at the relevant time (before effective filing date under AIA; at time of invention under pre-AIA law), not inventor insights.

**Prior Art Considered Entirely**: References must be assessed in their entirety, including portions teaching away from claims, though disclosure of alternative approaches alone doesn't constitute teaching away.

## Applicant's Rebuttal Burden

Once examiners establish a prima facie obviousness case with proper Graham findings, the burden shifts to applicants to:

- Demonstrate examiner factual errors, or
- Provide evidence of nonobviousness (secondary considerations or unexpected results)

Mere assertions that common knowledge is "unsupported" without substantive traversal of specific findings are insufficient.

## Level of Ordinary Skill Factors

Examiners consider:
- Problem types encountered in the art
- Existing solutions to those problems
- Rate of innovation advancement
- Technology sophistication
- Education levels of active practitioners

These factors guide what would be within the creative capacity of a skilled person, balancing against hindsight bias and examiner technical expertise.
