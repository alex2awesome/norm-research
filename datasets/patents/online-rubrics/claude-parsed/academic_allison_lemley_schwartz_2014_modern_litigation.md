# Modern Patent Litigation: Industry-Specific Quality Outcomes

This study analyzes all substantive decisions in patent cases filed in 2008-2009. Their data establishes industry-specific patent-quality patterns relevant to evaluating any individual patent's expected enforcement outcome.

## Headline Empirical Findings

**Patent-Holders Lose ~75% of Definitive Merits Decisions**: Across all technology areas, patentees lose nearly three-quarters of cases that reach a definitive merits ruling.

**Trial Outcomes Differ from Pretrial**: When cases proceed to trial, patentees win approximately 60% of the time. The difference reflects selection: weak patents are eliminated at summary judgment.

**Industry-Specific Win Rates**:
- **Chemical patents**: Found valid AND infringed >50% of the time
- **Biotechnology patents**: Win in only 5.6% of merits-decided cases
- **Software patents**: Win in fewer than 1 in 7 (~14%) merits-decided cases
- **Mechanical, electronic**: Intermediate

## Quality Implications by Industry

### Pharmaceutical / Chemical
Highest enforcement success rates. Quality markers:
- Compound claims with structural specificity
- Robust ANDA defenses
- Clear scope tied to formulation or compound

A pharmaceutical patent that survives Markman with broad construction has high probability of winning at trial.

### Biotechnology
Surprisingly poor enforcement (5.6%). Quality concerns:
- Genus claims often fail enablement (cf. Amgen v. Sanofi)
- Functional antibody claims highly vulnerable
- Method-of-treatment eligibility risks (Mayo)

Quality biotech patents must address each of: enablement of full genus, written description showing possession, eligibility-safe claim format.

### Software
Low enforcement success (<14%). Quality markers:
- Specific technical implementation, not abstract result
- Claims tied to particular hardware or improvement, not generic computer
- Survival of Alice/Mayo motion-to-dismiss is the key quality threshold

### Computer Hardware / Semiconductors
Moderate enforcement; standard-essential patents perform better than non-essential.

## Methodological Importance

The study covers all 5,000+ patent cases from a two-year window, examining both validity AND infringement (not just validity). This dual analysis matters because:

- A patent can be valid but not infringed (claims too narrow)
- A patent can be infringed but invalid (claims too broad)
- Quality requires the **simultaneous** survival of both validity and infringement findings

## Quality Markers from the Joint Validity-Infringement Analysis

A "winning" patent in modern litigation exhibits:

**Goldilocks Scope**: Broad enough to read on competitor products, narrow enough to escape prior art. Quality requires careful claim drafting that places asserted claims in this narrow band.

**Multiple Asserted Claim Layers**: Patentees typically assert several claims at varying breadth. Quality patents have a defensible mid-tier claim that can survive validity while still reading on the accused product.

**Clear Construction Anchors**: Claim terms with unambiguous construction (defined in spec, prosecution-history-supported, or settled in art) reduce Markman risk.

**Eligibility-Safe Drafting**: Particularly for software, biotech, and diagnostics, claims must avoid the "abstract idea / natural phenomenon plus conventional steps" pattern.

## Selection Effects and Quality Inference

Allison, Lemley & Schwartz emphasize selection effects:
- Patents selected for litigation are systematically the strongest (or the most aggressively asserted)
- The 25% patentee-win rate among litigated patents implies even lower expected validity in the unlitigated population
- Industry differences may partly reflect differential selection (weak software patents may be more likely to be litigated than weak chemical patents)

## Operative Quality Test

For evaluating an issued patent's expected litigation outcome:

1. What is the technology area's baseline win rate?
2. Where do the asserted claims sit on the breadth-narrowness tradeoff?
3. Is the prosecution history clean (no estoppels, consistent claim interpretation)?
4. Are the claims survivable under industry-specific eligibility/enablement law?
5. Is there evidence the accused product clearly falls within the literal claim language?

A patent that scores high on all five questions in a high-base-rate industry (chemical, pharma) is among the strongest possible. The same scoring in a low-base-rate industry (biotech, software) may still have low expected outcomes due to industry-wide structural challenges.

## Continuity Finding

Despite dramatic changes in patent law (Bilski, Mayo, Alice, KSR, eBay), the basic outcomes of patent litigation showed surprising continuity over the 1990s-2010s. This implies quality assessment frameworks need not be rebuilt with each Supreme Court decision; the underlying empirical patterns are stable.
