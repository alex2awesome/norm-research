# Examiner Time Allocation and Patent Quality

Frakes & Wasserman empirically demonstrate that the USPTO's examiner-time-allocation system causes systematic quality failures. Their work supplies a quantitative basis for many quality-relevant prosecution markers.

## Core Empirical Finding

Patent examiners' allocated time per application is reduced as they are promoted along the General Schedule (GS-7 to GS-14). When examiner time falls roughly in half across this promotion ladder:

- **Grant rates rise by 9 to 19 percentage points** (a 13-28% relative increase)
- The marginal patents granted are of **below-average quality** (measured by litigation invalidation, IPR institution, abandonment for failure to pay maintenance fees)
- Search behavior degrades: fewer prior-art citations added, less foreign-language and non-patent-literature art identified

## Examiner Behaviors Correlated with Quality Loss

The paper documents specific examination shortcuts that correlate with reduced time:

**Reduced Search**: Time-pressed examiners cite less prior art on average. The drop is concentrated in references the examiner had to find independently (vs. applicant-supplied art via IDS).

**First-Action Allowances**: Time pressure increases the share of applications allowed without any rejection. First-action allowances of broad claims are a quality red flag.

**Reduced Restriction Practice**: Examiners short on time are less likely to issue restriction requirements that would force separate examination of distinct inventions.

**Lower Rejection Counts**: The number of substantive 102/103/112 rejections per application falls with examiner promotion.

## Quality Outcomes for Time-Pressured Grants

Patents granted under tighter time constraints are more likely to:

- Be invalidated in subsequent litigation
- Be challenged in inter partes review
- Be abandoned via non-payment of maintenance fees (signal of low commercial value or anticipated invalidity)
- Generate more downstream litigation per patent

## Operative Quality Markers Derived from This Work

For evaluating examination quality on a given application:

**Time-Adequacy Indicators**:
- Number and substantive depth of office actions
- Number of independent prior-art references cited by examiner (not applicant)
- Presence of non-patent literature, foreign-language, or non-class references
- Substantive engagement with 112 issues, not just 102/103

**Red Flags for Time-Constrained Examination**:
- First-action allowance of broad genus or method claims
- Examiner cites only applicant-submitted art
- Final claims unchanged from filed claims
- No restriction requirement despite presence of distinct inventions
- Rapid prosecution (one or two short office actions before allowance)

**Examiner-Level Confounders**:
- Senior (high-GS) examiners systematically allocated less time per application
- High-GS allowance rates should be discounted accordingly
- Examiner art-unit assignment is quasi-random, so cross-application examiner identity matters

## Policy and Practice Implications

Frakes & Wasserman argue that PTO time-budgets, set in the 1970s and partially indexed to GS grade, are obsolete given modern claim complexity. Quality-improving reforms include:

- Increasing examiner time per application (especially at senior grades)
- Decoupling time-budgets from GS promotion
- Funding adequate search infrastructure (databases, technical training)
- Tracking downstream invalidation as a feedback signal to examination units

## Synthesis: Quality-Dependence on Time

The paper supports a strong inferential claim relevant to evaluating any individual patent grant: holding the quality of the underlying invention constant, the quality of the issued patent (its likelihood of surviving validity challenge) depends materially on whether the examiner had adequate time. Indicators of constrained time should be treated as evidence that the grant decision is less reliable.
