# Doctrine of Equivalents - Patent Claim Scope Beyond Literal Language

## What is the Doctrine of Equivalents?

The doctrine of equivalents is a legal rule that allows a court to hold a party liable for patent infringement if the infringing device or process does not fall within the literal scope of a patent claim but is nevertheless equivalent to the claimed invention.

It is a judicially created rule allowing a patentee to assert infringement even if an accused product or process does not literally infringe.

## Purpose and Scope Extension

Judge Learned Hand described its purpose: "to temper unsparing logic and prevent an infringer from stealing the benefit of the invention."

The Doctrine of Equivalents enlarges the scope of patent protection beyond literal claim scope.

## How It Works in Practice

### Element-by-Element Application
The doctrine of equivalents analysis is applied to **individual claim limitations**, not to the invention as a whole.

### Insubstantial Difference Test
The legal test (Warner-Jenkinson v. Hilton Davis, 1997): whether the difference between the feature in the accused device and the limitation literally recited in the patent claim is **"insubstantial."**

### Function-Way-Result Test (Graver Tank)
Originally articulated test: An accused element is equivalent if it performs:
- Substantially the same FUNCTION
- In substantially the same WAY
- To achieve substantially the same RESULT

## Key Limitations on the Doctrine

### Prosecution History Estoppel
If the patentee abandoned certain literal claim coverage through amendment (narrowing the literal scope), the patentee is estopped from later arguing that the surrendered coverage is insubstantially different.

After Festo (2002): Presumptive surrender for narrowing amendments related to patentability, but applicant can rebut showing:
- The equivalent was unforeseeable at the time of amendment
- The rationale underlying the amendment bears no more than a tangential relation to the equivalent
- There is some other reason suggesting that the patentee could not reasonably have been expected to describe the equivalent

### Ensnarement Doctrine
A patentee may not assert "a scope of equivalency that would encompass, or ensnare, the prior art."

### Public Dedication Rule
A patentee may not invoke the doctrine of equivalents to recapture subject matter disclosed but not claimed in a patent.

### All-Elements Rule
Each limitation in a claim must have an equivalent in the accused device or process. Cannot read out a claim limitation entirely.

## International Perspectives

### Germany - Schneidmesser's Questions
1. Does the variant solve the problem with means objectively having the same effect?
2. Would the skilled person realize at the priority date that the variant has the same effect?
3. Are these considerations based on the meaning of the patent's teachings?

### UK - Improver Questions / Actavis Test
Modern UK approach (Actavis v. Eli Lilly, 2017):
- Does the variant achieve substantially the same result in substantially the same way?
- Would it be obvious to the skilled person?
- Would the skilled person have concluded the patentee intended strict compliance with the literal meaning?

## Strategic Drafting Implications

To maximize doctrine of equivalents protection:
- Avoid unnecessary narrowing during prosecution
- Cover obvious variants in dependent claims
- Disclose alternatives in the specification (but don't dedicate them)
- Avoid arguments creating prosecution history estoppel

To minimize equivalents impact (defendants):
- Identify amendments and arguments during prosecution
- Search for surrendered subject matter
- Find disclosed-but-unclaimed variants (public dedication)
- Identify prior art that would be ensnared
