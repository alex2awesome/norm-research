# IPWatchdog: Common Patent Application Mistakes by Inventors

## Mistake 1 - Filing Too Early

A provisional filed before you have actually figured out the invention buys you priority for a half-baked idea. You then spend the next year evolving the invention - and your non-provisional claims will not be supported by the provisional. Wait until you can describe the invention in detail.

## Mistake 2 - Filing Too Late

The opposite trap. Public use, sale, offer for sale, or printed publication more than one year before filing is a 102(b) bar in the US (the AIA grace period was preserved for the inventor's own disclosures). In most foreign countries, any public disclosure before filing is fatal. Demos at trade shows count.

## Mistake 3 - Treating the Patent As a Marketing Document

Inventors love to write that their invention is "revolutionary," "the first of its kind," "superior to all existing solutions." None of these statements help patentability. They can be used by accused infringers to attack obviousness ("if you say it is revolutionary, then secondary considerations...") and 112 ("if you say it is the first, prove the prior art does not anticipate").

## Mistake 4 - Insufficient Disclosure of How It Works

Patent law requires enablement: a POSITA must be able to make and use the invention without undue experimentation. Inventors often describe what their invention does without describing how. A black-box description fails enablement.

## Mistake 5 - Disclosing Only the Commercial Embodiment

If you only describe the version you sell, your competitors can design around with minor variations. Disclose alternatives - different materials, different geometries, different orderings, different control schemes. The spec is your safety net.

## Mistake 6 - Letting the Inventor Write the Claims

Even technical inventors who can write a perfectly clear textbook chapter typically cannot write good claims. Claims have arcane drafting conventions (single-sentence, antecedent basis, specific transitional phrases) and strategic considerations (claim differentiation, claim hierarchy, infringement-detectability). Hire a patent attorney or registered agent.

## Mistake 7 - Failing to Update Inventorship

Inventorship is who actually conceived the claimed subject matter, not who paid for the work or who manages the team. Wrongly listing inventors can invalidate the patent. Reassess inventorship after each claim amendment.

## Mistake 8 - Ignoring the Duty of Disclosure

Inventors and attorneys have a duty (Rule 56) to disclose information material to patentability. If you know about a prior reference, disclose it via an Information Disclosure Statement (IDS). Inequitable conduct findings can render an entire patent unenforceable.

## Mistake 9 - Skipping the Search

Filing without a prior art search is filing blind. A $1,500 search before filing routinely surfaces references that, had they been known, would have led to a different (and stronger) claim strategy.

## Mistake 10 - Not Planning the Continuation Strategy

Treat the first filing as the start of a portfolio, not a one-and-done. Plan continuations, divisionals, and foreign filings as part of an integrated budget.
