# Rational Ignorance at the Patent Office: Quality Implications

Lemley's article is the canonical economic analysis of patent examination quality. He argues that, because most patents are never enforced, exhaustive examination is socially wasteful, and the system tolerates a known invalid-patent rate as the price of low-cost grant.

## Key Empirical Premises

- Approximately 95% of patents are never licensed or litigated; their validity is never tested
- Of patents that are litigated to a final decision, roughly 46% are held invalid (Allison & Lemley 1998)
- The marginal cost of more thorough examination is high; the social benefit is concentrated in the few percent of patents whose validity matters
- Litigation provides a more searching validity inquiry than ex parte examination

## Quality Implications for Examination

Lemley's analysis implies the following quality-relevant criteria for examination:

**Triage Toward High-Stakes Patents**: Effective examination should concentrate scrutiny on patents most likely to be commercially valuable (e.g., pharmaceutical compositions, claims drafted with broad commercial reach). Ex post mechanisms (post-grant review, IPR) should bear the validity-determination burden for the rest.

**Disclosure-Based Quality**: Even patents that will never be litigated must serve the disclosure function. Examiner quality work includes ensuring claims are clearly tied to specification disclosure even when validity will not be tested.

**Notice Quality**: Claims must be clear enough that a competitor reading the patent can determine its scope without litigation. Failure of notice (vague boundaries, functional language untethered to structure) is a quality defect even if the patent is never enforced.

## Concrete Markers of Low-Quality Examination

- Routine grants without substantive prior-art rejections
- First-action allowances of broad genus claims without examiner-cited art
- Examiner reliance solely on applicant-cited art (failing to add references via independent search)
- Allowance of claims using functional language without 112(f) treatment or written description support
- High allowance rates correlated with experienced-examiner short-cutting (cf. Lemley & Sampat 2008)

## Markers of High-Quality Examination

- Multiple substantive office actions identifying prior art outside the applicant's submitted IDS
- Examiner-initiated claim amendments that narrow overbroad terms
- Citation of foreign-language or non-patent literature prior art
- Substantive 112 rejections (enablement, written description, indefiniteness) rather than purely 102/103
- Final claim language that maps clearly onto specification disclosure

## Critical Quality Dimensions for Issued Patents

Even accepting Lemley's "rational ignorance" frame, an issued patent should still satisfy:

1. **Enablement**: A POSITA could practice the full claim scope from the disclosure
2. **Written Description**: The applicant possessed the full claim scope at filing
3. **Notice**: Claim boundaries are knowable without expensive claim-construction litigation
4. **Subject-Matter Eligibility**: The claim does not preempt an abstract idea or natural phenomenon
5. **Non-Obviousness**: A POSITA reviewing the cited art would not have arrived at the claim

## Reform Proposals (Quality-Relevant)

Lemley's proposed reforms target quality at the points where it matters:

- **Tiered Examination**: Optional gold-plated examination for applicants who want stronger validity presumptions
- **Robust Post-Grant Review**: Cheap administrative challenges to weed out invalid patents that become commercially significant
- **Reduced Validity Presumption**: Issued patents should not enjoy strong presumptions of validity given the limits of ex ante examination

## Operative Test for Examination Quality

Ask, for any examined patent:

- Did the examiner conduct an independent search beyond applicant-cited art?
- Were the substantive 112 issues (enablement, written description, indefiniteness) addressed?
- Are the final claims clearly tied to specification disclosure?
- Would a competent third party reading the patent know what is and isn't covered?
- Does the prosecution history show genuine engagement with patentability, not formulaic exchanges?
