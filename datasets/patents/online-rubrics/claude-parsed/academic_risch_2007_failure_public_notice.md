# Public Notice Failures and Patent Quality

Risch identifies a structural quality failure: patents do not provide consistent, predictable notice of their scope to potential infringers, licensees, or even the patent office itself. The notice failure stems from divergent claim interpretation across stages.

## The Notice-Interpretation Mismatch

**Prosecution Stage**: Examiner applies "broadest reasonable construction" (BRC). Claims are interpreted as broadly as the language permits.

**Litigation Stage**: Court applies Phillips v. AWH framework, interpreting claims based on intrinsic evidence (specification, prosecution history, claim language). Claims are typically construed more narrowly than BRC.

The two interpretation methods produce different claim scopes for the same words. This means:

- Examiner allows claim under BRC (broad) → Court construes narrowly under Phillips → Mismatch
- Public reading the patent cannot tell which scope governs
- Notice function fails

## Quality Implications

### Notice-Quality Markers

A patent provides high-quality notice when:

**Claim Scope is Predictable**:
- Examiner's BRC and litigation construction would yield similar scopes
- Claim terms are defined in specification with operational consequences
- Prosecution history clearly documents claim-scope commitments
- No latent ambiguities resolved differently by examiner vs. court

**Specification Supports Notice**:
- Embodiments are clearly disclosed and representative
- Prior art distinguished with specific technical reasons
- Definitions section or glossary present for novel terms
- Background articulates the scope of the invention

### Notice-Quality Defects

**Claim Drafting Defects**:
- Functional language without 112(f) treatment
- Coined terms without definition
- Multi-word phrases with multiple plausible meanings
- Inconsistent terminology between independent and dependent claims

**Specification Defects**:
- Embodiments inconsistent with claim language
- Missing definitions for novel claim terms
- Background that conflicts with claim scope
- Examples that imply different scope than the claims state

**Prosecution-Stage Defects**:
- Applicant arguments that contradict later litigation positions
- Acquiescence to broad BRC during prosecution but narrow construction in litigation
- Unaddressed examiner observations that create ambiguity

## Operative Quality Test

For any issued patent:

1. **BRC-Phillips Convergence**: Would the examiner's BRC and a litigation court's Phillips construction yield substantially similar scopes?
2. **Claim Term Definition**: Are key claim terms defined explicitly in the specification?
3. **Prosecution-Litigation Consistency**: Has the applicant taken consistent claim-scope positions across stages?
4. **Embodiment Representativeness**: Do specification embodiments match the claimed scope?
5. **Third-Party Predictability**: Could a competent third party reading the patent predict whether their planned activity falls within the scope?

A patent passing all five tests provides high-quality notice. A patent failing them creates the structural notice-failure Risch identifies.

## "Winning" Prosecution Arguments and Indefiniteness

Risch's framework illuminates a paradox: arguments that "win" allowance during prosecution can backfire by creating indefiniteness vulnerabilities:

- Applicant argues claim term has narrow meaning to overcome prior art rejection
- Prosecution history binds future construction to that narrow meaning
- Examiner's BRC reasoning conflicts with the litigation construction
- Indefiniteness or claim-construction disputes arise

Quality prosecution avoids "winning" arguments that create downstream notice failures.

## Reform Proposals (Quality-Relevant)

Risch proposes:

**1. Unified Construction Standard**: Apply Phillips at both prosecution and litigation, eliminating the BRC-Phillips mismatch.

**2. Mandatory Claim Term Definitions**: Require applicants to define key claim terms explicitly.

**3. Prosecution-History Binding**: Make prosecution-history estoppels more rigorously applied to constrain later litigation.

**4. Pre-Grant Public Comment**: Allow third parties to flag claim-scope ambiguities before grant.

## Operative Markers Derived

**Strong Notice Markers**:
- Definitions section in specification
- Examiner reasoning aligned with applicant's claim-scope positions
- Consistent terminology across claims and specification
- Prosecution history with explicit claim-scope commitments

**Weak Notice Markers**:
- Coined terms without definition
- Examiner's BRC reasoning differs from applicant's claim-scope arguments
- Inconsistent terminology
- Specification embodiments narrower than claim language

## Synthesis

Risch's framework supports the quality principle: **patent quality is a function of notice clarity, and notice clarity requires alignment between examiner-stage and court-stage interpretation**.

For evaluating any issued patent:

1. What is the BRC scope, and what is the likely Phillips scope?
2. Are they substantially similar?
3. Does the specification support the desired construction?
4. Does the prosecution history bind the scope predictably?
5. Could a competent third party predict scope without litigation?

A patent providing reliable answers to all five questions is high notice-quality. The vast majority of issued patents fail this test, supporting Risch's broader claim of systemic notice failure.
