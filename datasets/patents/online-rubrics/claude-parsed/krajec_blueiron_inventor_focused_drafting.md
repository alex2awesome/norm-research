# BlueIron IP (Russ Krajec): Inventor-Focused Patent Drafting

## Patents Are Investment Assets, Not Compliance Documents

Krajec's central thesis: a patent is a financial asset. Drafting choices should be evaluated by their effect on the asset's value (licensing potential, enforcement potential, defensive value at acquisition). A patent that is "filed" is not the goal; a patent that creates economic value is.

## The Investor's Lens on Claim Scope

A patent's value is roughly proportional to the size of the market its claims cover. A claim that reads on a $10M niche is worth less than one that reads on a $1B mass market - all else equal. When choosing claim scope, ask: what is the market my broadest accurate claim reads on?

## Detectability Drives Enforceability

A claim that requires reverse-engineering an internal algorithm is hard to enforce. A claim that reads on observable user behavior, network traffic, or physical product features is enforceable without litigation discovery. Detectability should be a first-order drafting consideration.

## File Internal-Process Patents Sparingly

Internal manufacturing processes, server-side algorithms, and back-office workflows are often poor patent candidates: hard to detect infringement, easy to keep as trade secrets. Trade secret protection may be stronger.

## Claims as Marketing for Acquirers

When a startup is acquired, the acquirer's IP team reviews the patent portfolio. A portfolio with claims that obviously read on competitors' products, or that obviously cover the company's flagship product, signals quality. A portfolio with vague aspirational claims signals filed-and-forgotten quality.

## Don't Over-Index on Issuance

Issuance is a milestone, not the goal. A patent that issued with overly narrow claims because the applicant gave up early in prosecution is worth less than a pending application with broader claims still being negotiated. Some applications should be appealed; some should be RCE'd; some should be abandoned and refiled.

## File on the Crown Jewel First

Identify the 1-2 features of your product that are (a) hardest for competitors to design around and (b) most central to customer value. File on those first. Defensive filings on minor features can wait.

## Document the Inventive Story

Beyond the spec, keep a record of: when the invention was conceived, what prior art the inventor knew of, what alternatives were considered, what failures occurred. This is the secondary considerations file - useful in 103 arguments and in litigation.

## Patent Family Architecture

A serious portfolio includes a parent, multiple continuations claiming different scopes, and foreign counterparts. Plan the family architecture at filing, not retroactively.

## Maintenance Fees as Portfolio Audits

The 3.5, 7.5, and 11.5 year maintenance fee deadlines are good times to audit each patent: is it still commercially relevant? does it still cover competitors' products? would a continuation add value? Many portfolios benefit from selective abandonment.
