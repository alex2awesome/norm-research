# Doctrine of Claim Differentiation

## Definition

The doctrine of claim differentiation is a principle used in patent law that asserts each claim within a patent should be interpreted as having a distinct meaning and scope. Two claims in the same patent will not have identical scope, but instead there is likely an intended difference in scope.

## Basic Principle

The doctrine holds that "the presence of a dependent claim that adds a particular limitation gives rise to a presumption that the limitation in question is not present in the independent claim."

Dependent claims, which rely on independent claims, are interpreted in a way that makes them more specific or limiting than the independent claims they reference.

## How It Works

Example: If a patent has a broad claim for a chemical compound and a narrower claim for that compound derived from salicylic acid, the doctrine would lead a court to presume the broad claim encompasses various types of compounds, not just salicylic acid derivatives.

The doctrine prevents broader claims from being interpreted so narrowly that dependent claims become redundant.

## Strength and Limitations

The Federal Circuit has described claim differentiation as a "firmly established" and "rebuttable" presumption. It's not an absolute rule, but it is a powerful starting point in any claim construction analysis.

If a claim will bear only one interpretation, similarity will have to be tolerated.

## Drafting Implications

- Use dependent claims to add specific limitations
- Each dependent claim should add a meaningfully different limitation
- This creates a presumption that the broader independent claim does not require those specific limitations
- Helps prevent narrow constructions of independent claims

## Limitations of the Doctrine

The presumption can be rebutted by:
- Clear disavowal in the specification
- Prosecution history showing the applicant intended a narrow construction
- The need to give meaning to the claim language as a whole
