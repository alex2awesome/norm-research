# Anticipat: Empirical Analysis of Office Action Response Strategies

## Argue-Only vs. Amend-and-Argue

Across a large dataset of office action responses, argue-only responses are allowed at substantially lower rates than amend-and-argue responses. The difference is large in 103 (obviousness) rejections, where amendment is often necessary to overcome a multi-reference combination.

## Single-Reference 102 Rejections - Argument Often Suffices

For 102 (anticipation) rejections, careful argument that the cited reference does not disclose every claim element can succeed without amendment. The reference either does or does not anticipate; if it does not, no amendment is needed.

## 101 Eligibility Rejections - Amendment Often Helps

For 101 rejections, adding technical-improvement language and concrete structural elements often helps - even when the underlying argument is that the claim was eligible as filed. Amendments that recite specific hardware tied to the claimed function move the needle.

## 112 Rejections - Almost Always Require Amendment

112 rejections (definiteness, written description, enablement) typically require amendment to resolve. Pure argument that the claim is definite or the spec supports it rarely succeeds without restructuring.

## Examiner Interview Effect

Office action responses preceded by an examiner interview have substantially higher allowance rates than responses without an interview. The effect is largest for complex cases with multiple rejections.

## After-Final Strategy

After-final responses face higher rejection rates than first responses to non-final actions. The most effective after-final strategies are: AFCP 2.0 (the after-final consideration pilot), pre-appeal brief request, or RCE with substantive amendment. Pure argument after final is least likely to succeed.

## Appeal vs. RCE

Appeals to the PTAB succeed roughly 30-40% of the time depending on art unit; RCEs roughly 60-70% of the time eventually result in allowance. Appeals are slower (1-2 years) but cheaper if successful; RCEs are faster but cost an RCE fee plus additional examination rounds.

## Citing the MPEP

Office action responses that cite specific MPEP sections supporting the argument tend to be more persuasive than purely substantive arguments. Examiners are trained on the MPEP and respond to MPEP-grounded arguments.

## Citing Federal Circuit Cases

Citing Federal Circuit cases for legal propositions (e.g., Phillips for claim construction, Williamson for 112(f)) is appropriate but should be selective. One or two well-placed citations beat a string-cite of every relevant case.

## Long Responses Are Not Better

Empirically, response length is weakly correlated with allowance. Short, focused responses that address each rejection on its own terms perform as well as long responses. Padding hurts clarity.

## Final Tip - Read the Examiner's Cited Passage

A common pattern: the examiner cites a reference at "col. 5, lines 12-25" and the cited passage does not in fact disclose what the examiner says it does. Always pull the cited passage and quote it back to the examiner if it does not support the rejection.
