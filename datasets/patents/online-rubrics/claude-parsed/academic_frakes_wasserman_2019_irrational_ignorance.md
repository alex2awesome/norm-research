# Irrational Ignorance: The Cost-Benefit Case Against Lemley 2001

Frakes & Wasserman directly challenge Lemley's "rational ignorance" thesis. Using their own empirical results on examiner time, they argue ex ante examination is the cheaper quality mechanism than ex post litigation.

## Headline Cost-Benefit Result

**Doubling examiner time per application**:
- Direct cost: ~$660 million per year (paid via user fees)
- Litigation savings: $491 million per year
- PTAB savings: $112 million per year
- Prosecution savings: $301 million per year
- Total savings: $904 million per year
- **Net benefit**: ~$244 million per year

The PTO is therefore "irrationally ignorant"—underinvesting in examination relative to the social-cost-minimizing optimum.

## Quality-Relevant Empirical Foundation

Frakes & Wasserman's cost-benefit calculation relies on their earlier empirical findings:

**Marginal Time → Quality**: Each additional hour of examiner time reduces grant probability of marginal applications by a measurable amount, and the patents not granted on the margin are the ones most likely to be invalidated downstream.

**Litigation Concentration**: A small fraction of granted patents account for most litigation costs; better examination at the margin disproportionately prevents these high-cost downstream cases.

**PTAB Predictability**: Patents that survive thorough examination are less likely to be instituted into IPR; PTAB savings reflect avoided low-quality grants.

## Quality Implications for Examination

The paper's policy framework implies the following quality-relevant criteria:

**Examiner Time as Quality Input**:
- An application that received above-median examiner time per claim is more reliably examined
- An application that received below-median time should be discounted (likely under-examined)
- Time per claim is the appropriate unit (since claim complexity drives examination effort)

**Substantive Examination as Quality Marker**:
- Multiple substantive office actions = sustained examiner attention
- Examiner-driven prior-art search beyond applicant IDS = quality work
- Substantive 112 issues addressed = thorough review

## Reform Proposals (Quality-Relevant)

**1. Increase Examiner Time Across the Board**: Quality grants require more time than current allocation provides.

**2. Decouple Time from GS Promotion**: Senior examiners receive less time despite handling more complex cases; reform should equalize time across grades.

**3. Substantive Examination Audits**: Random audits of issued patents should drive feedback to art units about systemic quality issues.

**4. Examiner Performance Metrics Tied to Quality**: Current metrics reward production over quality; quality-adjusted metrics should be introduced.

**5. Higher User Fees Funding Better Examination**: Fee increases for examination, not maintenance, would directly improve quality.

## Operative Quality Markers Derived

**Strong Examination Markers**:
- Multiple office actions before allowance
- Examiner cites prior art beyond applicant's IDS
- Substantive 112 rejections addressed
- Restriction requirement applied where appropriate
- Examiner amendments narrowing claims

**Weak Examination Markers (Time-Constrained)**:
- First-action allowance with no rejections
- All cited art is from applicant IDS
- Single short office action followed by allowance
- No substantive 112 engagement
- Senior examiner with high allowance rate

## Counter-Lemley Argument

Frakes & Wasserman directly challenge several Lemley (2001) premises:

**Lemley**: Most patents never matter; ex post litigation handles the few that do.
**F&W**: Litigation is expensive; preventing the bad grant ex ante is cheaper than challenging it ex post.

**Lemley**: Examination should be cursory because cost > benefit.
**F&W**: Their empirical work shows examination cost < litigation cost at the margin.

**Lemley**: Strengthen post-grant review.
**F&W**: Strengthen ex ante examination AND post-grant review; they are complements, not substitutes.

## Synthesis: Quality-Focused Examination Reform

The paper supports a strong inferential framework: any individual patent's quality is materially affected by the time and care invested in its examination. Time is a measurable resource; its expenditure is a quality marker.

For evaluating any individual patent grant:

1. How much examiner time was allocated (computed from GS-grade time-budget tables)?
2. How does the actual time used compare to the allocated time?
3. How many substantive rejections were issued?
4. What fraction of cited art originated with the examiner?
5. Were 112 issues substantively addressed?

A patent grant from an under-resourced examination is, in expectation, a lower-quality patent regardless of nominal validity. Frakes & Wasserman's contribution is to show this effect is large enough to be socially actionable.

## Operative Test

For any issued patent:

- Was examination conducted under standard time allocation or compressed time?
- Did the examiner make substantive use of the allocated time (multiple actions, broad search)?
- Are there indicators of quality-degrading shortcuts (first-action allowance, applicant-only art)?
- Does the prosecution history reflect the time investment a quality examination requires?

A patent passing all four tests is reliably examined; one failing them is, with high probability, in the lower-quality tail of issued patents.
