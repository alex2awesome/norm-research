# Policy Levers in Patent Law: Doctrinal Quality Controls

Burk & Lemley identify the doctrinal "policy levers" that judges and examiners use to tune patent law to industry-specific needs. Each lever functions as a quality-relevant criterion for evaluating claims and prosecution.

## The Core Doctrinal Levers

### 1. Person Having Ordinary Skill in the Art (PHOSITA)

The hypothetical PHOSITA's skill level affects nearly every patentability standard.

**Quality Implications**:
- Low-PHOSITA fields (some software, business methods): nearly any combination is patentable; quality requires examiner caution about over-grant
- High-PHOSITA fields (biotechnology, pharmaceuticals): obviousness bar is high; quality requires careful prior art analysis
- A patent's quality depends on whether the applicant accurately characterized the relevant skill level
- Mischaracterized PHOSITA (e.g., portraying biotechnology as low-skill) is a quality defect

### 2. Enablement (Scope of Disclosure)

How much must the specification teach to support the claims?

**Industry-Specific Standards**:
- **Pharmaceuticals**: Single working example often supports claims to closely related compounds
- **Biotechnology**: Following Amgen v. Sanofi, structural genus claims require full-scope enablement
- **Software**: Functional claiming widely accepted; structural detail rarely required
- **Mechanical**: Drawings and one embodiment usually sufficient

**Quality Marker**: Are the disclosed embodiments representative of the claimed scope under the relevant industry standard?

### 3. Written Description

Does the specification show the inventor "possessed" the claimed invention at filing?

**Quality Marker**: Continuation claims drafted years after filing should be scrutinized for whether the original specification supports the new claim language. Late-added genus claims or claims targeting competitor products are red flags.

### 4. Doctrine of Equivalents and Reverse DOE

Court-applied scope-modulating tools.

**Quality Implications**:
- Pioneer patents claim broad equivalents reach; quality requires the pioneer status to be genuine
- Improvement patents are vulnerable to reverse DOE; quality requires the patent itself to be a substantial advance over prior art

### 5. Subject-Matter Eligibility (35 U.S.C. 101)

The Mayo/Alice framework dramatically affects software, biotech, and diagnostic patents.

**Quality Markers**:
- Software claims that recite generic computer implementation of an abstract idea are low quality (Alice-vulnerable)
- Diagnostic claims that recite a natural correlation plus conventional steps are low quality (Mayo-vulnerable)
- High-quality eligibility-sensitive claims include specific technical implementations or non-conventional combinations

### 6. Inequitable Conduct

Failure to disclose material prior art known to applicant invalidates the patent.

**Quality Markers**:
- Robust IDS submissions with citations from international searches
- Co-pending applications cross-disclosed
- No pattern of selective non-disclosure of inventor's own prior publications

## Industry-Specific Calibration Examples

### Software
- Lower written description bar; functional claiming common
- Quality requires technical specificity to survive Alice
- Quality marker: claims tied to particular technical improvement, not abstract result

### Biotechnology
- High enablement and written description bar
- Quality marker: representative species supporting genus claims; structural-functional correlation disclosed

### Pharmaceuticals
- Compound claims with strong structural support are high quality
- Method-of-treatment claims face evolving eligibility issues
- Quality marker: pharmacological data supporting claimed utility

### Semiconductors
- Cumulative-technology concerns dominate
- Quality requires defined scope that permits design-around
- Quality marker: claims tied to specific process or structure, not generic "improved performance"

## Operative Test for Lever-Calibration Quality

For any patent, evaluate:

1. Does the application correctly identify the technological regime (industry)?
2. Is the PHOSITA skill level appropriately calibrated?
3. Does the enablement disclosure meet the industry-specific bar?
4. Is the claim breadth proportionate to the industry-specific written-description standard?
5. Does the application anticipate eligibility challenges relevant to its field?
6. Does the prosecution history reflect awareness of industry-specific case law?

Patents that mismatch their lever-calibration to their industry are systematically weaker, regardless of nominal grant.
