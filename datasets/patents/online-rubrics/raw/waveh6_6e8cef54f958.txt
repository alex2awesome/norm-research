STATEMENT OF THE CASE AND FACTS 


On or about March 8, 1988, the Internal Revenue Service 
(IRS) seized real property allegedly belonging to Petitioner in an 
attempt to satisfy an October 14, 1985 assessment alleged to 
have been made for 1983. The assessment arose from a 
deficiency that had allegedly been determined against the 
Petitioner. The Notice of Deficiency was sent to the Petitioner 
by certified Mail on May 24, 1985. The Commissioner of 
Internal Revenue, or one of his delegates, mailed the Notice of 
Deficiency to the Petitioner at P.O. Box 14955, Phoenix, 
Arizona 85063 (P.O. Box Address). (App C, Pg. A-10) 
Petitioner’s "last known address" for purposes of IRC, § 6212 
(b)(1) was 4544 West Continental Drive, Glendale, Arizona 
85308 (Continental Address). (App. A, Pg. A-3) 

A Notice of Lien was recorded on or about December 16, 
1986 in the Office of the County Recorder, in Maricopa County, 
Arizona. (App. C, Pg. A-10). The Petitioner did not discover 
the Notice until April 18, 1988. On or about March 21, 1988, 
the Respondent caused several Notices of Sale to be sent the 
prospective bidders. (App. C, Pg. A-10). The Notices of Sale 
were also published in various newspapers on April 10, 11 and 
12, 1988. (App. C, Pg. A-10). The information disclosed in the 
Notice of Lien and the Notices of Sale was return information 
(App. A, Pg. A-2; App. C., Pg. A-10) 

The Petitioner commenced this action on April 10, 1990 in 
the United States District Court in the District of Arizona arising 
from the disclosure of return information in the Notice of Lien 
and the Notices of Sale that had been sent to prospective bidders 
and which were published in various newspapers throughout the

IRC, § 7431(a)(1) provides a statutory action for damages 
when an employee of the United States makes an unauthorized 
disclosure of return and return information. In determining 
liability, the appellate Courts have adopted two very distinct 
rules of construction in this section, with some minor variations 
within these two rules. The Petitioner will refer to these two 
tules as the "confidentiality test" and the "validity test." 

The "confidentiality test" generally bars damages under IRC, 
§ 7431 for a disclosure: of return and return information, even 
when the disclosure is invalid at the time it is made, if there has 
been a previously valid disclosure of the same information. 
These courts have concluded that "Once tax return information 
is made a part of the public domain, the information is no 
longer confidential." See William E. Schrambling Accountancy 
Corp. v. United States, 937 F.2d 1485 (9th Cir., 1991); Lampert 
v. United States, 854 F.2d 335 (9th Cir., 1988) cert. denied, 490 
US 1034, 109 S.Ct 1931, 104 L.Ed.2d 403 (1989); United States 
v. Posner, 594 F. Supp. 930, 936 aff'd on other grounds 764 
F.2d 1535 (11th Cir., 1985); Thomas v. United States, 671 
F.Supp 15, aff'd 890 F.2d 18 (7th Cir., 1989)*;

IRC, § 7431(b) provides that "[no] liability shall arise under 
this section with respect to any disclosure which results from a 
good faith, but erroneous, interpretation of section 6103." This 
section does not provide a general "good faith" defense, but a 
defense based upon an erroneous interpretation of IRC, § 6103. 

A plaintiff need not plead that the disclosures were made in 
"bad faith." In order to state a claim under IRC, § 7431(a), the 
“complaint need allege only the elements of the claim for 
relief." Hrubec v. National Railroad Passenger Corporation, 
981 F.2d 962, 964 (7th Cir., 1992), citing, Gomez v. T. oledo, 446 
US 635, 64 L.Ed.2d 572, 100 S.Ct 1920 (1980). 

The good faith defense under IRC, § 7431(b) should be 
judged by an objective standard analogous to that in Harlow v. 
Fitzgerald, 457 US 800, 73 L.Ed.2d 396, 102 S.Ct 2727 (1982). 
Diamond v. U.S., 944 F.2d 431, 435-36 (8th Cir., 1991); 
Huckaby v. United States Dept. of Treasury, IRS, 794 F.2d 
1041, 1048 (Sth Cir., 1986); Rorex v Traynor, supra, at 387. 

In light of this objective standard, did the disclosures made in 
recording the lien and sending and publishing the Notices of 
Sale violate a clearly established Statutory right of which a 
reasonable person would have known? In Anderson y. 
Creighton, 483 US 635, 640, 97 L.Ed.2d 523, 107 S.Ct. 3034 
(1987) this Court gave guidance as to when a right is "clearly 
established." 


The contours of the right must be sufficiently clear 
that a reasonable official would understand that what he 
is doing violates that right. That is not to say that an 


15 


official action is protected by qualified immunity unless 
the very action has previously been held unlawful (cites 
omitted), but it is to say that in the light of preexisting 
law the unlawfulness must be apparent. (cites omitted) 


As the Petitioner discussed in Argument I, ante, no less than 
four (4) circuits have found that disclosure of return and return 
information is not authorized, when a mandatory statutory or 
internal administrative procedure that is a prerequisite to the 
disclosure is not followed. Not even the Ninth Circuit was in 
conflict with this principle until its decision in this case.” The 
Petitioner proved, based upon the facts and the prevailing state 
of the law that a "clearly established" statutory right was 
violated. That is assuming of course that the procedural validity 
of the underlying assessment can somehow result in a "good 
faith, but erroneous, interpretation of section 6103."

Jimmy C. Chisum appeals pro se the district court’s 
summary judgment for the United States in Chisum’s action 
alleging that the Internal Revenue Service ("IRS") wrongfully 
disclosed tax information in violation of 26 U.S.C. §7431(a)(1). 
We have jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. § 1291, and we affirm. 

We review de novo the district court’s grant of summary 
judgment. Kruso v. International Tel. &amp; Tel. Corp., 872 F.2d 
1416, 1421 (9th Cir. 1989), cert. denied, 496 U.S. 937 (1990). 
Viewing the evidence in the light most favorable to the non- 
moving party, we must determine whether there are any genuine 
issues of material fact and whether the district court correctly 
applied the relevant law. Alaska Airlines, Inc. v. United 
Airlines, Inc., 948 F.2d 536, 539 (9th Cir. 1991), cert. denied, 
112 S. Ct. 1603 (1992). 

Section 6103 of the Internal Revenue Code provides that 
income tax returns and return information are confidential. 26 
U.S.C. § 6103; Maisano v. United States, 908 F.2d 408, 410 
(9th Cir. 1990). Section 7431 provides a civil cause of action 
for damages for the intentional or negligent disclosure of 
confidential return information by officers or employees of the 
United States. 26 U.S.C. § 7431; Maisano, 908 F.2d at 410. 
Section 6103(k), however, authorizes the IRS to disclose return 
information if necessary for the collection of tax liability. 26 
U.S.C. § 6103(k)(6), Under section 301.6103(k)(6)-1(b) of the 
Treasury Regulations, the IRS may disclose return information 
"to apply the provisions of the Code relating to the establish- 
ment of liens against [the taxpayer’s] assets, or [a] levy on... 
the assets to satisfy any [outstanding tax] liability." Treas. Reg. 
§ 301.6103(k)(6)-1(b)(6); Maisano, 908 F.2d at 410. Addition- 
ally, under section 7431(b), there is no liability for “any 
disclosure which results from a good faith, but erroneous, 
interpretation of section 6103." 26 U.S.C. § 7431(b). 

Here, the IRS disclosed Chisum’s tax return information by 
recording a tax lien against Chisum and issuing a notice of sale 
of Chisum’s property, both to satisfy his tax obligation. Chisum 
argues that these disclosures nevertheless were unauthorized

under section 7431 because the underlying tax assessment was

The government concedes error in the mailing of the notice 
of deficiency and states that therefore the assessment and tax

The government argues, however, that although the assessment

a purpose clearly authorized by section 6103(k)(6). See 26

is no evidence in the record to indicate that the disclosures, 
although erroneous, were not made in good faith. See 26 U.S.C. 
7431(b). Accordingly, the district court did not err by granting 
summary judgment for the United States.

I, DEFENDANT’S MOTION FOR SUMMARY JUDGMENT 


A. Is summary judgment proper against the plaintiff as 
his claims are barred by the statute of limitations? 


Defendant argues that plaintiff's claims are barred by the 
statute of limitations. Plaintiff brings this action pursuant to 26 
U.S.C. §7431. Section 7431 provides that any action brought 
pursuant to 26 U.S.C. §7431 must be brought "within 2 years 
after the date of discovery by the plaintiff of the unauthorized 
disclosure." 26 U.S.C. §7431(d). 

The first alleged disclosure was the Notice of Federal Tax 
Lien filed in the Maricopa County Recorder’s Office on or 
about December 19, 1986. The undisputed facts establish that 
the plaintiff received the IRS Notice of intention to levy on 
December 24, 1986. DSOF 9. Plaintiff received notice of the 
second and third alleged disclosures when he received the 
Notice of Sealed Bid Sale on March 21, 1988. PSOF 18. This 
action was not filed until April 10, 1990. Because this suit was 
filed more than two years after plaintiff received notice of all 
three of the alleged unauthorized disclosures, his claims are 
barred by the statute of limitations and summary judgment is 
proper for the defendant.

(a) In General. --

Section 6103(k)(6) is an exception to the confidentiality

disclosures or return information to the extent necessary to 
locate assets and satisfy tax liabilities through liens, levies, 
seizures and sales. Section 6103(k)(6) specifically provides: 


9k) Disclosure of Certain Returns and Return

II. PLAINTIFF’S MOTION FOR PARTIAL SUMMARY JUDGMENT 


Plaintiff alleges in his motion that he is entitled to partial 
summary judgment that the assessments against him were 
invalid because of procedural defects. defendant correctly 
responds that plaintiff may not obtain a judicial determination 
of the validity of the assessment in the context of this §7431 
action. Elias v. Connett, 908 F.2d 521 (9th Cir. 1990)(plaintiff 
cannot challenge the validity of an assessment in a §2410(a) 
action); Elias v. United States, 67 A.F.T.R. 2d 91-328, 91-438 
(C.D. Cal. 1990)(the propriety of the underlying assessment 
cannot be collaterally challenged in an unlawful disclosure 
action filed pursuant to §7431). 

Moreover, no liability arises under the statute for a good 
faith interpretation of Section 6103. 26 U.S.C. §7431(b). 
Pursuant to §7431(b), bad faith is an element that plaintiff must 
prove in his cause of action and he must plead facts sufficient 
to establish bad faith. Davidson v. Brady, 732 F.2d 552, 553 
(6th Cir. 1984). Plaintiff has not show any facts to establish 
bad faith or that the disclosures were not made in good faith or 
that a genuine issue of material fact exists regarding the 
good\bad faith of the government. Nor has plaintiff shown that 
IRS officials were aware of any potential procedural defects in 
the assessment. Thus, even if procedural defects existed in the 
assessment, plaintiff would not prevail in this action.

William C. Blankstyn appeals pro se the district court’s 
dismissal of his action seeking injunctive relief and damages in 
connection with the Internal Revenue Service’s (IRS) levy on 
his wages to collect taxes assessed against Blankstyn for tax 
year 1985. Blankstyn also appeals the district court’s denial of 
his Fed. R. Civ. P. 59(e) motion. We have jurisdiction pursuant 
to 28 U.S.C. §1291. We affirm in part, vacate in part and 
remand. 


I. BACKGROUND 


Blankstyn filed a complaint in district court seeking to 
enjoin the IRS from levying on his wages and seeking damages 
for wrongful disclosure of return information. Blankstyn alleged 
that he never received a notice of deficiency for tax year 1985, 
and that the assessment was not properly made and recorded. 
Blankstyn alleged that the IRS’s collection of taxes assessed for 
1985 was therefore unlawful. Blankstyn sought a temporary 
restraining order, preliminary injunction, and permanent 
injunction to prevent IRS from levying on his wages to collect 
the 1985 tax assessment. Blankstyn also alleged that because 
the assessment was procedurally improper, the IRS’s disclosure 
of return information in connection with the wage levy violated 
26 U.S.C. §7431. Blankstyn sought damages for wrongful 
disclosure of return information and an injunction to prevent 
further wrongful disclosures. 

On April 19, 1991, the district court held a hearing on #’s 
request for a preliminary injunction. At the conclusion of the 
hearing, the district court issued a preliminary injunction 
effective for 10 days and ordered supplemental briefing. On 
April 30, 1991, the district court issued an order (1) consolidat- 
ing the motion for preliminary injunction with the trial on the 
merits, (2) treating the motion for preliminary injunction as a 
motion for permanent injunction, (3) denying the injunction, (4)

: 


granting judgment for the United States, and (5) dismissing the 
action. 


II. Injunction 


The district court found that Blankstyn claim for injunctive 
relief was barred by the Anti-Injunction Act ("Act"), 26 U.S.C. 
§7421(a). The Act prohibits a taxpayer from bringing a "suit 
for the purpose of restraining the assessment or collection of any 
tax ...." Id. The Act is strictly enforced. See Maxfield v. 
United States Postal Serv., 752 F.2d 433, 434 (9th Cir. 1984); 
see also Bob Jones Univ. v. Simon, 416 U.S. 725, 736-37 
(1974). Thus, ordinarily taxpayers are limited in district court 
"to suits for refund." United States v. Condo, 782 F.,2d 1502, 
1506 (9th Cir. 1986). 

There are several statutory exceptions and one judicial 
exception to the Act. See 26 U.S.C. §§6212(a), (c), 6213(a), 
6672(b), 6694(c), 7426(a),(b)(1), 7429(b); Enochs v. Williams 
Packing &amp; Navigation Co., 370 U.S. 1, 7 (1962). Under the 
judicial exception announced in Enochs, injunctive relief is 
available if the taxpayer can demonstrate that (1) under no 
circumstances could the government prevail, and (2) the 
taxpayer will be irreparably harmed in the injunction is not 
granted. Id.; see also Condo, 782 F.2d at 1506. 

Blankstyn contends that he never received a notice of 
deficiency for tax year 1985 prior to the assessment and levy. 
and, therefore, his claim for injunctive relief was permitted by 
section 6213. This contention lacks merit. 

Section 6213 provides that no assessment of a tax shall be 
made and no proceeding for collection shall begin until 90 days 
after the mailing of a notice of deficiency pursuant to section 
6212. Section 6213 further provides: "[nJotwithstanding the 
provisions of section 7421(a), the making of such assessment or 
the beginning of such proceeding or levy during the time such 
prohibition is in force may be enjoined by a proceeding in the 
proper court." 


A notice of deficiency is valid if it is mailed to the taxpaye- 
r’s last known address, even if it is never received by the 
taxpayer. Williams v. Commissioner, 935 F.2d 1066, 1067 (9th 


Cir. 1991); King v. Commissioner, 857 F.2d 676, 679 (9th Cir. 
1988); Cool Fuel, Inc. v. Connett, 685 F.2d 309, 312 (9th Cir. 
1982). A postal form certifying that a notice of deficiency has 
been mailed is sufficient, in the absence of contrary evidence, 
to establish that the notice was properly mailed. United States 
v. Zolla, 724 F.2d 808, 810 (9th Cir.) cert. denied, 469 U.S. 830 
(1984). 

Here, the IRS alleged that it properly mailed Blankstyn a 
notice of deficiency for tax year 1985. To support this claim, 
the IRS submitted (1) a copy of Blankstyn’s notice of deficiency 
for tax year 1985, dated March 26, 1990 and properly addressed 
to Blankstyn at his last known address, (2) the certified mailing 
list of notices of deficiency sent by the Ogden Service Center on 
March 26, 1990 (Form 3624), which listed the certified mail 
number for Blankstyn’s notice, and (3) a copy of the envelope 
in which the notice was mailed, returned marked "unclaimed" by 
the post office. Given this evidence, the district court correctly 
found that the IRS had established that it had properly mailed 
the notice of deficiency to Blankstyn. See Williams, 935 F.2d 
at 1067; Zolla, 724 F.2d at 810. Further, the district court 
correctly found that Blankstyn’s claim for injunctive relief under 
section 6213 was precluded because the notice was properly 
mailed, regardless of whether Blankstyn ever received it. See 
Cool Fuel, 685 F.2d at 312. 

Blankstyn also contends that he is entitled to injunctive 
relief under the judicial exception to the Act. This contention 
lacks merit. Blankstyn failed to establish that under no circum- 
stances could the government prevail. See Enochs, 370 U.S. at 
7. Further, Blankstyn has an adequate remedy at law because 
he can pay the tax and file an action for refund. See 26 U.S.C. 
§7422. Finally, the district court correctly found that Blankstyn 
would not suffer irreparable harm without an injunction. See 
Enochs, 370 U.S. at 7; Elias v. Connett, 908 F.2d 521, 526 (9th 


A -24 


Cir. 1990) (financial hardship does not constitute irreparable 
harm). 


II]. Wrongful Disclosure 


Blankstyn contends the district court erred by consolidating 
the trial on the merits of his wrongful disclosure claim with the 
preliminary injunction hearing and dismissing his wrongful 
disclosure claim. This contention has merit. 

Section 6103 provides that income tax returns and return 
information are confidential. 26 U.S.C. §6103; Maisano v. 
United States, 908 F.2d 408, 410 (9th Cir. 1990). Section 7431 
provides a taxpayer a civil cause of action for damages for the 
intentional or negligent disclosure of confidential return 
information by officers or employees of the United States. 26 
U.S.C. §7431(a); Maisano, 908 F.2d at 410. Section 6103, 
however, authorizes the IRS to disclose return information if 
necessary for the collection of a tax liability. 26 U.S.C. 
§6103(k)(6); see Maisano, 908 F.2d at 410. 

Here, Blankstyn alleged that because he did not receive a 
notice of deficiency and because the assessment was not 
properly recorded, the underlying assessment and lien were 
invalid and, therefore, the release of return information was 
unlawful. The district court correctly concluded that the IRS 
had established that the notice of deficiency was properly mailed 
and, therefore, that this allegation could not support a wrongful 
disclosure claim. Nevertheless, the district court failed to 
address Blankstyn’s argument that no assessment was ever 
made. See 26 U.S.C. § 6203. The only evidence in the record 
regarding the assessment was a copy of the Summary Record of 
Assessment, which did not identify Blankstyn or the assessment 
made against him. Thus, the district court erred by dismissing 
Blankstyn’s wrongful disclosure claim after the preliminary 


injunction hearing. ' See Hughes v. United States, 953 F.2d 
531, 542 (9th Cir. 1992); Maisano, 908 F.2d at 410. According- 


ly, we remand to the district court for further consideration of 


this claim. ”

The petition in this case was not timely filed and the 
Court therefore lacks jurisdiction. 

1. The first opinion of the court of appeals (Pet. App. 
A5-A7) was filed on September 13, 1993. That opinion 
affirmed the judgment of the district court dismissing 
petitioner’s claim, on the ground that petitioner’s suit 
was barred by the statute of limitations (id. at A6-A7). 
Petitioner thereafter filed a petition for rehearing. On 
January 24, 1994, the court of appeals denied the petition 
but withdrew the September 13, 1993, opinion and entered 
a new opinion, affirming the district court’s decision on 
the merits rather than on the statute of limitations (id. 
at Al-A3, A17; see id. at A3 n.1). The opinion on re- 
hearing did not vacate the earlier judgment of the court

Petitioner filed a second petition for rehearing with 
suggestion for rehearing en banc on February 7, 1994. 
Petitioner contended in that petition that the court’s 
January 24, 1994, decision on the merits was in error. 
That petition was denied by the court of appeals on May 
11, 1994 (Pet. App. A19). The petition for a writ of 
certiorari was filed on August 1, 1994, invoking this 
Court’s jurisdiction under 28 U.S.C. 1254(1). The peti- 
tion for a writ of certiorari was filed more than 90 days 
after the denial of the first petition for rehearing and 
less than 90 days after the denial of the second petition 
for rehearing.

In the present case, although the court of appeals 
revised its opinion in response to the first motion for 
rehearing, it did not alter its initial judgment in any 
fashion (Pet. App. A3, A7).' This Court has often noted 
that its “power is to correct wrong judgments, not to 
revise opinions” (Herb v. Pitcairn, 324 U.S. 117, 126 
(1945)). See also Black v. Cutter Laboratories, 351 USS. 
292, 297 (1956). Accordingly, the “mere fact that a 
judgment previously entered is reentered or [is] revised 
in an immaterial way does not toll the time within which 
review must be sought.” FCC v. League of Women 
Voters, 468 U.S. 364, 373 n.10 (1984), quoting FTC v. 
Minneapolis-Honeywell Regulator Co., 344 U.S. 206, 
211 (1952). 

Under Rule 40(a) of the Federal Rules of Appellate 
Procedure, “[a] petition for rehearing may be filed within 
14 days after entry of judgment unless the time is 
shortened or enlarged by order or by local rule.” The 
second rehearing petition in this case was not filed in 
compliance with this Rule; there is accordingly no basis 
for contending that the second rehearing petition 
further extended the time for filing a petition for a writ 
of certiorari. Because petitioner failed to file the 
petition for a writ of certiorari within 90 days of the 
denial of the first rehearing petition, the petition for a 
writ of certiorari is untimely and the Court therefore 
lacks jurisdiction.’

ARGUMENT 


THE PETITION FOR THE 
WRIT OF CERTIORARI WAS TIMELY 


The Respondent argues that the Petition for Writ of Certiorari 
was not timely, because the Ninth Circuit’s January 24, 1994 
memorandum disposition merely "revised" it’s September 13, 
1993 memorandum dispostion. In reaching this conclusion, the 
Respondent essentially interchanged the term "revised" with the 
term "withdrawn." The Ninth Circuit’s January 24, 1994 Order 
states, in part (See Appendix to Petititon, Page A-17): 


"The memorandum disposition filed September 13, 
1993 is withdrawn." (emphasis added) 


The order clearly shows that the September 13, 1993 
judgment was withdrawn and the new judgment on the merits 
was entered on January 24, 1994. While the decisions in both 
the September 13, 1993 and the January 24, 1994 affirmed the 
district Court’s grant of summary judgment, each judgment was 
separate and distinct from the other. 


This is clear from examination of both memoranda. The 
Court in the September 13, 1993 memorandum concluded that 
the action was time barred. Thus, the decision did not reach the 
merits of the case as noted by footnote one (1) at the end of the 
memorandum, which states to wit: 


_ "Because we hold that summary judgment for the 
United States was proper on the ground that Chisum’s 
action was time-barred, we do not address the district 
court’s alternative findings on the merits of Chisum’s 
claims." (App., Pg. A-7) 


In its January 24, 1994 memorandum, the Court decided the 
case on its merits and did not reach the case on the basis that 
the action was time-barred as noted by footnote one (1) at the 
end of the memorandum, which states to wit: 


"Because we hold that summary judgment for the 
United States was proper on the merits of Chisum’s 
claims, we do not address the district court’s alternative 
finding that Chisum’s action was time-barred. (App., 
Pg. A-3) 


The footnotes in each case clearly show that the judgments 
are distinct. Taking the totality of the entire record in the Ninth 
Circuit, it is clear that the September 13, 1993 judgment was 
superceded by the January 24, 1994. It was not merely revised 
but substantially altered the rights of the parties. 


The Respondent’s reliance on FCC v. League of Women 
Voters, 468 US 364 (1984) and FTC v. Minneaplis-Honevwell 
Regulator Co., 344 US 206 (1952) is misplaced. Both are 
distinguishable from this case and tend support the Petitioner. 


First, FCC, involved the timeliness of an appeal where the 
appeal was taken from an amendment to the judgment involving 
an award of attorney’s fees. This Court merely recognised that 
an awared of attorneys fees ’"is uniquely separable from the 
cause of action" that is settled by a court’s judgment on the 
merits, and therfore a postjudgment request for attorney’s fees’ 
and thus would not suspend the time for bringing an appeal. 


(quotes in original). Id. at 287-88. n.10. 


This Court in FTC, supra, held the FTC’s petition for Writ of 
Certiorari was untimely, because FTC filed a memorandum with 
the appellate court for affirmance and enforcement of the 
uncontested parts I and II of the FTC’s cease and desist order 
after the time for petitioning for rehearing from the original 
judgment had expired, where the "Final Decree" entered by the 
Appellate Court merely repeated the previous judgment. This 
Court declined to accept the FTC’s memoranudm as a petition 
for rehearing or a motion to amend a previous judgment. 


The January 24, 1994 judgment by the Ninth Circuit 
substantially changed its previous judgment of September 13, 
1993. Had the Respondent continued from its quote of FTC, 
supra, at 211, it would have supported the conclusion that the 
Petition in the instant case was timely. This Court in FTC, 
supra went on to state that: 


"Only when the lower court changes matters of 
substance, or resolves a genuin ambiguity, in a judgment 
previously rendered should the period within which an 
appeal must be taken or a petition for certiorari filed 
begin to run anew. The test is a practical one. The 
question is whether the lower court, in its second order, 
has disturbed or revised legal rights and obligations 
which, by its prior judgment, had been plainly and 
properly settled. (emphasis added) 

Id. 211-12 


A Court of Appeals can supercede its judgment. Id. at 211. 
In determining whether the Petition in this case was timely this 
Court should consider the following. A Court of Appeals enters 
judgment affirming a district Court. A timely petition for 
hearing is filed and denied and then a Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari is filed and denied. Shortly thereafter, the Court of 
Appeals sua sponte supercedes it judgment with a new judgment 
but still affirms the judgment of the district Court, on a legal 
theory in conflict with this Court. Under the Respondent’s 
theory, the party affected by the superceding judgment could not 
petition for rehearing and could not petition this Court for 
review because the time for petitioning had expired with the 
original judgment.’ 


1/ The Petitioner is not aware of a time limit imposed upon a Court of 
Appeals to sua sponte supercede its previous judgment. If such a time limit 
exists then assume under the circumstances discussed, that the superceding 
judgment was entered within the imposed time limit. 


4 


The Ninth Circuit "disturbed or revised [the] legal rights” of 
the parties in its January 24, 1994 judgment. FTC, supra, at 
212. To say otherwise ignores the obvious. Accordingly, the 
Petitioner was entitled to file his second Petition for Rehearing. 
It was filed within the time provided by Rule 40(a), Federal 
Rules of Appellate Procedure, for the January 24, 1994, 
judgment. Thus, the time for bringing the Petition for Writ of 
Certiorari was suspended until the Court of Appeals disposed of 
the Petition for Rehearing, because there was no "judgment" to 
be reviewed. Missouri v. Jenkins, 495 US 33, 46 (1990)
