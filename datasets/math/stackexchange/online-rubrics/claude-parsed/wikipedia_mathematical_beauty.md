# Mathematical Beauty — Wikipedia

The collated mainstream synthesis of what mathematicians mean by "beauty" in math results and proofs.

## Hardy's Three Qualities (1940)
G.H. Hardy: a beautiful result possesses three "purely aesthetic qualities":
1. **Inevitability** — necessity, logical compulsion.
2. **Unexpectedness** — surprise; non-obvious connections.
3. **Economy** — efficiency, conciseness.

Hardy explicitly rejected "enumeration of cases" as one of the duller forms of mathematical argument.

## General Characteristics
- **Simplicity and brevity.** Short, simple proofs are preferred over laborious or case-heavy ones.
- **Elegance.** Often associated with form of exposition; some distinguish elegance from beauty.
- **Connected values.** Beauty is associated with abstractness, purity, simplicity, depth, order.

## 1990 Survey of Mathematicians
Top three theorems by aesthetic rating:
1. Euler's identity ($e^{i\pi}+1=0$).
2. Euler's polyhedron formula ($V-E+F=2$).
3. Euclid's theorem on infinite primes.

## Critiques and Counter-Criteria
- **Rota (1997).** Challenged unexpectedness as sufficient — surprising results are not always beautiful.
- **Monastyrsky (2001).** Explicit, constructive realisations enhance beauty beyond mere existence.

## Properties of Beautiful Proofs (collected)
- Visual clarity and intuitive appeal.
- Multiple independent proofs indicate quality.
- Avoidance of computational heaviness or computer verification (a contested criterion).
- Reveal connections between seemingly unrelated areas.
- Reveal the *essence* of why a theorem is true, not just *that* it is true.

## Implication
Wikipedia consolidates the working aesthetic vocabulary into roughly: inevitability, unexpectedness, economy, depth, connection, essence, simplicity. This list maps onto most modern rubrics.
