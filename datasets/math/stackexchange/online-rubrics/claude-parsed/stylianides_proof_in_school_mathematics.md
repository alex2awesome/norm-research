# Stylianides: Proof and Proving in School Mathematics

An influential framework that defines what counts as proof at the elementary/school level along **four major features**, with explicit criteria for each. The framework received an AERA publication award.

## The Four Features of an Argument-as-Proof

A mathematical argument counts as a proof to the extent that it satisfies four requirements:

### 1. Foundation (Set of Accepted Statements)

The proof rests on statements (axioms, definitions, prior theorems) that are:

- **Known to the audience** (within the conceptual reach of the class)
- **Accepted by the audience** as true (no smuggled premises)
- **Made explicit** at points where they are used

### 2. Modes of Argumentation (Valid Reasoning)

The argument uses **valid modes of reasoning**:

- Logical deduction (modus ponens, contrapositive)
- Mathematical induction
- Case analysis (covering all cases)
- Indirect proof (when used correctly)
- **Avoidance** of empirical generalization from examples (which is *not* a valid mode for proof)

### 3. Modes of Argument Representation (Form)

The argument is presented using **representations the audience can follow**:

- Verbal explanation
- Symbolic algebra
- Diagrams (when their generality is justified)
- Tables, graphs (when used to indicate, not prove)
- Multi-modal combinations

### 4. Social Dimension (Community Acceptance)

A proof's status depends on whether the **discourse community** accepts:

- The starting points
- The rules of inference
- The representational choices
- The conclusion as following

## The Definition

> A mathematical argument counts as proof in a community when its foundation, modes of argumentation, and modes of representation are all *within the conceptual reach* of that community and are *accepted* by it.

## Two Functions of Proof Stylianides Emphasizes

Proofs simultaneously serve to:

1. **Promote conviction** — provide grounds to believe the conclusion.
2. **Promote understanding** — explain *why* the conclusion holds.

A "proof" that does only (1) (e.g., black-box appeal to authority) or only (2) (e.g., a heuristic explanation that doesn't establish truth) is incomplete.

## Implied Rubric for Proof Quality

For each proof, ask:

- Are the **starting statements** accessible and accepted?
- Is the **mode of reasoning** logically valid (not empirical generalization)?
- Is the **representation** appropriate for the audience?
- Does the **community** (or would a competent referee) accept it?
- Does it provide **conviction** that the result is true?
- Does it provide **understanding** of why it is true?

## Anti-Pattern: Empirical Verification Mistaken for Proof

A particularly important Stylianides finding: students (and even teachers) frequently treat **checking many cases** as proof. This violates the modes-of-argumentation requirement. A rubric for proof must explicitly mark this anti-pattern as a failure mode, even when the conclusion is correct.

## Adapting the Framework to Higher Mathematics

The same four features apply to research mathematics, with the audience shifted upward:

- **Foundation**: prior published results, accepted axioms (ZFC, choice, etc.)
- **Argumentation**: still must be deductive; computer-assisted proofs raise new questions
- **Representation**: research-level notation, diagrams, computer code as supplementary
- **Community**: the relevant subfield's referees and readers
