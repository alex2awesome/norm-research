# Greiffenhagen: Judging Importance Before Checking Correctness

A complementary study (same data corpus as "Checking Correctness") on how mathematicians judge the **significance** of a paper *before* its correctness has been verified. This separates importance from correctness as independent dimensions of mathematical quality.

## The Two-Stage Process at Elite Journals

Top mathematics journals (e.g., *Annals of Mathematics*, *Inventiones*) introduced a two-stage process in the early 2000s:

1. **Stage 1 — Quick Opinion on Importance**: A senior mathematician (often anonymous) gives a brief judgment on whether the paper is important enough to be worth a full referee report.
2. **Stage 2 — Full Refereeing for Correctness**: Only if stage 1 is positive, a (typically younger) referee does line-checking.

This means **importance is judged independently of correctness** — and often by a different person.

## Criteria for "Quick Opinion" Importance Judgments

Senior mathematicians, asked for quick opinions, look at:

1. **The main theorem statement** — does it answer a recognized open problem?
2. **The improvement over prior work** — is the result genuinely new, or a marginal extension?
3. **The technique** — does the paper introduce a new method or use existing ones?
4. **The breadth of impact** — does the result matter only to a narrow subfield, or does it have cross-area significance?
5. **The author's prior work** — track record in the area is treated as evidence.
6. **The paper's exposition quality** — clear writing is taken as a positive signal.
7. **The setup of the problem** — is the problem genuinely interesting, or contrived?
8. **Connection to long-recognized problems** — does it fit into a Hilbert-style program?

## What Constitutes "Important" Mathematics (Editor Consensus)

From the interview data, importance includes:

- **Closing a long-open problem** (highest signal).
- **Introducing a new technique** that others will reuse.
- **Connecting previously separate areas**.
- **Significantly improving a known result** (e.g., better bounds, weaker hypotheses).
- **Producing tools** that solve adjacent problems.
- **Settling a foundational question** (axiomatic, definitional).
- **Disproving a conjecture** (often more valuable than proving expected results).

## Implicit Rubric for Significance

A piece of mathematics is significant to the extent it scores on:

1. **Problem-recognition**: is the problem one the field acknowledges as important?
2. **Result strength**: how much stronger than previous results?
3. **Technique novelty**: does it introduce or substantially refine a method?
4. **Cross-area reach**: how many subfields will the result or technique affect?
5. **Foundational depth**: does it shift conceptual understanding, not just resolve a calculation?
6. **Generative potential**: does it open new problems?
7. **Improvability**: does it leave room for future work, or is it a final answer?

## The Division of Labor Logic

Greiffenhagen explains *why* this two-stage system exists:

- **Quick opinions are cheap**: a senior mathematician can read an abstract and intro and judge importance in 30 minutes.
- **Correctness checking is expensive**: a careful refereeing of a 50-page paper takes weeks.
- **Senior judgment** is best applied to the importance question (where experience matters).
- **Junior judgment** is best applied to the correctness question (where time and recent technical familiarity matter).

This division has implications for rubric design: **importance and correctness should be evaluated by different processes**, not collapsed into a single "quality" score.

## Implied Two-Axis Rubric

For any mathematical work, score independently:

- **Importance** (significance, novelty, impact, generativity, scope of result)
- **Correctness** (rigor, gap-acceptability, plausibility-with-field, internal consistency)

A paper can be **important and incorrect** (worth fixing), **unimportant and correct** (publishable in a low-tier journal but not advancing the field), **important and correct** (the elite category), or **unimportant and incorrect** (rejected).

## How This Changes the Quality Picture

Most rubrics conflate "is this good math" into one dimension. Greiffenhagen's empirical work shows the **community itself uses two dimensions**, evaluated independently by different people:

- A correct but unimportant paper can be technically sound yet rejected from elite journals.
- An important but flawed paper can be sent back for fixes — its importance is what gets it that chance.

A rubric design that scores these axes separately matches actual community practice.

## Anti-Patterns Detected in Importance Judgments

Quick-opinion givers note these red flags:

- Papers that **claim importance** by name-dropping (e.g., "this answers a question of X") without showing the connection.
- Papers that **artificially inflate** the difficulty of the problem.
- Papers that **mismatch technique and claim** (e.g., elementary methods claiming to settle a deep question, or heavy machinery for a result anyone could prove).
- Papers in **manufactured subfields** with no engagement with mainstream questions.
- Papers that **report many small theorems** rather than one substantive result.
