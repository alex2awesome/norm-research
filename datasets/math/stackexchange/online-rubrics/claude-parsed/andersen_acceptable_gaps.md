# Andersen: Acceptable Gaps in Mathematical Proofs

Based on qualitative interviews with seven research mathematicians about their refereeing practices, Andersen extracts the criteria mathematicians actually use to judge whether a gap in a published proof is acceptable.

## The Empirical Setup

Mathematical proofs published in journals **always contain intentional gaps** — steps the author trusts the reader to fill in. The acceptability of such gaps is governed by community norms, not by any formal rule. Andersen's interviews surface those norms.

## Factors That Make a Gap More Acceptable

A gap is more likely to be accepted by a referee if:

1. **The omitted step is routine** for the intended audience (a standard calculation, a textbook lemma, an obvious case).
2. **The author has a reputation** for reliability — their previously published proofs have held up.
3. **The omitted step is "plausible"** — the referee can see that something true and provable lies in the gap, even if they don't trace it.
4. **The conclusion is corroborated** by other lines of argument or by special cases the referee can verify.
5. **Filling the gap is technically straightforward** but tedious (a long calculation or case enumeration).
6. **The gap is signposted** — the author marks where it is and indicates how it would be filled.

## Factors That Make a Gap Unacceptable

A gap becomes unacceptable when:

1. **It hides the main idea** of the proof (the gap *is* the proof).
2. **It is at a step where the conclusion is contested** or where intuition fails.
3. **The omitted step requires a non-routine technique** that the author has not signaled.
4. **The author has a history of errors** or works in an area with low community trust.
5. **The gap is unmarked** — the reader is misled about what is being claimed.
6. **The result depends sensitively** on the gap (small changes break the conclusion).

## What Referees Actually Do

- **Referees do not check every detail.** They sample, focusing on the steps that look most likely to harbor errors.
- **Reputation enters legitimately**: a paper by an established author is given more benefit of the doubt.
- **The author is ultimately responsible** for correctness; the referee's job is to find serious problems, not to certify every line.
- **Referees skip steps they find plausible** — gap-filling is itself a default community behavior, not a deviation.

## Implied Rubric for Proof Writing

A proof is well-written (in the community-acceptable sense) when its gaps are:

- **Routine** for the audience
- **Plausible** at sight
- **Signposted** when non-trivial
- **Not concentrated** at the load-bearing step
- **Recoverable** with bounded effort

A proof is poorly written when its gaps are:

- At the load-bearing step
- Unmarked or disguised as "clear"
- Of a kind that requires the reader to redo the author's work
- Numerous and non-routine for the intended audience

## Theoretical Significance

Andersen's findings show that **mathematical rigor is a social-epistemic practice**, not a formal property of texts. The "acceptable gap" structure means that what counts as a proof depends on the community's distribution of trust and competence — not on syntactic completeness.
