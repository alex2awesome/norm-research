# Engel: Problem-Solving Strategies — The Heuristic Inventory

Engel's book is the closest thing the olympiad world has to a canonical heuristic catalogue. Each chapter names a **strategy** that is itself a criterion for evaluating a solution: did the solver recognize that this strategy was the right one?

## The 14 Core Strategies (Chapter Headings)

A high-quality olympiad solution typically deploys one of these strategies, recognized at the outset and used cleanly:

1. **The Invariance Principle** — find a quantity preserved under the moves and show it cannot reach the target value.
2. **Coloring Proofs** — assign colors so that a parity or counting argument forces the result.
3. **The Extremal Principle** — pick the maximum/minimum element of a set; the choice forces structural conclusions.
4. **The Box (Pigeonhole) Principle** — count more objects than boxes; some box has multiple objects.
5. **Enumerative Combinatorics** — count cleverly with bijections, generating functions, inclusion-exclusion.
6. **Number Theory** — divisibility, modular arithmetic, classical congruences, primes.
7. **Inequalities** — AM-GM, Cauchy-Schwarz, rearrangement, Jensen, SOS.
8. **The Induction Principle** — strong induction, downward induction, infinite descent.
9. **Sequences** — recurrences, telescoping, generating functions.
10. **Polynomials** — roots, symmetric functions, irreducibility, Vieta.
11. **Functional Equations** — substitution patterns, fixed points, regularity from algebra.
12. **Geometry** — synthetic, coordinate, complex-number, vector, transformational methods.
13. **Games** — strategy stealing, pairing, mirror strategies, Sprague-Grundy.
14. **Further Strategies** — extremal-by-construction, double counting, invariants over more general structures.

## Implicit Criteria for a Good Olympiad Solution

A solution is judged by:

1. **Strategy recognition**: did the solver identify *which* of the standard strategies applied?
2. **Strategy execution**: was the chosen strategy applied cleanly (no false moves)?
3. **Economy**: minimum machinery needed.
4. **Generality**: does the argument also handle the general case, not just the stated $n$?
5. **Complete case enumeration**: are all cases covered?
6. **Bound tightness**: when bounds are involved, are they tight (with equality conditions stated)?
7. **Construction explicitness**: existence claims are backed by explicit constructions when possible.

## Engel's Implicit Pedagogical Rubric

Engel implicitly teaches that a student's skill is measured by:

- **Repertoire breadth**: how many of the 14 strategies the student has internalized.
- **Pattern recognition speed**: how quickly the student maps a problem to its strategy.
- **Cross-strategy fluency**: ability to combine strategies (e.g., extremal + invariance).
- **Failure recovery**: ability to abandon a strategy that isn't working and try another.

## Anti-Patterns in Olympiad Solutions

- **Brute force when a strategy applies** (e.g., case-checking by hand when invariance immediately settles it).
- **Strategy mismatch** (using induction when extremal is the right tool).
- **Incomplete induction** (failing to verify the base case, or using strong induction without justifying).
- **Implicit case omission** (claiming "WLOG" when there is no symmetry).
- **Tight inequalities without equality conditions** (a sign of incomplete understanding).
- **Symbol-pushing without geometric/structural insight**.

## Why Engel's List Functions as a Rubric

Olympiad problems are **deliberately constructed** so that one of these strategies (often a combination) is the intended path. The rubric for grading is essentially: did the solution find the intended path or an equivalently elegant alternative?

Engel's book is therefore both a strategy catalogue *and* an implicit evaluation framework: a solution that uses none of these strategies (or uses one badly) is suspect, even if it arrives at the right answer.

## Adaptation to Research Mathematics

Although olympiad-specific, Engel's strategy list maps onto research-mathematics moves:

- **Invariance principle** ↔ conserved quantities, cohomology classes
- **Extremal principle** ↔ minimal counterexample arguments
- **Coloring** ↔ partition arguments, parity invariants
- **Pigeonhole** ↔ counting arguments in infinite settings (e.g., compactness)
- **Functional equations** ↔ representation theory of solutions

A research solution can be evaluated for whether it deploys these moves with the same recognition-and-execution clarity that Engel demands of olympiad solvers.
