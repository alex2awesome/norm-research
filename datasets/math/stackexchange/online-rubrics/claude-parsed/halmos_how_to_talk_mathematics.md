# How to Talk Mathematics — Paul R. Halmos (1974)

Notices Amer. Math. Soc. 21 (3): 155–158, 1974. The companion to *How to Write Mathematics*. Halmos's rules for giving colloquia, AMS invited addresses, and seminar talks.

## Halmos's principles for a good math talk

1. **Prepare obsessively.** A 50-minute talk should be rehearsed end-to-end at least twice.
2. **Time discipline.** Aim for 45–50 minutes; running over 55 is rude. The audience's clock controls.
3. **Tell the audience what you'll tell them, tell them, then tell them what you told.** Three iterations of the main message.
4. **Start with a concrete example or motivating problem**, not a definition.
5. **Use the blackboard, not slides** (1974 advice; modern equivalent: handwritten tablet or sparse slides).
6. **Write large.** Top of the board to bottom; left to right; never erase what is still in use.
7. **Include some proof.** Don't just announce theorems; show one proof — even a sketch — so the audience hears what makes the result possible.
8. **Skip the routine.** Trust the audience to fill in standard manipulations; spend stage time on the hard idea.
9. **Connect to known mathematics.** Place the result in the larger landscape; tell the audience why a non-specialist should care.
10. **Don't apologize.** Never say "this won't be very interesting" or "I don't have time to explain". Cut the apologetic content.
11. **Don't talk to the board.** Face the audience; the back of your head does not enunciate.
12. **One main idea per talk.** A talk is not a paper. Sacrifice everything to make the one idea clear.
13. **End on time, then stop.** The conclusion is a brief summary plus a question — nothing more.
14. **Q&A is part of the talk.** Repeat each question for the room before answering.
15. **Watch the audience.** When eyes glaze, slow down or back up.

## Halmos's negative rules — what kills a talk

- Reading the slides aloud.
- Stacking definitions before any motivation.
- Long introductory historical surveys.
- Citing your own results in the first 10 minutes ("As I showed in 1962…").
- Using notation defined only on a slide that is no longer visible.
- Symbol-heavy slides with no English glue.
- Detailed proofs that no one in the room can follow in real time.
- Running over time.

## On audience adaptation

> "There is no such thing as a good talk in the abstract. There are only good talks for particular audiences."

A talk to specialists differs from a talk to graduate students differs from a colloquium for the whole department.

## Modern descendants

Bryna Kra's "Giving a Talk", Terence Tao's blog post on talks, Peter Norvig's "How to be a Programmer" all explicitly cite Halmos's structure.
