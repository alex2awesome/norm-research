# SEP: Mathematical Style — Dimensions of Style

The SEP entry distills several frameworks for analyzing mathematical style. Style is treated as both descriptive (how mathematics is written) and partly constitutive (different styles enable different objects of inquiry).

## Historical Style Distinctions

### Chevalley's Two Eras
- **The "ε-style"** of 19th-century Weierstrassian rigor — intensive use of inequalities, careful quantifier order, replacement of equality with inequality where appropriate.
- **The "axiomatic style"** of 20th-century mathematics — definition-first, structure-first, suppression of computational detail.

A piece of mathematics can be evaluated for whether it adheres consistently to one tradition or mixes them confusingly.

## Granger's Framework: Style as Conceptual Structuring

Style is "a way of introducing the concepts of a theory, of connecting them, of unifying them." Specifically:

- **Mode of introduction**: Are concepts introduced via examples, axioms, motivating problems, or counterexamples?
- **Mode of connection**: How is one concept linked to its neighbors — by definition, by theorem, by analogy?
- **Mode of unification**: What organizing principle ties the concepts together?

Different presentations of the same content (Granger's example: complex numbers as trigonometric/operational/matrix) preserve mathematical truth but differ in cognitive accessibility, range of generalization, and pedagogical fitness.

## Hacking's Constitutive View

Style of reasoning **introduces new objects of inquiry**. There is no neutral standpoint from which to ask whether a given object exists "before" the style that brings it into mathematical view. Implication for evaluation: a mathematical work cannot be judged purely "intrinsically"; it must be judged within a stylistic tradition that defines what its objects are.

## Style Axes (the SEP's working classification)

Mathematical style operates on multiple axes simultaneously:

1. **Geometric vs. algebraic** — does the proof reason via figures or via symbol manipulation?
2. **Conceptual vs. computational** — does it argue from structure or from calculation?
3. **Structural vs. intuitive** — does it appeal to axioms or to mental images?
4. **Local vs. methodological** — is it tied to a specific community/nation, or part of a universal method (Crombie's six "styles of scientific reasoning")?
5. **Constructive vs. classical** — is the existence of objects exhibited or only shown?
6. **Synthetic vs. analytic** — does the proof start from premises and build, or from the goal and decompose?

## Tappenden's Insight

Tappenden's "Proof style and understanding in mathematics" connects style choices to:

- **Visualization** — what a proof allows the reader to see
- **Unification** — what other results it groups with
- **Axiom choice** — which background commitments it activates

A change in style is rarely cosmetic; it commits the reader to different ways of seeing.

## Implied Rubric for Style Evaluation

For a piece of mathematics:

- Is its **stylistic tradition identifiable** (geometric? axiomatic? constructive?), or does it shift confusingly?
- Are its **modes of introduction, connection, unification** explicit?
- Does the chosen style **maximize understanding for the intended reader**?
- Is the style **consistent with the theorem's content** (not e.g. computational style for a structural claim)?
- Does it **acknowledge alternatives** (e.g., "there is also a geometric proof")?

## Conditions Under Which Style Matters

The entry notes that style critique is most relevant when:
- The same theorem can be proved in multiple styles (style is then a separable variable).
- Pedagogical clarity is the goal (different styles serve different audiences).
- Methodological purity is at stake (e.g., a number-theory result proved by complex analysis).
