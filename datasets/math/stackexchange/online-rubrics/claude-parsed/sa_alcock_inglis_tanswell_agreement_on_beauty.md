# Sa, Alcock, Inglis, Tanswell: Do Mathematicians Agree About Mathematical Beauty?

A cross-cultural empirical study testing whether mathematicians' aesthetic judgments are shared across cultures and expertise levels. Uses comparative judgment methodology to elicit fine-grained ratings.

## The Empirical Setup

Three populations:

1. **British mathematicians** (research-active)
2. **Chinese mathematicians** (research-active)
3. **British mathematics undergraduates**

Each population was asked to make pairwise comparative judgments of beauty between mathematical proofs. The comparative-judgment technique avoids the limitations of Likert scales and yields a continuous beauty parameter for each proof.

## Headline Empirical Findings

1. **Aesthetic agreement is robust within each population.** Mathematicians within a group converge on which proofs are most beautiful.

2. **Agreement holds across cultures.** British and Chinese mathematicians substantially agree on beauty rankings — cultural difference is *not* a major source of variation.

3. **Agreement holds across expertise levels.** Undergraduates and research mathematicians substantially agree, though with a smaller variance for experts (less noise in expert ratings).

4. **Agreement holds across types of mathematical objects.** Whether the items are proofs, theorems, or formulae, the agreement structure persists.

## How This Resolves the Earlier Disagreement-Finding Literature

Earlier work (Inglis & Aberdein 2020 on social conformity) had suggested that mathematicians' aesthetic judgments are not strongly stable. The Sa et al. study reconciles this:

- **Aesthetic *parameters*** (the underlying ranking) are shared.
- **Aesthetic *labels*** (the specific adjective applied: "elegant" vs "beautiful" vs "deep") may shift under social pressure.
- The disagreement was about **which adjective**, not about **which proofs are most beautiful**.

## Implications for Rubric Design

A rubric for mathematical beauty:

- Can rely on **mathematician consensus** as a real signal — it is not a private subjective phenomenon.
- Should aggregate across **multiple raters** to extract the shared parameter.
- Should distinguish **rank judgments** (robust) from **adjective choice** (less robust).
- Can in principle be **empirically calibrated** by collecting comparative judgments on a benchmark set.

## The Comparative-Judgment Method

The methodology itself is rubric-relevant. Rather than ask "rate this proof 1–10 for beauty," ask "which of these two proofs is more beautiful?" The pairwise data is then aggregated using Bradley-Terry or Thurstonian models to extract a single beauty parameter per proof.

This is a more reliable extraction technique than direct rating, and is recommended for any aesthetic-evaluation rubric in mathematics.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Beauty (cross-cultural)

A piece of mathematics is beautiful (in the empirically robust sense) when:

- A diverse panel of mathematicians, given pairwise comparisons, places it high on the inferred parameter.
- The placement is **stable across cultures** (British, Chinese, plausibly other communities).
- The placement is **stable across expertise levels** (experts and trained novices both rank it highly).
- The robustness implies the proof has features that are **objectively detectable** — features a future automated rubric should be able to measure.

## What Mathematicians Agree About (the inferred shared dimensions)

Although Sa et al. do not enumerate the underlying features, the empirical robustness of agreement suggests these features are:

- **Compactness / economy** (cf. Zeki et al.)
- **Surprising compression** of deep content
- **Cross-domain unification**
- **Inevitability** (the result feels necessary)
- **Generativity** (the proof opens further questions)
- **Insight density** (high ratio of conceptual content to text)

These should appear as components in any beauty-axis of a complete mathematical-quality rubric.

## Caveats

- The study uses curated proofs, not arbitrary research output. The agreement may be lower in border cases.
- The proofs studied are largely **classical** (well-known beautiful theorems). Agreement on **frontier research** is empirically untested and may be lower.
- The cross-cultural sample is two cultures (British, Chinese). Other cultures (Russian, Indian, African) are not yet tested.
