# Schoenfeld: Four-Component Framework for Mathematical Problem Solving

NOTE: An existing file `schoenfeld_mathematical_problem_solving_framework.md` covers the broad framework. This file zooms in on the **four-component evaluation framework** Schoenfeld uses for analyzing problem-solving episodes — which functions as a rubric for the *quality of solution attempts*.

## The Four Components

To be successful at mathematical problem-solving, one must possess (and a solution can be evaluated for):

1. **Cognitive Resources** — the propositional and procedural knowledge available.
   - Theorems known
   - Standard techniques mastered
   - Mental images of typical objects
   - Worked-example repertoire

2. **Heuristics** — strategies for making progress when stuck.
   - Polya-style: working backward, drawing a figure, looking for analogies
   - Domain-specific: trying small cases, exploiting symmetries, varying constraints
   - General: simplifying the problem, generalizing the problem

3. **Control (Metacognition)** — efficient deployment of resources and heuristics.
   - When to use what tool
   - When to abandon a fruitless approach
   - How to allocate time across sub-problems
   - When to back off and reconsider

4. **Belief Systems** — perspectives on the nature of mathematics.
   - "Mathematics is about pattern, not arbitrary rules"
   - "Problems should yield to genuine thinking, not just memory"
   - "If I'm stuck, I haven't tried the right approach yet"
   - vs. counter-productive beliefs: "Math is mysterious," "Either you see it or you don't"

## Empirical Findings (Expert vs. Novice Comparison)

Schoenfeld's protocol analysis reveals systematic differences:

- **Experts spend more time on analyzing the problem before computing.**
- **Experts more frequently verify** the adequacy of intermediate answers.
- **Experts allocate time strategically** across sub-problems based on difficulty.
- **Novices over-rely on familiar procedures** without checking applicability.
- **Novices fail to switch strategies** when an approach is failing.

A solution is therefore evaluated for whether it shows expert-like or novice-like patterns — independently of whether it arrives at the correct answer.

## Episodes in Problem Solving (Schoenfeld's micro-rubric)

Schoenfeld decomposes problem-solving into *episodes*. A solution can be coded for which episodes occur and in what order:

1. **Read** — the solver reads the problem statement.
2. **Analyze** — explores meaning, draws diagrams, identifies what is known/unknown.
3. **Explore** — tries different approaches, makes conjectures.
4. **Plan** — settles on a specific approach.
5. **Implement** — executes the plan.
6. **Verify** — checks the result.

A solution that skips Analyze and Plan (jumping straight from Read to Implement) is novice-like, even if it gets the right answer. A solution that loops back to Analyze when Implement fails is expert-like.

## Implied Rubric for Solution Quality

A mathematical solution is high-quality when it shows:

- **Adequate cognitive resources** for the problem.
- **Use of appropriate heuristics** — not random trial.
- **Strong control behaviors**: time spent analyzing, willingness to abandon failing approaches, verification of intermediate results.
- **Productive beliefs** evident in the approach.
- **Healthy episode structure**: Analyze and Plan are present, not skipped.
- **Recovery behavior**: when an approach fails, the solver returns to Analyze rather than persisting in error.

A solution is poor (in process terms) when:

- It jumps from Read to Implement.
- It persists in a failing approach without abandoning it.
- It contains no verification step.
- It shows belief in mathematics as memory rather than reasoning.
- It uses heuristics inappropriately or randomly.

## On Belief Systems as Quality Markers

Schoenfeld emphasized (1987, 1992) that **beliefs structure problem-solving capacity**. Beliefs that hurt:

- "There is one right way to solve this problem."
- "If I haven't seen this type before, I can't solve it."
- "Math problems should be solvable in 5 minutes."
- "Long calculations mean you're doing it wrong."

Beliefs that help:

- "Multiple approaches usually exist; some are easier."
- "Hard problems require restructuring, not just more effort."
- "Verification is part of solving, not optional."
- "Persistence on the wrong approach is unproductive; switch."

A rubric for evaluating solutions can include implicit belief markers — what does the work reveal about the solver's view of mathematics?

## Adaptation to Research Mathematics

Schoenfeld's framework is school-mathematics-focused, but the structure transfers:

- A research paper can be evaluated for whether its **resources** (theorems known) are adequate to the problem.
- For whether its **heuristics** are well-chosen.
- For whether the author shows **control** (e.g., signaling when an approach didn't work and why).
- For whether the author's **beliefs** about the area show through (e.g., "this problem requires a structural approach, not computation").

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Polya** (heuristics): Schoenfeld's heuristics component formalizes Polya's catalog.
- **Weber** (strategic knowledge): Weber's strategic knowledge sits inside Schoenfeld's "control + heuristics."
- **Hamami & Morris** (plan reconstruction): Schoenfeld's "plan" episode aligns with their planning theory.
