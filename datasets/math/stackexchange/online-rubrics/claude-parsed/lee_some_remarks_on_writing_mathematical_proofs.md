# Some Remarks on Writing Mathematical Proofs — John M. Lee, University of Washington (revised 2019)

A widely-used proof-writing guide for undergraduate and graduate students.

## General Considerations About Mathematical Writing

1. **Identify your audience.** Know who is reading and what they already know. For homework: write to convince a fellow student in the same class.
2. **Write in paragraph form.** Math proofs communicate to a *human reader.* "There is an overwhelming consensus that an ordinary prose narrative is much better suited to this purpose than formal symbolic statements." Paragraph-style proofs are easier to read than symbolic or two-column proofs.
3. **Use proper English.** Complete sentences, correct punctuation, no fragments, no run-ons, no dangling modifiers, subject-verb agreement, parallel structure, correct spelling.
4. **Write clearly.** Don't be stingy with intuitive explanations. State at the beginning what type of proof you're using ("we will prove the contrapositive", "by induction").
5. **Include motivation.** Explain why definitions are made as they are, why one might expect the theorem to be true, how one might have been led to the proof, why the proof is structured this way, how the result will be used. "Mathematicians call this the *motivation*, and it's an essential part of good mathematical exposition."
6. **Use first-person singular sparingly.** Default to "we" (writer + reader); use "I" only when really referring to yourself.
7. **Avoid most abbreviations.** s.t., w.r.t., w.l.o.g., iff are blackboard-only. Exception: i.e., e.g. (with the comma).
8. **Proofread.** Read end-to-end after thinking you're done.

## Special Considerations for Proof Writing

9. **State what you're proving.** Precede the proof with a precise English statement of the theorem.
10. **Label your theorems.** Theorem / Proposition / Lemma / Corollary — choice signals importance:
    - *Theorem* = important result.
    - *Proposition* = interesting in its own right but less important.
    - *Lemma* = useful for proving another result, perhaps not interesting alone.
    - *Corollary* = follows easily from a previous result.
11. **Show where proofs begin and end.** "Proof" at the start; QED symbol at the end.
12. **Write with precision.** For each statement, ask:
    - *What does it mean?* Every term well-defined; every symbolic name previously defined or quantified before its first use.
    - *Why is it true?* Justify by axiom, previously proved theorem, definition, hypothesis, previous step, or rules of logic.
13. **Include more than just the logic.** Intersperse formulas with words about why one step follows from another; minimise reader's work.
14. **Include the right amount of detail.** "If you think you probably know roughly how an argument would go but it seems too tedious to work through in detail, then you need to work through it!" Aim for the reader's "Aha!" experience.
15. **Distinguish formal vs. informal writing.** Formal = definitions, logical steps. Informal = motivation, intuition, sketch. Make the distinction easy for the reader to track.
16. **Proofread again** — preferably after a wait, or have someone else read it.

## Writing Mathematical Formulas

17. **In-line vs. displayed.** Single symbols and short formulas in line; large/important formulas centered and displayed. Numbered formulas must be displayed.
18. **Formulas function grammatically as part of a sentence** — never standalone. Either as a noun (object) or a clause (with relation as verb).
19. **Read each sentence aloud** — including formula readings — to check grammar.
20. **Displayed formula at end of sentence requires a period.** (Repeated for emphasis.)
21. **Don't begin a sentence with a math symbol.** Reword.
22. **Don't put two in-line formulas separated only by punctuation** — interpose a word.
23. **Relations/operators connect formulas, not English words.** "If x is real and >2" is BAD; rewrite.
24. **Logical symbols (∃, ∀, ⇒, ⇔, ∴, ∵)** should not replace English words. Exception: ⇒ and ⇔ between symbolic statements.
25. **Built-up expressions** (sums, integrals, matrices, fractions) either displayed or written so they fit on a line. In-line fractions use slashes.
26. **Multiple-equality syntax** (a=b=c=d) means each step is true and the chain reads forward. Mix only relations satisfying transitivity.

## Implication for "Good Math Writing"
Lee's framework is the most common pedagogical baseline: precision + motivation + paragraph prose + appropriate detail + proofreading. Each is a distinct evaluation axis.
