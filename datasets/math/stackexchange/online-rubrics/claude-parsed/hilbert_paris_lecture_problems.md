# Hilbert: Mathematical Problems — Criteria for a Good Problem

In the introduction to his famous 1900 address, Hilbert lays out explicit criteria for what makes a mathematical problem (and by extension, mathematical work) good.

## The Three Core Criteria

A good mathematical problem is:

1. **Clear and easily comprehended**
   > "What is clear and easily comprehended attracts, the complicated repels us."
   
   The very statement of the problem must be intelligible. Obscurity in formulation is a symptom of weakness, not depth.

2. **Difficult, but not inaccessible**
   > "Difficult in order to entice us, yet not completely inaccessible, lest it mock at our efforts."
   
   The problem must lie at the edge of current capability — too easy is uninteresting, too hard is barren.

3. **Significant**
   > "The deep significance of certain problems for the advance of mathematical science... and the important role which they play in the work of the individual investigator."
   
   The problem must matter — its solution must move mathematics forward, not merely add another datum.

## The "First Man on the Street" Standard

Hilbert quotes (approvingly) an unnamed French mathematician:

> "A mathematical theory is not to be considered complete until you can explain it to the first man whom you meet on the street."

The standard for *complete* understanding is **lay-comprehensibility of the result**, not formal closure. A theory whose statement requires expert vocabulary is unfinished.

## Hilbert's View on the Vitality of a Field

> "As long as a branch of science offers an abundance of problems, so long is it alive; a lack of problems foreshadows extinction."

A research program is judged not by its theorems alone but by **the open problems it generates**. A proof that closes a problem and opens no new ones is less valuable than one that opens many.

## On Judging Problem Value

Hilbert is honest that it is hard to evaluate a problem in advance:
> "The final award depends upon the gain which science obtains from the problem."

True merit emerges through the **methods developed** in pursuit of the solution, not from the answer alone. A problem whose solution required new techniques is more valuable than one solved by existing means.

## Implicit Criteria from the 23 Problems

Looking at the 23 problems Hilbert posed, additional features emerge:

- **Foundational reach**: many problems concern the foundations of a subject (consistency of arithmetic, axiomatization of physics).
- **Generality**: most problems are general statements, not specific instances.
- **Connection across fields**: problems link logic, geometry, number theory, physics.
- **Concreteness**: even foundational problems are stated as specific questions, not vague programs.
- **Operational test**: each problem has a definite criterion for when it would be "solved."

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Work

A piece of mathematical work scores higher when:

- Its **statement is clear** to a non-specialist mathematician.
- It addresses a **previously hard, open problem**.
- It develops **new methods** in the course of its solution.
- It **opens further questions**, not only closes the original.
- It has **foundational** or cross-disciplinary reach.
- It has a **definite operational success criterion** (you know when you've succeeded).

## The Optimistic Vision

> "Wir müssen wissen — wir werden wissen."
> ("We must know — we will know.")

Hilbert frames good mathematics as **answerable**: every well-posed mathematical question has a determinate answer. This is itself a normative criterion: a "problem" that cannot in principle be settled is not a good problem.

## Anti-Patterns Hilbert Implicitly Identifies

- Problems whose statement is itself unclear (often a sign of confusion, not depth).
- Problems too easy to be interesting.
- Problems too hard to admit any approach.
- Problems whose solution would not advance any larger goal.
- Theories so technically encrusted that they cannot be conveyed to the educated public.
