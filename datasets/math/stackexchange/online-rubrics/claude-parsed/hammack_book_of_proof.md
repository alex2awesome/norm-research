# Book of Proof — Richard Hammack (3rd ed., open access)

A widely-adopted, free, open-textbook for transition-to-proof courses. Approved by the AIM Open Textbook Initiative. Used at hundreds of U.S. universities.

## Structure of the book

I. **Fundamentals** — sets, logic, counting.
II. **How to Prove Conditional Statements** — direct, contrapositive, contradiction.
III. **More on Proof** — non-conditional, induction, cases, existence, uniqueness.
IV. **Relations, Functions, Cardinality** — equivalence, partitions, bijections.

## Hammack's golden rules of proof

1. **Always state what you are proving** at the start.
2. **Always state what you have proved** at the end (Q.E.D., $\square$).
3. **Use complete English sentences** with mathematical symbols inside them, never instead of them.
4. **Never start a sentence with a symbol.**
5. **Use "let" to introduce new variables.**
6. **Use "thus", "therefore", "hence" to mark logical conclusions.**
7. **One method per proof when possible.**
8. **Distinguish "if … then" (implication) from "iff" (equivalence).**

## Hammack's mistakes-to-avoid list

- Confusing converse with contrapositive.
- Using "any" ambiguously.
- Forgetting one direction of an iff.
- Writing the proof of the converse and calling it the proof of the implication.
- Misnaming the proof method ("This is a proof by contradiction" — but no contradiction is reached).
- Variables introduced inside one case carried into another.
- Induction proofs without explicit base case.
- Existence proofs without verification of the witness.
- Set-equality proofs that show only one inclusion.

## Hammack's proof templates (echoed from Velleman)

For each logical form $\forall, \exists, \Rightarrow, \Leftrightarrow, \neg, \wedge, \vee$, Hammack gives a starting template and a completion template — same idea as Velleman, but with an emphasis on writing in well-formed English paragraphs.

## Distinctive features

- **Sample student proofs** — annotated, with comments on what's right and wrong.
- **Two-column comparison** of correct and incorrect proofs.
- **Exercise sets** with full solutions for many.
- **A chapter on proofs by contrapositive** as separate technique from contradiction (a distinction many textbooks blur).

## Implicit rubric for grading a Hammack-style proof

A solution is "good" when:
1. The logical form of the statement is identified.
2. The matching proof template is followed.
3. Variables are introduced before use, with type stated.
4. Each step is in a complete English sentence.
5. The conclusion is explicit ($\square$ or "as required").
6. Only the necessary technique is used; no scaffolding from other methods leaks in.
7. The reader can verify the proof line-by-line without filling in unstated steps.
