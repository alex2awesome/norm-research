# Resnik & Kushner: The Critique of Steiner's Explanatory Account

A foundational critique of Steiner's "characterizing property" account of mathematical explanation. Important because it surfaces the **context-dependence** of explanatoriness and provides counterexamples.

## The Two Counterexamples

Resnik & Kushner offer:

1. **A proof that meets Steiner's criterion but does not feel explanatory** to working mathematicians.
2. **A proof that feels paradigmatically explanatory but fails Steiner's criterion** (the IVT proof on the rationals — fails because the proof of the Intermediate Value Theorem does not generalize to rationals).

These together suggest Steiner's criterion is neither necessary nor sufficient for explanatoriness.

## The Stronger Conclusion

Resnik & Kushner conclude that any **objective distinction** between explanatory and non-explanatory proofs is suspect. Explanatoriness is:

- **Audience-relative**: explains for *whom*?
- **Context-relative**: depends on what was previously puzzling.
- **Time-relative**: a proof can become more or less explanatory as the field develops.
- **Goal-relative**: explanatory for *what purpose*?

## What This Means for Rubric Design

If Resnik-Kushner are right, "explanatoriness" is not a single property of a proof. A rubric must:

- **Specify the audience**: explanatory for an undergraduate, a researcher, a non-mathematician?
- **Specify the prior puzzlement**: what was hard to understand before this proof?
- **Specify the goal**: explain *why* (Steiner), or explain *that* (verification), or explain *how* to prove similar things (heuristic)?
- **Acknowledge that disagreement** about explanatoriness is sometimes deep, not resolvable by a better criterion.

## The Hafner-Mancosu Reply

Hafner & Mancosu (2005) defend Steiner partially:

- Resnik-Kushner's counterexamples rely on **the authors' own intuitions**, not on surveys of practicing mathematicians.
- Empirical work (later: Inglis-Aberdein, Sa-Alcock-Inglis-Tanswell) shows mathematicians **do agree substantially** on which proofs are explanatory.
- Steiner's criterion may need refinement, but the underlying distinction is robust.

## The Resulting Pluralism in Explanation Accounts

The Resnik-Kushner critique has led to a **family of accounts**, each capturing one aspect of explanatoriness:

- **Steiner**: characterizing property + variation.
- **Lange (2014, 2017)**: bottom-up; symmetry, simplicity, modal necessity as separate criteria.
- **Kitcher**: unification — fitting into argument patterns.
- **Counterfactual accounts**: causal-style asymmetry tests.
- **Inglis & Mejía-Ramos**: cognitive — produces understanding in trained readers.

A complete rubric for explanatoriness uses several of these criteria, scored independently.

## Implied Multi-Criterion Rubric

For each proof, evaluate explanatoriness on:

1. **Steiner test**: characterizing property + deformation.
2. **Lange test (depending on result type)**: symmetry, simplicity, or modal necessity exploited?
3. **Kitcher test**: shared pattern with other proofs?
4. **Counterfactual test**: would the conclusion fail if the proof's content failed?
5. **Cognitive test**: does it produce understanding in trained readers?

A proof can be explanatory in one sense but not another. Mark each axis separately.

## Anti-Patterns Detectable from Multiple Critiques

A proof's explanatoriness is suspect when:

- It **fails Steiner's deformation test** (works only for the specific case).
- It **uses unique tricks** not part of any general pattern (fails Kitcher).
- It **does not exploit the structure** of the result (fails Lange).
- It **does not generate understanding** in mathematicians familiar with the area (fails Inglis-Mejía-Ramos).

A proof may legitimately fail one of these and pass others — the multi-criterion approach captures this.

## The Audience Specification Requirement

Following Resnik-Kushner, no rubric for explanatoriness is complete without specifying:

- **For whom** is this proof being evaluated?
  - Same-area researcher?
  - Cross-area researcher?
  - Graduate student?
  - Undergraduate?
  - Non-mathematician?
- **In what context** — what does the audience already know?
- **For what purpose** — is the goal verification, understanding, or method-extraction?

A proof can be highly explanatory for one audience and obscure for another. A rubric that ignores this fails.

## Connection to Empirical Aesthetic Studies

The empirical work of Inglis & Aberdein, Sa et al., and Zeki et al. partially vindicates Steiner against Resnik-Kushner: there is **substantial inter-rater agreement** on aesthetic and explanatory judgments. But the agreement is not perfect, and Resnik-Kushner's emphasis on context-dependence remains important: a single rubric cannot resolve all disagreements.

## Implications for AI Evaluation

When asked "is this proof explanatory?", an evaluator should:

- **Specify the assumed audience and context**.
- **Use multiple criteria** (not Steiner alone).
- **Report disagreements explicitly** (which criteria does it pass/fail?).
- **Acknowledge that some disagreements are deep** and not resolvable by more analysis.
