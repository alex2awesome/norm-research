# Beauty Is Not Simplicity: An Analysis of Mathematicians' Proof Appraisals — Matthew Inglis & Andrew Aberdein (2015)

*Philosophia Mathematica* 23 (1): 87–109. The first large-scale empirical study to identify the dimensions on which mathematicians actually evaluate proofs.

## Method

- Asked 80+ research mathematicians to rate proofs on 80 adjectives.
- Factor analysis on the responses.

## Result: 4 statistically separable dimensions

The 80 adjectives reduced to four orthogonal factors:

1. **Aesthetics** — beautiful, elegant, profound, inspired, charming, ingenious, sublime, deep.
2. **Intricacy** — complicated, intricate, deep (loads weakly), elaborate, dense, sophisticated.
3. **Utility** — useful, applicable, fertile, fruitful, informative, generative.
4. **Precision** — careful, meticulous, rigorous, exact, detailed, formal.

## Headline finding

**Aesthetics and simplicity are largely uncorrelated.** Mathematicians do not equate "beautiful" with "simple" — contrary to the long-standing folk theory of mathematical aesthetics (Hardy's "elegance from minimal hypotheses").

In fact, beauty correlates positively (mildly) with intricacy: many proofs called beautiful are also called intricate.

## Subsequent work (Aberdein, Inglis, Sa, Tanswell, Alcock)

- "Diversity in proof appraisal" (2019): even within the four-factor structure, mathematicians disagree substantially about specific proofs.
- "Do Mathematicians Agree About Mathematical Beauty?" (2022): the answer is "only weakly". Beauty appraisals correlate ~0.3 across raters — well above chance, well below consensus.
- Aesthetic judgments are partly social (testing a "social conformity account").

## The Inglis-Aberdein 16-adjective scale

A practical 16-item Likert instrument: 4 adjectives × 4 dimensions. Used in subsequent studies of math aesthetics:

| Aesthetics | Intricacy | Utility | Precision |
|---|---|---|---|
| beautiful | intricate | useful | precise |
| elegant | complicated | applicable | meticulous |
| profound | elaborate | fruitful | rigorous |
| inspired | sophisticated | informative | careful |

## Implications as a rubric

For evaluating a proof or solution (e.g., on Math Stack Exchange):
1. Score on each of the four orthogonal axes — they capture different qualities.
2. Don't conflate brevity with beauty.
3. Don't conflate detail with rigor — detail loads on intricacy, not precision.
4. Useful and beautiful are independent — a proof can be one without the other.
5. Disagreement is normal — aesthetic appraisal is partly subjective.

## Why this matters

This is the empirical answer to "what do mathematicians actually mean by 'good'?". Theoretical lists (Hardy, Tao, Rota) propose criteria; Inglis & Aberdein measure which criteria are statistically separable in practice.
