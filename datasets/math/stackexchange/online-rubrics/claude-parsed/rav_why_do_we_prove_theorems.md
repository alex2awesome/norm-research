# Rav: Why Do We Prove Theorems? — Proofs as Bearers of Mathematical Knowledge

A foundational paper in the philosophy of mathematical practice, arguing that proofs (not theorems) carry the substantive knowledge of mathematics. Provides a multi-functional account of proof.

## The Core Thesis

> "Proofs rather than theorems are the bearers of mathematical knowledge."

The standard view treats proofs as mere certifications that theorems are true. Rav inverts this:

- **Theorems are summaries**.
- **Proofs are the knowledge**.
- A theorem statement, divorced from proof, is a thin claim. The proof contains the methods, strategies, definitions, and connections that make up mathematical understanding.

## What Proofs Convey Beyond Theorem-Truth

A proof carries (in Rav's enumeration):

1. **Strategies and methods** — reusable approaches.
2. **Conceptual relationships** — links between definitions and other theorems.
3. **Heuristic patterns** — moves that succeeded for this problem.
4. **Definitions clarified** — the proof shows why the chosen definitions are right.
5. **Sub-results worth their own attention** — lemmas reusable elsewhere.
6. **Counterexamples ruled out** — implicit boundary information.
7. **Generalization paths** — directions in which the result extends.
8. **Restriction reasons** — why certain hypotheses are needed.
9. **Connection to other proofs** — embedding in the literature.
10. **Methodological precedents** — what kind of proof this is, for future reference.

## Why We Re-Prove Theorems

Rav addresses a puzzle central to mathematical practice: why publish a new proof of a theorem already proved?

If proofs were just verifications, multiple proofs would be redundant. But mathematicians frequently produce and value alternative proofs. Rav's answer:

- A **new proof carries new knowledge**: different strategies, methods, conceptual links.
- A **shorter proof** simplifies the area.
- A **more explanatory proof** improves understanding.
- A **more general proof** expands applicability.
- A **purer proof** (Detlefsen-Arana) clarifies what the theorem really depends on.
- A **different-style proof** (geometric vs. algebraic) connects the result to a different community.

Each new proof is a separate contribution to mathematical knowledge.

## Critique of Formalism

Rav targets the formalist view that ordinary proofs are abbreviations of formal derivations:

- The formalist view **predicts** that any informal proof can be (in principle) formalized in some axiomatic system.
- Rav argues this **does not capture practice**: the methods, strategies, and concepts that proofs convey are not preserved in formalization.
- Even if every informal proof could be formalized, **the knowledge carried by the informal version exceeds the formal one**.

## Systemic Cohesiveness

Rav's positive proposal: mathematical knowledge is reliable because of **systemic cohesiveness** — the network of proofs links into a tightly woven whole. Each new proof:

- **Strengthens connections** in the network.
- **Triangulates** results that have multiple proofs.
- **Reveals weaknesses** when proofs conflict or are hard to reconcile.

Reliability is therefore a **network property**, not an absolute certificate.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Work

A piece of mathematics is good (in Rav's terms) when:

- Its **proof carries substantial knowledge** beyond the theorem statement.
- Its proof's **methods are reusable** in other contexts.
- Its proof **clarifies the definitions** used.
- Its proof **identifies extractable lemmas** of independent interest.
- Its proof **suggests generalizations and restrictions**.
- Its proof **strengthens the network** by linking to other results.

A proof is poor (in Rav's terms) when it:

- Verifies the conclusion using a one-off trick that yields no transferable knowledge.
- Is opaque about its methods.
- Hides connections to known results.
- Cannot be modified to handle slight variants.

## Connection to AI-Generated Proofs

Rav's framework has direct implications for evaluating machine proofs:

- A formally verified proof that yields no methods, strategies, or insights is **not a contribution to mathematical knowledge** in Rav's sense — even if its theorem statement is correct.
- An AI that produces correct proofs but no extractable concepts produces **theorem-truth without mathematical knowledge**.
- A complete rubric must measure **knowledge content** of a proof, not only its truth-warrant.

## The Multi-Function Rubric (Rav's expanded list)

For each proof, evaluate:

1. **Truth-warrant**: does it establish the theorem? (necessary)
2. **Method-content**: does it teach a strategy?
3. **Concept-content**: does it clarify or refine definitions?
4. **Connection-content**: does it link to other parts of mathematics?
5. **Heuristic-content**: does it teach a way of approaching similar problems?
6. **Generalization-suggestiveness**: does it indicate where the result extends?
7. **Restriction-clarity**: does it show what limits the result?
8. **Network-density**: does it strengthen the web of mathematical knowledge?

A great proof scores high on multiple axes; a routine verification scores high on (1) only.

## Why Multiple Proofs Are Real Contributions

Rav's framework explains why journals continue to publish new proofs of well-known theorems: each new proof contributes distinct knowledge. This is empirically observed; Rav's framework is the philosophical justification.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Avigad ("Mathematical Method")**: Rav's framework directly inspires Avigad's multi-functional account.
- **Hamami & Morris (plan-based understanding)**: the plan a proof embodies is part of Rav's "method content."
- **Detlefsen-Arana (purity)**: a pure proof carries different knowledge than an impure proof of the same theorem.
- **Lakatos**: dialectical proof contributes the discovery process, which is part of "method content."
