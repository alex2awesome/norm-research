# The Two Cultures of Mathematics — W. T. Gowers (2000)

In V. Arnold, M. Atiyah, P. Lax, B. Mazur (eds.), *Mathematics: Frontiers and Perspectives*. Argues that mathematics has two coexisting cultures with different value systems, each legitimate, that often misjudge each other.

## The two cultures

**Theory-builders** value:
- Long programs that organize and unify (Grothendieck, Langlands).
- Conceptual abstractions that subsume many phenomena.
- "Right" definitions; the right framework is half the work.
- Generality; results that apply across categories.
- Completeness; full structural theorems.
- Beauty in the sense of harmony and inevitability of the framework.

**Problem-solvers** value:
- Single hard concrete problems (Riemann hypothesis, Erdős-style combinatorics).
- Bare-hands ingenuity; new tricks that are not yet absorbed into a theory.
- Sharp constants and best-possible bounds.
- Surprise; counter-intuitive results.
- Beauty in the sense of clever technique.
- Demonstrated power of a method by what it solves, not by what it organizes.

## Two complementary slogans

> Theory-builder: "The point of solving problems is to understand mathematics better."
> Problem-solver: "The point of understanding mathematics is to become better able to solve problems."

## Gowers's main argument

A significant part of the mathematical establishment looks down on combinatorics and discrete mathematics as not "deep". This judgment is a category error: it applies the criteria of one culture to the work of the other. Both cultures are essential to a healthy mathematics.

## Implicit rubric (from the essay's distinctions)

When evaluating a piece of mathematics, ask which culture it belongs to:

| Quality | Theory-builder version | Problem-solver version |
|---|---|---|
| Depth | Connects across categories | Reveals new combinatorial structure |
| Beauty | Inevitability of the framework | Surprise of the technique |
| Importance | Subsumes many subfields | Settles a long-open conjecture |
| Generality | Functorial, abstract | Sharp, explicit constants |
| Progress | New theory absorbs old work | New trick solves old problem |
| Test of value | Tools become universal | Bound is tight |

## Closing message

Mathematics has both cultures and needs both. Evaluation must use the criteria internal to the culture from which the work comes; cross-cultural snobbery is both unfair and intellectually limiting.
