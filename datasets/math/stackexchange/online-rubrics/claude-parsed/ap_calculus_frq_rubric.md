# AP Calculus AB / BC Free-Response Question Scoring Rubric — The College Board

The most widely-used calculus problem-solving rubric in the United States. Each FRQ is graded on a 0–9 scale (most years) with explicit "what earns this point" criteria published by the College Board.

## Structure of the rubric

Each question is decomposed into typically 5–9 distinct earnable points. Each point is awarded for a specific behavior:

- **Setup point** — correctly translates the problem into mathematics (correct integral, derivative expression, etc.).
- **Antiderivative / derivative point** — correct calculation of the relevant calculus object.
- **Numerical answer point** — correct final value.
- **Justification / interpretation point** — explanation of why the answer is correct, or what it means in context.
- **Units point** — correct units in the answer.

## Typical question scoring example (AP Calculus AB problem)

A 4-point free-response problem might allocate:
- 1 point for setting up the correct integral.
- 1 point for finding a correct antiderivative.
- 1 point for the answer (within 3 decimal places).
- 1 point for a correct interpretation in context with units.

## Universal AP Calculus rules

1. **Decimal answers must be correct to 3 decimal places** (rounded or truncated).
2. **Algebraic answers need not be simplified.**
3. **A correct answer with no work shown** earns the answer point only (not the setup or method points).
4. **Wrong algebra propagated through correct subsequent work** can still earn the method points.
5. **Bald answers** (no work) earn no partial credit.
6. **Cross-out work** is not graded; clearly marked work is.
7. **Calculator results must be specified** as such (e.g., "fnInt(2x, x, 0, 1) = 1").

## Scoring philosophy

Unlike Putnam, AP Calc rubrics maximize *partial credit*: every step has its own point. A student who sets up an integral but can't compute it still earns at the setup point.

## What makes a "good" AP Calculus solution (from the rubric)

1. **Show every step.** Bald answers don't earn full credit.
2. **Use proper notation.** $\int f(x) \, dx$, not $\int f$. $\frac{dy}{dx}$, not $y'$ unless context is clear.
3. **Label answers with units.**
4. **Justify with reference to specific calculus theorems** when relevant: "Since $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$, by the Intermediate Value Theorem there exists $c \in (a,b)$ with $f(c)=0$."
5. **For interpretation questions**, write a complete sentence with units and reference to context.
6. **Calculator-active questions** must specify the calculator command if the answer is not by-hand.
7. **Cross out clearly** anything you don't want graded.

## Influence

- Defines the standard for K–12 calculus assessment in the U.S.
- Underlies AP Statistics, AP Physics with Calculus, and similar rubrics.
- Adopted by many state university entrance assessments.
- Indirectly defines what high-school calculus teachers value in solutions: setup, computation, and justification weighted equally.
