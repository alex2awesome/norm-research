# Tappenden: Proof Style and Understanding — Visualization, Unification, Axiom Choice

A philosophical analysis of how proof style choices (presentation, axiom selection, visualization) shape what readers understand. Provides a rubric for the often-unstated stylistic dimensions of mathematical work.

## Core Thesis

Stylistic choices in mathematical exposition are not cosmetic. They:

- **Determine which connections become visible** to readers.
- **Determine which axioms feel natural** vs. artificial.
- **Determine how generalizable** a result appears.
- **Affect the prospects** for further development of the area.

## Three Major Stylistic Dimensions

### 1. Visualization

A proof's reliance (or non-reliance) on visualization is a substantive choice with consequences.

- **Visualizable proofs**: support the reader's geometric intuition; tend to suggest extensions to similar geometric configurations.
- **Non-visualizable proofs**: more general but less supportive of pattern-based extension.
- **Choosing axioms that maximize visualizability** is a research strategy.

Implication: a proof scoring well on Tappenden's framework offers **the maximum visualization compatible with the result's generality**.

### 2. Unification

A proof's choice of framework determines how many other results it groups with.

- A proof in a unifying framework (e.g., category theory, gauge theory) groups the result with many cousins.
- A proof in a specialized framework leaves the result isolated.
- The **unification afforded by a proof** is a quality dimension distinct from beauty or rigor.

### 3. Axiom Choice

Different axiomatizations of the "same" content can be more or less natural — and "naturalness" is partly determined by which definitions support the most explanatory proofs.

- The **best axiom system** is the one that makes the deepest theorems quickest to prove.
- The **best definitions** are those that capture invariant structures across many examples.
- A proof that relies on **artificial-looking axioms** may indicate that the framework is wrong, even if the proof is correct.

## The "Goodman-Style" Problem for Mathematics

Tappenden draws an analogy to Goodman's grue/green problem:

- Many properties could in principle organize a mathematical area.
- Most of them give terrible classifications (mathematical analogs of "grue").
- A few give natural classifications that support extensive theorem-proving.
- The choice of "good" properties is an **objective standard** but not derivable from logic alone.

A proof that **identifies and uses good properties** scores higher than one that uses bad-but-equivalent properties.

## "Right Definitions" as a Research Goal

> "Mathematicians often set finding the 'right'/'proper'/'correct'/'natural' definition as a research objective, and success — finding the 'proper' definition — can be counted as a significant advance in knowledge."

This means:

- **Good definitions are themselves mathematical achievements**, not preliminaries.
- A paper that **introduces a new and right definition** may be more valuable than one that proves a theorem with existing definitions.
- A rubric for mathematical work must include **definition-quality** as a dimension.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Style

A piece of mathematical work scores higher when:

- Its **proof is as visualizable** as the result allows.
- Its **framework unifies** the result with many others (or, when isolated, this is acknowledged).
- Its **axioms feel natural** — support deep theorems with short proofs.
- Its **definitions track invariant structure** across examples.
- Stylistic choices are **acknowledged as choices** (alternative styles noted).
- The **right definitions** are explicitly identified as a contribution where applicable.

A piece scores lower when:

- Visualization is suppressed for ideological reasons.
- Framework choices isolate the result unnecessarily.
- Axioms are artificial, requiring contortion to prove deep theorems.
- Definitions are arbitrary, not capturing structure.
- Style is presented as inevitable rather than chosen.

## Definition Quality Indicators

A definition is "good" when:

- It captures invariants across many examples.
- It supports short proofs of deep theorems.
- It generalizes naturally to higher dimensions or related contexts.
- It makes the right things equivalent and the right things distinct.
- It survives independent rediscovery.

A definition is "bad" when:

- It works only for the specific examples it was crafted for.
- It requires arbitrary choices that could go either way.
- It makes deep results seem accidental.
- Different mathematicians keep proposing "improvements" that look fundamentally different.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Bourbaki**: structuralist style is one extreme of unification.
- **Avigad**: justifying definitions is one of his "functions of proof."
- **Detlefsen-Arana**: purity is a constraint on stylistic choices.
- **Lakatos**: monster-barring vs. concept-stretching is a debate about definitional quality.

## Implications for AI-Generated Mathematics

A machine that produces correct proofs but uses **bad definitions** or **unnatural axioms** is not producing good mathematics. The community's standards include not just correctness but the cultivation of **good frameworks** — and this is currently very hard to automate.
