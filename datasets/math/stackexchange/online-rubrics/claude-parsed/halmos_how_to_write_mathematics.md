# How to Write Mathematics — Paul R. Halmos (1970)

Originally published in L'Enseignement Mathématique, Vol. 16 (1970), and reprinted in the AMS volume *How to Write Mathematics* (Steenrod, Halmos, Schiffer, Dieudonné, 1973). One of the most-cited essays on mathematical exposition.

## Core principles (Halmos's rules for good mathematical writing)

1. **Have something to say.** The most important ingredient of good exposition is having an important idea worth communicating. Without that, no amount of stylistic polish helps.

2. **Have something to say to a specific audience.** Identify the reader explicitly — a beginning graduate student, a working specialist, an undergraduate. Aim every sentence at that reader.

3. **Organize first.** Spend a long time on the *outline*. Decide the order of theorems, lemmas, definitions, examples *before* writing prose. Reorganization is the bulk of writing.

4. **The spiral plan.** Don't present material in strict logical order — that is unreadable. Use the "spiral" method: introduce concept A loosely, then B, then return to A in more depth, etc. Logical order ≠ pedagogical order.

5. **Say something.** Every sentence must communicate. Avoid filler, throat-clearing, and meta-commentary about what you are about to do.

6. **Speak in complete sentences.** Mathematical symbols sit inside English sentences. Read your text aloud — if it doesn't parse, fix it. Never start a sentence with a symbol; never abut two formulas with no word between.

7. **Use words, not symbols.** Where a word will do, prefer the word. Reserve symbols for the parts that genuinely need precision. Excessive notation is the most common defect.

8. **Use the right words.** Distinguish "any" / "every" / "all" / "some" carefully. Avoid the ambiguous "any". Distinguish "we" from "I" from "the reader" with intent.

9. **Resist symbols.** Avoid pronouns of formulas (using a single symbol for two different things in nearby sentences). Don't reuse a symbol for two purposes inside one paper.

10. **Use consistent notation.** Pick one letter type for one type of object (lowercase Latin for points, uppercase for sets, Greek for ordinals, etc.) and never deviate.

11. **Avoid pronouns and unclear references.** "It", "this", "that" must always have an unambiguous referent within reading distance.

12. **Write in the active voice when possible.** Use "we" rather than passive constructions. Avoid "It is shown that..."; prefer "We show that...".

13. **Write good English first.** Mathematical writing is a subset of English writing. Strunk & White applies.

14. **Use the present tense.** Theorems are eternally true; describe them in the present tense ("Let G be a group", not "Let G have been a group").

15. **Tell the reader what you are doing and why.** Start each section with motivation. Don't drop a definition without explaining what problem it solves.

16. **State the theorem first; then prove it.** Don't make the reader guess what is being proved during the proof.

17. **Choose notation that helps the reader.** Mnemonic letters; respect tradition. Don't call a function $f$ if every other reader has called the same function $\phi$ for fifty years.

18. **Avoid "obviously", "clearly", "trivially".** Either prove it or genuinely show why it is obvious by giving the argument briefly. These words are often signs of a gap.

19. **Cross-reference clearly.** Theorem 3.4.1 is better than "as shown above". Make it possible to read the paper out of order.

20. **Anticipate reader confusion.** Imagine the reader stopping at every sentence and asking "what?". Pre-emptively answer.

21. **Resist "cute" notation, jokes, puns.** They date badly and trip the non-native English reader.

22. **Revise. Then revise again.** Halmos rewrote each paper at least six times. The first draft is for the writer; later drafts are for the reader.

23. **Don't repeat yourself, but do reinforce.** Vary phrasing; remind the reader of definitions used long ago.

24. **The first sentence matters most.** Spend disproportionate effort on the opening sentence of every section.

25. **Include examples.** A theorem without an example to anchor it is opaque. Examples should come *before* the abstract statement when possible.

## Halmos on style

> "The basic problem in writing mathematics is the same as in writing biology, writing a novel, or writing directions for assembling a harpsichord: the problem is to communicate an idea."

> "The best way to learn to write is to write. The next best way is to read. The worst way is to be told."

## Reception

Universally cited; used as the canonical reference in essentially every "how to write mathematics" course since 1970. Halmos's twin essays *How to Talk Mathematics* and *How to Write Mathematics* form the basis of much subsequent advice.
