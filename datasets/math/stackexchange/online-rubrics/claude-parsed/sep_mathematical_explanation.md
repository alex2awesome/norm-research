# SEP: Mathematical Explanation — When Does a Proof Explain?

The SEP entry on mathematical explanation surveys the major accounts of what makes a proof not merely valid but explanatory. The accounts together yield a multi-dimensional rubric for "explanatoriness."

## The Core Distinction

Following Aristotle, "proof of the fact" (ὅτι, *that*) vs. "proof of the reasoned fact" (διότι, *why*). All proofs in good standing establish that the conclusion is true; only some establish *why*. A rubric for "good proof" must measure the latter separately.

## Steiner's Account: Characterizing Properties

A proof is explanatory when it deduces the conclusion from a **characterizing property** — a property unique to the entity in question within its domain. Test:

- **Vary the characterizing property**: does the proof yield a corresponding theorem under deformation? If so, the original was explanatory; if not, it was an accidental verification.
- **Reverse test**: change the conclusion; does the proof break in a corresponding way?

A proof that uses generic machinery (e.g., a clever generating-function trick) without invoking what is specific to the object is non-explanatory in Steiner's sense.

## Lange's Bottom-Up Framework

No single criterion. Different domains have different markers of explanatoriness:

- **Symmetry**: a proof of a symmetric result is explanatory if it derives that symmetry from a corresponding symmetry in the setup.
- **Simplicity**: a proof of a simple result is explanatory if it exploits a similar simplicity in the setup.
- **Modal/necessity**: a proof of a mathematically necessary truth is explanatory if it shows the constraint that forces the necessity.

## Kitcher's Unificationist Account

A proof explains by **unifying** — fitting the result into a small set of derivation patterns that account for many phenomena. Criteria:

- **Argument-pattern frugality**: how many distinct patterns does the explanation use?
- **Stringency**: how restrictive is each pattern (more restrictive = more explanatory)?
- **Conclusion coverage**: how many results follow from the same pattern?

A proof that is one-off (uses a unique trick) is less explanatory than one that instantiates a pattern shared by many results.

## Counterfactual Accounts

A mathematical fact F explains another fact G when both hold:

- **CF1** (relevance): If F were false, G would be false.
- **CF2** (asymmetry): It is *not* the case that if G were false, F would be false.

This requires reasoning across "impossible worlds" but operationalizes the asymmetry of explanation.

## Internalist / Cognitive Account (Inglis & Mejía-Ramos)

A proof is explanatory to the extent it generates understanding by:

1. **Directing attention** to conceptually significant sections.
2. **Reorganizing information** into coherent schemas.
3. **Reducing cognitive load** on the reader's working memory.

This is empirically testable: explanatoriness is whatever produces understanding in trained readers.

## Implied Rubric for Explanatoriness

A proof can be scored along these dimensions:

- **Characterizing property used?** (Steiner test)
- **Symmetry/simplicity/necessity exploited?** (Lange tests)
- **Pattern shared with many proofs?** (Kitcher test)
- **Counterfactual asymmetry holds?** (CF tests)
- **Generates understanding in trained readers?** (Inglis test)

## Caveats from the SEP

- Explanatoriness is **proof-relative, not theorem-relative** — a single theorem may have explanatory and non-explanatory proofs.
- It is **partially audience-relative** — what explains for an undergraduate may not explain for a researcher.
- It is **partially context-relative** — what counts as explanatory depends on what was previously puzzling.

## Anti-Patterns

A proof scores low on explanatoriness when it:

- **Uses generic machinery** that does not invoke what is specific.
- **Is a "magic trick"** — establishes the conclusion through a manipulation no one could have anticipated.
- **Verifies without revealing structure** (e.g., long induction with no organizing idea).
- **Hides asymmetries** (works equally well for related true and false claims).
