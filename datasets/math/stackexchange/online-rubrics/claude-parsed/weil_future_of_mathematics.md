# Weil: The Future of Mathematics — Criteria for Great Mathematics

## Core Criterion: Simplification

Weil's central thesis is that **the great mathematical ideas are simplifying ideas**. The mathematician's job is not to multiply technical machinery but to clarify:

- "The great ideas must be simplifying ideas"
- "The creator must always be one who clarifies, for himself and for others, the most complicated tissues of formulas"
- A mathematical work that adds complexity without compensating clarification is suspect.

## Criteria for Mathematical Work

1. **Foundational reach over detail**: Mathematics "is not a science that prospers on details painstakingly collected." Significance trumps thoroughness.
2. **Conceptual unity**: Works that reveal underlying unity across apparently disparate areas are valued more than incremental refinements.
3. **Survival under simplification**: Today's complicated apparatus is judged by whether future mathematicians will see it as essential or merely transient. (As Apollonius's conic-section details now seem inessential, much modern technique will likely be discarded.)

## Pedagogical Criteria for Mathematical Exposition

Weil distills the requirements for good mathematical exposition (and by implication, for good mathematical writing):

1. **Understanding over memorization**: "Knowledge of a proof means the understanding of its machinery and the ability to reconstruct it." A reader who can recite but not reconstruct does not understand.
2. **Connection to general principles**: Teaching/writing should follow "the method which is related to the most general principles" rather than chasing local elegance or speed.
3. **Logical rigor of distinctions**: The student/reader must distinguish:
   - **Premises and conclusion**
   - **Necessary and sufficient conditions**
   - **A theorem and its converse**
4. **Intellectual excitement**: Good exposition brings the reader "to the brink of the unknown" — exposing live questions, not only settled answers.

## Implied Rubric for Judging Mathematical Work

- Does it **simplify** something previously complicated?
- Does it **reveal a principle** rather than merely accumulate facts?
- Will its key ideas **survive** future reorganization, or are they tied to current notation?
- Does its exposition let a reader **reconstruct**, not just verify?
- Does it scrupulously distinguish **necessary vs. sufficient**, **theorem vs. converse**?
- Does it **bring the reader to the unknown**, or does it close every door?
