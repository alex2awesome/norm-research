# Responses to "Theoretical Mathematics" by Jaffe and Quinn — Atiyah, Borel, Mac Lane, Mandelbrot, Ruelle, Thom, Uhlenbeck, Witten and others (1994)

Bull. Amer. Math. Soc. 30 (2): 178–211, 1994. arXiv:math/9404229. A multi-author response to Jaffe & Quinn's call to formally distinguish "rigorous" from "speculative/theoretical" mathematics. Each author articulates implicit values for what counts as "good mathematics".

## Atiyah's response — what makes mathematics valuable

1. Mathematics is a creative human enterprise; rigid formalism is sterile.
2. **Speculation, conjecture, and physical intuition are essential** to mathematical progress; they should not be banished or ghettoized.
3. The goal of theory is to **organize past experience** so the next generation can absorb essentials painlessly. Quote: "The aim of theory really is, to a great extent, that of systematically organizing past experience in such a way that the next generation, our students and their students and so on, will be able to absorb the essential aspects in as painless a way as possible."
4. A field that admits only fully rigorous work and rejects conjecture-driven exploration cuts off its own growth.
5. Style: a mathematician should aspire to elegance, vision, and the ability to see distant analogies, not merely to formal correctness.

## Borel's response

- Rigour is essential, but rigour without ideas is pedantry.
- The historical record shows great progress driven by partly heuristic arguments later made rigorous (Euler, Riemann).

## Mac Lane's response

- Rigour is what distinguishes mathematics from other sciences.
- BUT the *form* of rigour evolves; what was acceptable to Euler is not acceptable now.
- A "good" mathematician can move fluently between heuristic exploration and formal proof.

## Mandelbrot's response

- Computer experimentation and visualization are legitimate first-class methods.
- Discovery often precedes proof by decades; both are mathematical.

## Thom's response

- Conceptual understanding is more important than formal verification.
- A "theorem" understood without proof is more valuable than a proof not understood.

## Ruelle's response

- Mathematics and physics share a continuum; the boundary is not a value judgment.
- Predictive heuristic arguments deserve credit even when the underlying objects are not yet defined.

## Witten's response

- Many of the most important developments in late-20th-century mathematics came from physics and were initially rigorous only in the physical sense.
- Demanding full rigour up-front would have prevented mirror symmetry, Seiberg-Witten theory, etc.

## Implicit shared rubric across responses

A piece of mathematics is judged on:

1. **Rigour appropriate to its claim** — formal proof for a formal claim; well-founded heuristic for a heuristic claim.
2. **Honesty about epistemic status** — speculation labeled as such, theorems labeled as such.
3. **Generative power** — does it open new questions and tools?
4. **Connection to other fields** — pure-pure, pure-applied, pure-physical.
5. **Long-term clarification** — does the next generation understand the subject better because of this work?
6. **Communicability** — can the ideas be transmitted to non-specialists?
7. **Aesthetic appeal** — beauty, elegance, inevitability remain valid criteria.

## Closing influence

This collection is the canonical citation when defending mathematics done in modes other than fully formal proof — applied mathematics, mathematical physics, computer-assisted exploration, conjectural number theory.
