# On Proof and Progress in Mathematics — William P. Thurston (1994)

Bull. Amer. Math. Soc. 30 (2): 161–177, 1994. arXiv:math/9404236. Foundational essay on what mathematicians actually do, what proof is for, and how mathematical understanding is communicated.

## Central thesis

Mathematics is not the production of proofs; it is the production of human understanding of mathematical structures. Proofs are one means to this end, not the end itself.

## What mathematicians do (Thurston's reframing)

> "We mathematicians need to put far greater effort into communicating mathematical ideas. To accomplish this, we need to pay much more attention to communicating not just our definitions, theorems, and proofs, but also our ways of thinking."

## How understanding is communicated — a non-uniqueness theorem

A single mathematical concept (Thurston gives the example of the *derivative*) can be understood in many genuinely different ways:

1. Infinitesimal — the ratio of two infinitesimals.
2. Symbolic — a formal manipulation rule on expressions.
3. Logical — the limit definition with $\epsilon$, $\delta$.
4. Geometric — slope of the tangent line.
5. Rate — speed of change of one quantity vs. another.
6. Approximation — best linear approximation.
7. Microscopic — what the function looks like under infinite zoom.

Different people hold different combinations of these. Good exposition transfers the ones the reader is missing.

## What makes a proof "good"

- A proof is a social-epistemic act: it convinces the relevant community.
- The standard for "good proof" depends on what understanding the audience already has.
- A formal proof in a proof assistant is typically *bad* communication, even if it is rigorous.
- The best proofs raise more questions than they settle.

## The Jaffe–Quinn debate context

Thurston is responding to Jaffe and Quinn's call for a sharp distinction between "rigorous" and "speculative" mathematics. Thurston argues this misses the point: most mathematical progress lives in the productive intermediate zone where conjecture, partial proof, and intuition coexist.

## Thurston's checklist for evaluating mathematical work

Implicit throughout the essay:

1. Does it transfer understanding?
2. Does it open new questions?
3. Does it connect previously unrelated ideas?
4. Does it survive future generations of mathematicians who think differently?
5. Does it raise the entire field's level, not just add a theorem?
6. Is the exposition multi-modal (geometric + algebraic + intuitive + formal)?

## Famous quote on his own work

Thurston regrets having "proved theorems that nobody else could understand", saying his foliations work, while correct, set the field back because it discouraged others. The lesson: proving alone is insufficient if the proof is not absorbed.

## Influence

Together with Atiyah's reply to Jaffe–Quinn and Tao's "What is Good Mathematics?", this essay forms the modern canon on the philosophy of mathematical practice.
