# Wittgenstein on Mathematical Proof: Surveyability, Rule-Following, Concept-Forming

Wittgenstein's later philosophy of mathematics (especially *Remarks on the Foundations of Mathematics*) yields several distinctive criteria for what makes a mathematical demonstration a *proof*. These criteria are sharply different from formalist criteria and provide a complementary rubric.

## Three Core Wittgensteinian Criteria

### 1. Surveyability (Übersichtlichkeit)

A proof must be **surveyable**: one must be able to grasp it as a unified, finite construction.

- A "proof" too long to be grasped as a whole is not, on Wittgenstein's view, a proof at all — it is an inductive promise.
- This puts pressure on **massively long machine-checked proofs** (the four-color theorem, Kepler conjecture) — they may be correct but lack the epistemic status of *surveyable* mathematical proof.
- Implication for rubrics: a proof's **graspability as a whole** is itself a quality dimension, not merely a presentational nicety.

### 2. Concept-Forming Function

> "A proof alters the grammar of a proposition."

A proof does not merely discover a pre-existing truth — it **fixes the meaning** of the terms it uses by placing them in a new system of relations. Implications:

- A proof that does not produce any conceptual reorganization is a routine derivation, not a genuine proof.
- The **best proofs change how we understand the concepts** involved.
- A rubric should ask: *what concepts did this proof reshape?*

### 3. Rule-Following / Algorithmic Determinacy

A mathematical proof must demonstrate that **rules generate consequences through finite, algorithmic steps**. The reader must, in principle, be able to check each step mechanically.

- "Vagueness" in the inferential moves is *not* a sign of depth but a sign that the proof is incomplete.
- Each step must be **rule-following in a public sense** — verifiable by any competent reader applying the same rules.

## The Decidability Criterion

> "Only where there's a method of solution is there a [mathematical] problem."

A purported mathematical question without an effective decision procedure is not, for Wittgenstein, a well-formed question. This is a strong claim — most mathematicians today reject it in full generality — but it functions as a useful hygienic criterion: a "problem" with no operational test for success is suspect.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Work

A piece of mathematics scores higher when:

- It is **surveyable** — the reader can hold the proof in mind as a single object, not as a chain of opaque steps.
- It **forms a new concept** or sharpens an existing one.
- Each step is **mechanically verifiable** by a competent reader.
- The problem it addresses has a **clear operational success criterion**.
- It does not rely on **infinitary intuitions** that cannot be cashed out in finite calculation.

## On Long Proofs

Wittgenstein's surveyability criterion is the most controversial. A natural response: a long proof is acceptable if:
- It can be **decomposed into surveyable lemmas** (each independently graspable).
- Its **overall plan** is surveyable even if the executions are not.
- A trained reader can **see the architecture** without reading every line.

This puts a quality burden on long proofs to provide a **surveyable scaffolding**.

## Anti-Patterns Wittgenstein Warns Against

- Proofs that rely on **"intuition" that cannot be made into a public rule** (a private appeal).
- Proofs that **claim** to settle a question without exhibiting a method.
- Proofs that **leave the concepts they use unchanged** but produce a long manipulation (these are at best calculations, not proofs in the deeper sense).
- Proofs that are too **long to survey** without compensating structure.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Lakatos's** dialectical proof aligns with Wittgenstein on concept-formation.
- **Surveyability** is a precursor to modern concerns about computer-assisted proofs (Tymoczko, Detlefsen).
- **Rule-following** sits in tension with Avigad's "ability to spell out implicit detail" — the question is whether the implicit details are public-rule-followable in principle.
