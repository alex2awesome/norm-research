# Talks Are Not The Same As Papers — Terry Tao

## Core Principle
A talk should *complement* a paper by providing "a high-level and more informal overview." Avoid treating a talk like a projected paper.

## Differences From Papers

A talk should provide:
1. High-level, informal overviews.
2. Focus on interesting/important components with greater detail.
3. Explanation of *how* the final proofs were obtained.
4. Background review aiding comprehension.

## Engaging the Audience

5. **Make talks friendly to non-experts.** Devote initial minutes to basic examples and background material. Even experts appreciate fresh perspectives on basics.
6. **Use pedagogical scaffolding.** Present a new theorem's proof by first reviewing the proof of a standard theorem in similar style, then highlighting key differences.
7. **Structure for breadth.** Colloquia and general talks benefit from narrative: pose open problems, reveal mysterious phenomena, frame surprising results, build suspense, include judicious humour.

## Format Guidance
- **Blackboard.** Flexible, nonlinear, allows audience reference.
- **Transparencies / slides.** Convey detailed information efficiently but allow style to obscure substance.
- **Computer slides.** Best for animations/graphics; avoid form-over-substance.

## Time Management
- Practice the talk to gauge pacing.
- Move optional material to the end or to "deleted scenes" for flexibility.

## Implication for Good Math Communication
"Good" math exposition is genre-aware: it knows whether it is a paper, a talk, or a blog post and uses each medium's strengths.
