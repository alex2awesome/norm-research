# Learning to Think Mathematically: Problem Solving, Metacognition, and Sense Making — Alan H. Schoenfeld (1985, 1992)

The standard cognitive-science framework for what makes a *solution* (not just a proof) good. Schoenfeld's four-component model is taught in every mathematics-education program and underlies most modern problem-solving rubrics.

## Schoenfeld's four components of mathematical competence

A successful problem-solving performance requires all four:

### 1. Resources
The propositional and procedural knowledge available:
- Definitions, theorems, formulas, algorithms.
- Worked examples in long-term memory.
- Routine subroutines (factoring, integration by parts).

### 2. Heuristics
Pólya-style strategies for moving when stuck:
- Look for analogous problems.
- Specialize / generalize.
- Work backwards from the goal.
- Draw a figure.
- Decompose the problem.
- Consider an auxiliary problem.
- Vary the problem.

### 3. Control (Metacognition)
The decisions that orchestrate the use of resources and heuristics:
- *When* to switch strategies.
- *How long* to pursue an approach before abandoning.
- *Monitoring* progress: are we closer to the goal?
- *Resource allocation* across sub-problems.
- *Reflection* after solution: was that the right approach?

### 4. Belief systems
The implicit "world view" the solver brings:
- Math is a coherent body of ideas (vs. a set of disconnected tricks).
- Problems should be approached by understanding (vs. by template-matching).
- A real problem takes time (not 5 minutes).
- Solutions can be checked, generalized, and reflected on.

## Schoenfeld's diagnostic for "what went wrong"

For any failed problem-solving attempt, classify the failure:
- **R** — missing the resource.
- **H** — had the resource, didn't think to use it.
- **C** — used it, but didn't notice the dead end / didn't allocate time.
- **B** — gave up because of belief that it was unsolvable.

## Implications as a rubric for grading solutions

A "good solution" exhibits:
1. Correct *resources* used appropriately.
2. *Heuristic choice* visibly justified.
3. *Control* — abandoned dead ends are honestly described, not airbrushed; time is spent on the genuinely hard step.
4. *Reflection* — after the solution, alternative approaches and generalizations are discussed.

## The famous classroom protocol

Schoenfeld interrupts students mid-problem with:
- "What exactly are you doing?"
- "Why are you doing that?"
- "How does it help you?"

A student who cannot answer these is solving by reflex, not by reasoning.

## Influence

- Standard reference in math-ed PhD programs.
- Basis of the "metacognitive coaching" literature.
- Modern AI work on chain-of-thought reasoning explicitly invokes Schoenfeld's "control" component.
