# Kuhn: Five Criteria for Theory Choice (Adapted to Mathematics)

Kuhn famously identified five values that scientists invoke in theory choice. While developed for empirical science, these transfer to mathematical work and provide a standard rubric.

## The Five Criteria

A theory (or piece of mathematical work) is evaluated for:

1. **Accuracy** — agreement with observed data; in mathematics, agreement with prior theorems and accepted results.
2. **Consistency** — internal logical consistency, and consistency with other accepted theories.
3. **Scope** — extent of consequences beyond the data the theory was built to explain.
4. **Simplicity** — ability to organize phenomena that would otherwise be confused or isolated.
5. **Fruitfulness** — capacity for further research; opening of new questions.

## Adaptation to Mathematical Work

For a piece of mathematics:

1. **Accuracy / correctness**: Does it follow from accepted axioms / prior results without error?
2. **Consistency**: Is it internally consistent and consistent with the rest of mathematics?
3. **Scope**: Does it apply to many cases, or just one?
4. **Simplicity**: Does it organize previously isolated phenomena under one principle?
5. **Fruitfulness**: Does it open new problems, suggest new methods, generate further work?

These map roughly onto **rigor, coherence, generality, elegance, and generativity** — and provide a five-axis rubric.

## Kuhn's Crucial Caveat: Criteria Underdetermine Choice

Kuhn emphasized that these five criteria **do not uniquely determine theory choice**:

- **Which features satisfy each criterion is disputable**. (Is "simplicity" about ontological commitment or about mathematical form?)
- **The criteria are imprecise**. Reasonable people disagree about how well a theory scores on each.
- **The criteria can conflict**. Simplicity may push one way, scope another.
- **Different scientists weight them differently**. There is no algorithm for combining the five into one verdict.

This caveat applies to mathematics: there is no formula for combining Kuhn's five into "the best theorem." Reasonable mathematicians can disagree on overall ranking even when they agree on individual axis scores.

## What This Means for Rubric Design

A multi-axis rubric (like Tao's, or Inglis-Aberdein's) is the *correct response* to Kuhn's underdetermination:

- **Score independently** on each axis.
- **Do not collapse** to a single quality number.
- **Acknowledge** that ranking is partly community-relative.
- **Use the multi-axis profile** for comparison, not a single ordering.

## Implications for Mathematical Paradigms

While mathematics is less paradigm-driven than empirical science, paradigm-shifts do occur:

- **Algebraization of geometry** (Descartes).
- **Set-theoretic foundation** (Cantor, Zermelo).
- **Bourbakist structuralism** (mid-20th century).
- **Categorical foundations** (Mac Lane, later Lurie).
- **Constructive / computational foundations** (Bishop, Martin-Löf).

Each shift was initially controversial; each was eventually evaluated using the five Kuhnian criteria (does the new framework give better accuracy, consistency, scope, simplicity, fruitfulness?).

A paradigm shift in a mathematical subfield should be evaluated by:
- Does it give **simpler proofs** of established theorems?
- Does it have **wider scope** (applies to more cases)?
- Is it **consistent** with established mathematics?
- Is it **fruitful** (produces new theorems, opens new problems)?
- Is it **accurate** (the established results survive in the new framework)?

## The Incommensurability Issue (Adapted)

Kuhn famously argued that competing paradigms can be **incommensurable** — they cannot be directly compared because they share no common standard. In mathematics, this appears in:

- **Constructive vs. classical**: theorems true in classical math may not be provable constructively.
- **Standard vs. non-standard analysis**: different ontologies of "real number."
- **Set theory vs. category theory**: different foundational primitives.

A rubric must recognize that mathematicians working in different foundational frameworks may legitimately disagree about what counts as a theorem of a given form. This is not a failure of the rubric but a recognition that **mathematical taste is partly framework-relative**.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Work

For each piece, score on five axes:

- **Accuracy**: rigorous correctness.
- **Consistency**: internal and external coherence.
- **Scope**: range of application.
- **Simplicity**: organizing power.
- **Fruitfulness**: generative potential.

Then **note framework dependencies**: does the work require a specific paradigm? If so, evaluate it within its paradigm before comparing across.

## Kuhn's Lasting Lesson for Rubric Design

A rubric for "good mathematics" cannot deliver **algorithmic verdicts**. It can:

- Score on multiple axes.
- Reveal trade-offs.
- Make **disagreement productive** by locating it on specific axes.
- Acknowledge framework dependencies.

It cannot:

- Combine the axes into a single ranking that all reasonable judges should accept.
- Eliminate the role of taste.
- Resolve framework-level disagreements.

These limits are not failures — they are accurate reflections of how mathematical communities actually work.
