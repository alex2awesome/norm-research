# Avigad: Understanding Proofs — Abilities Behind Comprehension

Avigad's chapter analyzes "understanding a proof" not as a single mental state but as a cluster of demonstrable abilities. This makes it usable as a rubric.

## The Core Move: Understanding = Cluster of Abilities

Where formalists treat proofs as syntactic objects to be checked, Avigad treats understanding as a set of practical capacities the reader can exhibit.

## Abilities Constitutive of Understanding a Proof

A reader who understands a proof can:

1. **Spell out implicit details** — reconstruct the steps the author left to the reader (the standard "exercise" steps).
2. **Identify the main idea** — name the one or two key insights, distinguished from setup and bookkeeping.
3. **Locate the difficulty** — point to the steps that bear the real conceptual load.
4. **Anticipate next steps** — predict where the proof is going from a partial reading.
5. **Recognize role of each lemma** — explain why each lemma is needed and what would fail without it.
6. **Modify the proof** — adapt it to slightly different hypotheses or to a strengthened conclusion.
7. **Translate between presentations** — express the same proof in different notation, level of formality, or style.
8. **Recognize equivalent proofs** — see when two superficially different arguments are "the same proof."
9. **Compare to alternatives** — articulate how this proof differs from or improves upon known alternatives.
10. **Apply the technique** — use the proof's method to attack new problems.
11. **Justify the choice of definitions** — explain why the concepts the proof uses cut the problem at its joints.

## Implications for "Understanding" as a Rubric

A proof's exposition can be **judged by how well it enables the reader to acquire these abilities**. A proof that gives only a syntactic certificate (verifiable but unilluminating) fails the understanding criterion even if it is logically valid.

## The Formal/Informal Distinction

A formal axiomatic derivation "spells out every detail precisely." An ordinary textbook proof "proceeds at a higher level, relying on the reader's ability to *see* that each successive step is warranted."

Good mathematical exposition operates **in the gap**: leaves implicit what the trained reader can fill in, makes explicit what the trained reader cannot. Mismatches in this calibration are exposition failures.

## Implied Rubric for a Proof's Quality

- Does the exposition leave **the right things** implicit (those a competent reader can fill in)?
- Does it make **the right things** explicit (the genuinely hard moves)?
- Can the reader extract the **main idea** as a single sentence?
- Does it support the **modification ability** — could the reader prove a slight variant?
- Does it justify its **choice of definitions**?

## Connection to Computer-Verified Proofs

Avigad notes that a fully formal proof in a system like Coq or Lean is **logically perfect but typically does not yield understanding**. This is empirical evidence that understanding is not reducible to logical correctness — it is an additional dimension that any complete rubric for proof quality must include.
