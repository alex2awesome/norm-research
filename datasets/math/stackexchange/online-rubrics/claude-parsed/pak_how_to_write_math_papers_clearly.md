# How to Write Math Papers Clearly — Igor Pak (Blog post & Notices essay, 2017)

A widely-circulated essay arguing that clarity is paramount in math writing — and offering 12 distinct citation styles as one concrete diagnostic of clarity.

## Core Philosophy
- Math writing serves dual purposes: "recording mathematical content and a means of communication."
- "Clarity suffers the most" under rigid mathematical writing conventions.
- Clarity is paramount and should never be sacrificed for grammar rules.

## Citation Style Spectrum (decreasing reliability)
1. **Direct attribution with source.** "Roth proved Murakami's conjecture in [Roth]."
2. **Name and reference.** "Roth proved Murakami's conjecture [Roth]."
3. **"See" reference.** "see [Roth]" for various source types.
4. **Multiple sources with "see also."** Indicates secondary contributions.
5. **Parenthetical secondary source.** Suggests minor corrections.
6. **Parenthetical primary source.** [Woolf] as definitive version.
7. **Multiple textbooks cited.** "see e.g. [Faulkner, Fitzgerald, Frost]."
8. **Parenthetical multiple citations.** Classical/well-known results.
9. **Footnoted single source.** Author hasn't verified the reference.
10. **Unusual historical sources.** Letters, etc.
11. **Personal communication.** Unverified correspondence claims.
12. **"Claims to have proved."** Indicates known gaps or errors.

## Stance on Grammar
Prioritise clarity over traditional grammar rules — particularly for non-native English speakers.

## Implication for "Good Math"
A clear paper signals its claims precisely: who proved what, where, with what reliability. Each citation choice is itself a load-bearing piece of clarity.
