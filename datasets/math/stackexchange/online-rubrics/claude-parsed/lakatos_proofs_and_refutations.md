# Lakatos: Proofs and Refutations — What Counts as a Proof

Lakatos overturned the formalist picture of mathematics as a static body of theorems. His criteria for what makes a piece of mathematical work good are dynamic and dialectical.

## What Counts as a Proof (Lakatos's Position)

A "proof" in living mathematics is **not a formal derivation in a calculus**. It is a **thought-experiment** that:

- Decomposes a conjecture into sub-conjectures (lemmas).
- Invites criticism of any lemma.
- Improves the theorem statement, the lemmas, and the underlying concepts in response to counterexamples.

A textbook formal proof is the **fossil** of this process; the live proof is the dialectical history.

## The Method: Proofs and Refutations Cycle

Good mathematical work exhibits the following moves:

1. **Naïve conjecture** ("All polyhedra satisfy V−E+F=2").
2. **Proof attempt**: an informal argument, broken into lemmas.
3. **Global counterexample**: an object that satisfies the hypothesis but not the conclusion.
4. **Local counterexample**: an object that breaks one lemma.
5. **Lemma-incorporation**: rewrite the theorem to exclude the counterexample, with the offending lemma promoted to a hypothesis.
6. **Concept-stretching**: redefining a term ("polyhedron") in light of new examples — this is a **legitimate move**, not cheating.
7. **Monster-barring vs. monster-adjusting**: how the community handles awkward cases reveals its mathematical taste.

## Heuristic Rules (Lakatos's prescriptions)

- **Rule 1**: If you have a conjecture, set out to prove it AND to refute it. Inspect both lemmas and possible counterexamples in the same field of vision.
- **Rule 2**: If you have a global counterexample, try to find the **guilty lemma** and either reject it or qualify the theorem.
- **Rule 3**: If you have a local counterexample, check whether it is also global; if not, improve the proof analysis.
- **Rule 4**: If you have a counterexample of any type, try to use it to **stretch the concept** so the theorem survives in deeper form.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Quality

- Does the work **acknowledge the dialectical history** — counterexamples, prior weaker forms, monster-cases?
- Is the theorem statement the **strongest** consistent with the proof, or is it weakened pre-emptively?
- Are the **definitions revised** in light of the proof, rather than fixed in advance?
- Does the proof **expose its lemmas individually** so they can be challenged?
- When unusual cases arise, are they treated honestly (monster-adjusting) rather than hidden by definitional fiat (monster-barring)?

## Anti-Patterns Lakatos Identifies

- **Euclidean / deductivist style**: presenting a proof as if it sprang fully formed, hiding the discovery process.
- **Definitional dogmatism**: refusing to revise definitions when counterexamples arise.
- **Hidden lemmas**: appealing to "obvious" steps that, on inspection, are the actual content of the theorem.
