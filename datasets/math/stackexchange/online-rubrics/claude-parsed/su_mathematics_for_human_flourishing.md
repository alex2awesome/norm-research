# Mathematics for Human Flourishing — Francis Su (2020)

Yale Univ. Press. Su's MAA presidential address turned book. A modern, virtue-ethics rubric for what mathematical practice should do for the practitioner and for the community. Important counterpoint to purely technical rubrics: includes hospitality, justice, equity.

## The five fundamental human desires that math meets

1. **Play** — math as exploration, puzzles, pattern, freedom to invent.
2. **Beauty** — math as aesthetic experience, elegance, surprise, harmony.
3. **Truth** — math as the most reliable way to know.
4. **Justice** — math as the language of fairness (allocations, voting, fairness theorems).
5. **Love** — math as a community practice; collegial, mentoring, generative.

## The 13 virtues that mathematical practice cultivates

(Throughout the chapters; here in the order Su develops them)

1. **Hopefulness** — the conviction that a problem has a solution.
2. **Perseverance** — staying on a hard problem.
3. **Disposition toward beauty** — pursuit of elegance, not just correctness.
4. **Inventiveness** — willingness to create new objects, definitions, methods.
5. **Independence of mind** — not deferring to authority.
6. **Confidence in struggle** — comfort with not yet knowing.
7. **Joyfulness** — finding pleasure in the work.
8. **Thinking for oneself** — re-deriving, not memorizing.
9. **Imagination** — visualizing, playing, hypothesizing.
10. **Circumspection** — checking, doubting, looking again.
11. **Mathematical intuition** — recognizing what is plausible.
12. **Hospitality** — welcoming newcomers; making the field accessible.
13. **Love** — for the work, for the community.

## "Mathematical hospitality" as a rubric

Su's most-cited concept: a piece of mathematical writing or teaching is judged not just on technical merit but on whether it *hosts* readers — whether the author has anticipated the reader's confusion, used inclusive language, provided multiple entry points, given full credit, and made the work accessible to outsiders.

## Specific applications to mathematical writing

A mathematical exposition is "hospitable" when:
1. It begins with a concrete example a non-specialist can follow.
2. It defines every non-standard term on first use.
3. It cites generously, including underrepresented contributors.
4. It admits the messy history of how the result was found.
5. It welcomes alternative perspectives in the prose.
6. It does not use jargon as a gatekeeping device.
7. It offers an "on-ramp" for outsiders before going to the technical core.

## A non-technical evaluative axis

Su explicitly argues that "good mathematics" cannot be measured only by the Hardy/Tao technical criteria. It must also include:
- Whether the work *invites* others into the field.
- Whether the practice exhibits care for the community of practitioners.
- Whether mathematical truth is being used in service of human flourishing.

## Influence

- Adopted as required reading in many graduate math-pedagogy seminars.
- AMS / MAA equity statements draw on Su's framework.
- Often cited in NSF broader-impact statements.
