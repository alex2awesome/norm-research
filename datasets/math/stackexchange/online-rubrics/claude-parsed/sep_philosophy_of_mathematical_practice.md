# SEP: Philosophy of Mathematical Practice — Epistemic Virtues Mathematicians Value

The SEP entry consolidates the epistemic virtues that mathematicians explicitly value in research and exposition. This is one of the most useful single-source rubrics for "good mathematics."

## The Core Epistemic Virtues

Mathematicians value, beyond mere truth and correctness:

1. **Rigor** — conclusions follow from premises through acceptable deductive steps. Standards are **contextual**: differ across subfields and historical periods. "Rigor pluralism" recognizes multiple legitimate models.

2. **Mathematical explanation** — *why*, not just *that*. Mathematicians produce multiple proofs of known theorems specifically to gain explanatory value. The classical distinction: "proof of the fact" vs. "proof of the reasoned fact."

3. **Purity of methods** — proofs using only concepts belonging to relevant disciplinary boundaries.
   - **Geographical purity**: stays within a field (don't use complex analysis for an arithmetic claim if avoidable).
   - **Topical purity**: uses only concepts in the theorem's statement.

4. **Visualization / diagrammatic reasoning** — diagrams fulfill epistemic functions that text alone cannot. A proof that suppresses an available diagram for ideological reasons may be inferior, even if formally correct.

5. **Depth** — non-obvious, non-routine, requiring genuine ideas (cf. Gowers).

6. **Simplicity** — minimal apparatus.

7. **Ontological parsimony** — minimal commitment to existence claims.

8. **Epistemic economy** — minimal background knowledge required of the reader.

9. **Fruitfulness** — generates further results, opens new directions.

10. **Beauty** — see Inglis-Aberdein, Zeki et al. on the empirical reality of this judgment.

11. **Generalizability** — the proof's method extends to other cases.

## Methodological Frame

A piece of mathematics should be evaluated:

- **Bottom-up**, by detailed analysis of its actual structure, not top-down from formal principles.
- **As situated practice**, recognizing that mathematicians are cognitively bounded, work collaboratively, rely on testimony, negotiate community standards.
- **With pluralism**: different proofs of the same theorem can each have distinct virtues; ranking them on a single axis loses information.

## Product vs. Process Distinction

A mathematical contribution has two aspects:

- **Product**: the finished proof, theory, or theorem (judged by rigor, depth, beauty, etc.).
- **Process**: the activity that produced it (judged by reasoning style, use of diagrams, collaboration, conjecture-and-refutation cycle).

A complete evaluation considers both.

## Implied Rubric (operational)

For each proof, score on the eleven virtues above:

- Rigor (contextually appropriate)
- Explanatory power
- Purity of method
- Use of visualization where helpful
- Depth (non-routine ideas)
- Simplicity
- Ontological/epistemic economy
- Fruitfulness
- Beauty
- Generalizability

Plus process virtues:
- Did it engage with counterexamples? (Lakatos)
- Did it survey alternatives? (Avigad)
- Was its style appropriate to the audience? (Tappenden)

## On "Rigor Pluralism"

The entry emphasizes that **there is no single canonical standard of rigor** even among research mathematicians. Different subfields apply different standards. A rubric that uses a single rigor scale will mis-evaluate work that meets one subfield's standards but not another's. The right question is not "is this rigorous?" but "is this rigorous *for its subfield*?"
