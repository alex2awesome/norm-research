# Halmos: I Want to Be a Mathematician — Style and Practice

Halmos's "automathography" contains scattered passages that serve as practical criteria for mathematical work, reading, and writing. (Note: a separate file already covers Halmos's "How to Write Mathematics." This file is the autobiography-derived material.)

## The Active-Reading Imperative

> "Don't just read it; fight it! Ask your own questions, look for your own examples, discover your own proofs. Is the hypothesis necessary? Is the converse true? What happens in the classical special case? What about the degenerate cases? Where does the proof use the hypothesis?"

This is a six-question rubric for **reading a mathematical text**:

1. **Is the hypothesis necessary?** (i.e., can it be weakened?)
2. **Is the converse true?** (a check on what the result really says)
3. **What happens in the classical special case?** (does it reduce to something familiar?)
4. **What about the degenerate cases?** (does the result fail there, and why?)
5. **Where does the proof use the hypothesis?** (locates the load-bearing step)
6. **Construct your own examples and proofs** (active engagement)

A reader who cannot answer these has not understood. By symmetry, a piece of mathematics that *prevents* the reader from answering these is poorly written.

## The "Say Something" Principle

> "To have something to say is by far the most important ingredient of good exposition."

Halmos's first criterion for exposition: **substance**. No technical polish substitutes for actually having a non-trivial point. Many mathematically correct papers fail this criterion: they prove something but say nothing.

## The Spiral-Writing Method

Halmos describes his own practice: write a section, set it aside, return to it, rewrite it again, often many times. **Quality emerges from re-writing**, not from first-draft fluency.

A rubric implication: **a piece of mathematical writing should show signs of revision** — coherent organization, no false starts in the visible text, terminology stable across sections.

## Minimum-Resistance Organization

> Organize material "to minimize the resistance and maximize the insight of the reader."

This is operational. For each presentational choice, ask:

- **Does this ordering** make the reader's path easier or harder?
- **Does this notation** require effort proportional to its payoff?
- **Does this proof structure** reveal the idea or hide it?

## On the Apprenticeship Model

Halmos argues mathematics is learned by **doing alongside a master**. This implies that good exposition acts *as* a master would: it doesn't merely present results but **guides the reader through the moves**, including motivation and pitfall-avoidance.

## On Lecturing

Halmos emphasizes that a lecture is **not a proof** — it is preparation for the listener to study the proof later. Therefore:

- A lecture should give the **main idea**, not every detail.
- It should **convince** that the proof works, not deliver the proof.
- It should leave the listener **prepared to fill in details on their own**.

## Implied Rubric for Mathematical Work (combining criteria)

A piece of mathematical exposition is good to the extent that it:

- **Has something to say** (substance).
- **Survives the six-question test** (reader can ask Halmos's six questions and find answers).
- **Shows evidence of revision** (no first-draft sprawl).
- **Minimizes reader resistance** (organization helps, doesn't fight).
- **Acts like a master apprenticing the reader** (motivation, pitfalls, choices made explicit).
- **Distinguishes lecture from proof** (knows what register it is in).

## Anti-Patterns Halmos Critiques (from various passages)

- **Pretentious rigor** without substance.
- **Order-of-discovery** organization (presenting a proof in the order it was found, not the order that helps the reader).
- **Notation overload** that adds nothing.
- **Reverence for definitions** without motivation.
- **Mistaking length for depth**.

## On Mathematical Style

Halmos famously preferred **simple, direct, conversational prose**. The best mathematical writing reads like a thoughtful person explaining something they care about, not like a formal document.
