# A Primer of Mathematical Writing — Steven G. Krantz (1996, 2nd ed. 2017)

AMS, MBK/112. The most comprehensive single-author handbook for mathematical writing across all genres a mathematician produces (papers, talks, grants, theses, letters, referee reports).

## Topics covered

1. **The writing process**
   - Identifying your audience.
   - Outlining; the role of the table of contents.
   - First draft / revision cycle.
2. **Diction**
   - Careful use of words.
   - Corruption of language ("nice", "interesting", "very", "essentially").
   - Accuracy in writing.
   - Singular vs. plural.
   - Pronouns and antecedents.
   - Acronyms — define on first use; never coin gratuitously.
   - Jargon — distinguish technical terms from in-group slang.
3. **Effective use of notation**
   - Mnemonic letters.
   - Consistency throughout the paper.
   - Don't reuse a symbol; don't double-use a letter.
   - Subscript and superscript discipline.
4. **Sentence and paragraph structure**
   - Active voice preferred.
   - Avoid the "we will see" / "as we shall see" filler.
   - Paragraph = one idea.
   - Topic sentence first.
5. **Grammar and syntax**
   - Subject-verb agreement.
   - Comma overuse and underuse.
   - Parallel construction.
   - Restrictive vs. non-restrictive clauses.
6. **Mathematical specifics**
   - Theorem environments: theorem, lemma, proposition, corollary, definition.
   - When to display vs. inline a formula.
   - Punctuation of displayed formulas.
   - Numbering schemes.
7. **Proofreading**
   - Read aloud; read backwards; have a colleague read.
   - The role of the copyeditor.
8. **TeX and computer tools**
   - Why TeX.
   - Common TeX errors that propagate to readers.
9. **The genre catalogue** (Krantz's distinguishing contribution — guidance for each)
   - Research paper.
   - Survey article.
   - Opinion piece.
   - Book review.
   - Referee report.
   - Letter of recommendation.
   - Grant proposal.
   - CV / job application.
   - Talk / colloquium.
   - Thesis.
   - Email.
10. **Publishing process**
    - Choosing a journal.
    - Dealing with referees.
    - Galley proofs and indices.
    - Copyright.

## Krantz's general principles (distilled)

1. **The reader is your guest**, not your adversary.
2. **Write for a specific reader.** Picture them.
3. **Cut everything that does not serve the reader.**
4. **Words before symbols.**
5. **Motivate every definition.**
6. **State every theorem in full** before you prove it.
7. **Examples first, then abstraction.**
8. **Use the present tense for permanent mathematical content.**
9. **Avoid "I" and "one"; use "we" sparingly.**
10. **Cite generously; plagiarize never.**
11. **Acknowledge contributions from colleagues, students, referees.**
12. **Revise. Then revise again. Then once more.**
13. **The first sentence is the most important.**
14. **The last sentence is the second most important.**
15. **Never publish a draft.**

## On letters of recommendation

Krantz devotes a chapter to the rhetoric of recommendation letters: what hiring committees actually parse, the meaning of standard phrases, the dangers of damning with faint praise.

## On referee reports

A good report is short, specific, and constructive. Begin with a summary of the paper as the referee understands it. Then list errors. Then list suggestions. End with a recommendation.

## On grant proposals

The first paragraph must answer: what problem, why important, why now, why you, what success looks like. Cut buzzwords.

## Influence

Standard reference for graduate students and early-career mathematicians; widely assigned alongside Higham's SIAM handbook.
