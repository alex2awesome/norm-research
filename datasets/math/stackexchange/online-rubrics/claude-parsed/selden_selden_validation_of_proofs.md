# Selden & Selden: Validation of Proofs Considered as Texts

A foundational empirical study in mathematics education on **how readers determine whether an argument constitutes a proof**, and the dimensions involved in proof validation.

## The Core Distinction

The Seldens distinguish two activities:

1. **Proof construction**: producing an argument.
2. **Proof validation**: judging whether a given argument is a valid proof.

These are distinct skills. A reader may follow a proof line by line yet be unable to assess whether it is genuinely a proof.

## Validation as a Reading Activity

Validation is a **reflective reading practice** that includes:

- Pausing at each step to ask "is this step justified by the previous lines?"
- Identifying implicit assumptions
- Checking that what is claimed is what was to be proved
- Tracking variable scopes and quantifier order
- Detecting circular reasoning
- Recognizing whether each lemma is correctly applied

## Empirical Finding: Surface vs. Deep Reading

Undergraduates **focus on surface features**:

- The presence of standard proof markers ("Proof.", "QED")
- The visual/typographic conventions of textbook proofs
- The presence of formal symbols
- Length (longer = more authoritative)

They are **less able to detect**:

- Circular arguments
- Affirming-the-consequent errors
- Mismatched quantifiers
- Cases that should have been considered but were not
- Whether the argument actually establishes the claim

## Eight Validation Behaviors (Selden & Selden expanded framework)

In their later work, the Seldens identify validation behaviors:

1. **First-pass reading** to get the gist
2. **Re-reading** with attention to logical structure
3. **Asking and answering self-questions** at each step
4. **Filling in implicit details mentally**
5. **Producing sub-proofs** for steps in doubt
6. **Constructing examples** to test the claim
7. **Constructing counterexamples** to test the claim
8. **Comparing the claim to what was proved** (do they actually match?)

## Implied Rubric for Both Proof and Validation

A proof can be evaluated for whether it **supports validation**:

- Are quantifiers explicit and in clear scope?
- Is the conclusion stated identically to the theorem?
- Are standard markers ("we want to show," "by hypothesis," "QED") used?
- Are case enumerations complete?
- Are the lemmas used named or referenced?

A reader's understanding can be evaluated for **validation skill**:

- Can the reader detect a planted error?
- Can the reader identify the load-bearing step?
- Can the reader produce a counterexample to a flawed variant?
- Can the reader articulate why each step is justified?

## Implications for AI Evaluation

This work suggests that **automatic proof checking** (formal verification) and **proof understanding** are different things, just as Avigad argues. Surface-feature recognition (looks-like-a-proof) is much easier than deep validation (actually-is-a-proof). A complete rubric must distinguish them.
