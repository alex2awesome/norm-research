# Proof Writing — Art of Problem Solving (AoPS) Wiki

Community-curated reference page used by olympiad and pre-olympiad students.

## Fundamental Principles

1. **Precision.** "A single word can change the intended meaning of a proof, so it is best to be as precise as possible."
2. **Accuracy.** Distinct from precision; the proof must say true things.
3. **Clarity.** Readable to the intended audience.
4. **Abstract thinking.** "Proofs require the ability to think abstractly, that is, universally" — not just for the example at hand.

## Proof Types and Methods

5. **Formal vs. informal.** University-level mathematicians do not accept informal proofs as valid; informal proofs serve as teaching tools at best.
6. **Two-column format.** Standard for early geometry proofs.
7. **Construction / induction.** One of two predominant formal methods at advanced levels.
8. **Contradiction.** The other major formal method, requiring derivation of a contradiction.

## Practice Recommendations

9. **Use AoPS forums** to practice writing solutions with clarity and completeness.
10. **Read other students' solutions** to identify improvements in your own.
11. **Accept constructive criticism** from peers.
12. **Contribute to mathematical wikis** to develop precise mathematical writing.
13. **Study Olympiad problems** for advanced practice.

## Implication for "Good Math Writing"
For competition math, the rubric is heavily weighted toward precision + universality + correctness; literary value is largely orthogonal. This forms one pole of the math-writing rubric spectrum.
