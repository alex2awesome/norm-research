# Greiffenhagen: Checking Correctness in Mathematical Peer Review

A landmark sociological study (95 interviews with mathematics journal editors, plus 100+ referee reports) on what referees actually do when checking proofs. Provides an empirical rubric for *how* mathematical work is judged in practice.

## The Two-Stage Reviewing System (Elite Journals)

Many top mathematics journals use a two-stage process:

1. **Significance check** — a senior mathematician gives a "quick opinion" on whether the work is important. (See the companion paper for criteria.)
2. **Correctness check** — only if stage 1 is positive, a younger referee does the technical verification.

This separates **importance** from **correctness** — they are independently evaluated dimensions, not a single "quality."

## What Referees Look For (the operational rubric)

Referees do not read line-by-line. They:

1. **Identify the skeleton** — the proof's main structure and key steps.
2. **Locate the turning points** — places where the proof's hardest reasoning occurs.
3. **Scan for suspicious elements**:
   - Results that seem "too strong" (claim more than the technique supports).
   - Techniques that look insufficient for claimed conclusions.
   - Shifts in writing quality (a sloppy paragraph in an otherwise clean proof signals trouble).
   - Inconsistencies in notation across sections.
4. **Spot-check key passages** in detail.
5. **Trust routine portions** to which standard techniques apply.

## What Gets Checked vs. Trusted

### Checked in detail
- The "load-bearing" steps where the proof's hardest argument lies.
- Steps where the conclusion seems implausibly strong.
- Areas outside the author's known expertise.
- Sections with inconsistent presentation quality.
- Steps where intuition or community consensus would push the other way.

### Trusted without full verification
- Routine technical steps that are "standard" in the field.
- Work by established authors with track records.
- Sections the referee judges plausible from field knowledge.
- Calculations that "could be checked but would not change the result."

## Plausibility Checks

The dominant verification mode is the **plausibility check**: confirming the overall argument structure is sound and the result aligns with established knowledge in the subfield. The goal is to be **"reasonably convinced,"** not absolutely certain.

This is consistent with Andersen's "acceptable gaps": the practice is *socially structured trust*, not formal verification.

## Tier Sensitivity

Standards vary substantially between journals:
- **Elite journals**: more rigorous scrutiny, often double-checking by editor as well.
- **Mid-tier journals**: rely heavily on referee judgment and author reputation.
- **Lower-tier journals**: minimal correctness checking; emphasis on "no obvious errors."

A complete rubric for "rigorous proof" must therefore specify the **tier of expectation**.

## Implicit Rubric Dimensions Referees Apply

From the interviews, referees implicitly score papers on:

1. **Author reputation** (track record of correct proofs)
2. **Result reasonableness** (does the conclusion fit the field's expectations?)
3. **Technical coherence** (can the referee follow the logical flow?)
4. **Presentation quality** (does writing rigor match claimed difficulty?)
5. **Community context** (what do other experts in this subfield know?)
6. **Scope of new technique** (is the method clearly within the author's competence?)
7. **Provenance of claimed lemmas** (are cited results well-established?)
8. **Consistency** (do all the parts of the paper agree with each other?)

## Implications for Rubric Design

A complete rubric for "is this proof correct" should include:

- A **tier of expectation** (elite vs. routine).
- **Author-trust signals** as a legitimate input.
- **Identification of load-bearing steps** as the focus of detailed checking.
- **Plausibility-with-the-field** as a primary criterion (not isolated verification).
- **Consistency checks** across sections.
- An honest acknowledgment that **certainty is not the standard** — reasonable conviction is.

## What This Means Philosophically

Greiffenhagen's findings reinforce the Andersen / Lakatos picture: **mathematical certainty is socially distributed**, not a property of texts. Two consequences:

1. The same proof could be "correct" in one community and "doubtful" in another (different bases of trust).
2. **Verification by AI** does not replace community verification — it adds a new dimension that the community will then need to integrate.

## Anti-Patterns Detectable in Peer-Review Practice

A paper raises red flags when:
- Writing quality is **inconsistent across sections**.
- The result is **stronger than the technique seems to support**.
- The **load-bearing step is hidden** in routine-looking notation.
- The author lacks track record in the **specific technique** used.
- The proof **lacks any signposts** of where the difficulty lies.
