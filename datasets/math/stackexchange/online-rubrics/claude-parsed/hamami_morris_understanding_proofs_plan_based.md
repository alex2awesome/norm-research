# Hamami & Morris: Understanding Proofs as Plan Reconstruction

Hamami and Morris develop a plan-based account of what it means to understand a proof. This complements Avigad's abilities-based account and offers a more procedurally explicit rubric.

## Core Thesis

> **Understanding a proof = rationally reconstructing the underlying plan** that the prover executed.

Proofs are not free-form deductions; they are the surface-level expression of a structured plan with sub-goals, sub-strategies, and orchestration. A reader understands when they can recover this plan from the linear text.

## The Bratman Background

Hamami & Morris build on Michael Bratman's planning theory of agency:

- A **plan** is a partial mental representation that organizes activity over time.
- Plans have a **hierarchical structure**: goals, sub-goals, means, sub-means.
- Plans are **stable** (resist revision without reason) yet **revisable** (under new information).
- Plans support **coordination** with other agents (collaborators, future readers).

A mathematical proof embodies a plan in this sense; understanding the proof means recovering the plan.

## Components of a Proof Plan

To understand a proof, the reader must identify:

1. **The overall goal** (the theorem statement).
2. **Major sub-goals** (the lemmas, the cases, the constructions).
3. **The strategy linking sub-goals to the goal** (e.g., "we will reduce to the compact case, then use Stone-Weierstrass").
4. **Tactical choices** at each sub-goal (e.g., "we induct on the dimension because the statement is dimension-stable").
5. **Failure points avoided** (the route was chosen because the obvious alternatives don't work).
6. **The role of each step** in advancing the plan (no decorative steps).

A reader who can list these components understands; one who cannot, does not.

## Partial / Graduated Understanding

Hamami & Morris emphasize: **understanding is graded**. A reader can:

- Have **no plan reconstruction** (only a syntactic check).
- Have a **partial plan** (top-level goal but not sub-strategies).
- Have a **full plan** but no ability to **modify** it.
- Have a **modifiable plan** that supports adaptation to variants.

A rubric for understanding can place readers (or generated proofs) on this gradient.

## The Rationality Constraint

The plan must be **rationally reconstructed** — the reader must see *why* each choice was made, not just *what* was done. This rules out:

- Memorizing the proof without understanding why each step was chosen.
- Following a script without grasping the alternatives that were rejected.
- Recognizing the steps without grasping their integration into the plan.

## Implied Rubric for Proof Quality (from the writer's side)

A proof exposition supports plan-recovery when it:

- **States the strategy** at the top (e.g., "the proof proceeds in three stages").
- **Names sub-goals** as they arise (e.g., "we now establish the key lemma that...").
- **Justifies tactical choices** (e.g., "we induct on $n$ rather than $m$ because...").
- **Acknowledges abandoned routes** (e.g., "the obvious approach via X fails because...").
- **Connects each step** to its role in the plan, not as an isolated computation.

A proof exposition obstructs plan-recovery when it:

- **Hides the strategy** — proceeds step by step with no overview.
- **Conceals motivations** — says what without saying why.
- **Treats sub-goals as deus ex machina** — produces lemmas with no apparent motivation.
- **Mixes plan-level and tactical-level reasoning** at the same level of detail.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Avigad's abilities** are largely *what a reader can do* once the plan is recovered.
- **Lakatos's dialectic** is the plan-as-it-developed (with monsters and lemma-incorporation), vs. the polished plan-as-presented.
- **Polya's heuristics** are templates for plan-construction.

## Implications for AI / Auto-Generated Proofs

A proof generated by a system that has no plan (only a search tree of valid steps) may be:

- Logically correct
- Syntactically well-formed
- And yet **unintelligible** because no plan was ever formulated

This identifies a specific gap in machine-generated proofs: they may pass formal verification but fail the plan-reconstruction test.
