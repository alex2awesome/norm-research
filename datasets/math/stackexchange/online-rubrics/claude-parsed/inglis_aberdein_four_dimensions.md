# Inglis & Aberdein: Four Empirical Dimensions of Proof Appraisal

This is the first major empirical study to derive — by factor analysis of working mathematicians' judgments — the dimensions on which proofs are actually evaluated.

## Method

Mathematicians were given proofs and asked to rate them against many adjectives. Factor analysis on the responses produced a small number of underlying dimensions.

## The Four Dimensions of Proof Appraisal

A proof is judged along **four roughly orthogonal dimensions**:

1. **Aesthetics** — beauty, elegance, profundity. Adjectives loading here include: *beautiful, elegant, profound, inspired*.
2. **Intricacy** — complexity, difficulty of execution. Adjectives: *intricate, complicated, dense, sophisticated*.
3. **Utility** — usefulness, generative power. Adjectives: *useful, applicable, fruitful, informative*.
4. **Precision** — rigor, careful execution, correctness. Adjectives: *precise, rigorous, meticulous, careful*.

## Headline Empirical Finding

> **Beauty and simplicity are almost entirely unrelated.**

The classical Hardy/Birkhoff doctrine that beauty equals simplicity is **empirically false**. Mathematicians can find a proof beautiful even when it is intricate, and a simple proof need not be beautiful.

## Follow-up Findings

- **Aesthetic and utility judgments are positively correlated** (a "useful" proof is more likely to be called beautiful) — supporting Poincaré/von Neumann over the pure-aesthetic camp.
- **Aesthetic judgments are subject to social conformity** (Inglis & Aberdein 2020). When mathematicians know what others have said, their own judgments shift. This complicates any "pure aesthetic perception" view.
- **Expertise matters**: novice and expert ratings of equation aesthetics differ; expertise reshapes aesthetic perception.

## Implied Rubric for Evaluating a Proof

A complete evaluation needs **separate ratings on all four axes**. A proof can be:

- High aesthetics, low intricacy: a Hardy-style "elegant" proof.
- High aesthetics, high intricacy: an "inspired tour-de-force" (e.g., Wiles).
- Low aesthetics, high utility: a "workhorse" proof.
- High precision, low aesthetics: a careful technical lemma.

Collapsing these into one "good proof" score loses information.

## Adjectival Rating Scale (Inglis-Aberdein "personality" scale)

A short 16-adjective instrument: **four adjectives per dimension** with high internal reliability. This provides a validated rubric for empirical proof evaluation.

## Implications for AI Evaluation of Math

- Use **multiple axes**, not a single quality score.
- Do not assume **simplicity = beauty**.
- Track **utility separately** from aesthetics.
- Distinguish **rigor (precision)** from **insight (aesthetics)** from **virtuosity (intricacy)**.
