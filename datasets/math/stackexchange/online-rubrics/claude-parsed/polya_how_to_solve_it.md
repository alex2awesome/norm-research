# How to Solve It — George Pólya (1945)

The most influential treatment of mathematical problem-solving heuristics. Pólya's framework underlies essentially every later "guide to solving math problems" and "guide to writing solutions".

## The four-step method

### Step 1. Understand the problem
- What is the unknown?
- What are the data?
- What is the condition?
- Is it possible to satisfy the condition?
- Is the condition sufficient to determine the unknown? Insufficient? Redundant? Contradictory?
- Draw a figure. Introduce suitable notation.
- Separate the various parts of the condition.

### Step 2. Devise a plan
- Have you seen this problem before? In a slightly different form?
- Do you know a related problem? Could you use it?
- Could you restate the problem? More generally? More specifically? Analogously?
- If you cannot solve the problem, can you solve a related, easier problem? Part of the problem? A more general problem? A more special case?
- Did you use all the data? All the conditions?

### Step 3. Carry out the plan
- Check each step: can you see clearly that the step is correct? Can you prove it is correct?

### Step 4. Look back
- Examine the solution.
- Can you check the result? Can you check the argument?
- Can you derive the result differently? Can you see it at a glance?
- Can you use the result, or the method, for some other problem?

## Pólya's heuristics for "Devise a plan" (the famous list)

1. **Analogy** — find a similar problem.
2. **Generalization** — solve a problem of which yours is a special case.
3. **Specialization** — try a small / extreme / boundary case first.
4. **Decomposition** — break into sub-problems.
5. **Recombination** — assemble pieces of known results.
6. **Working backwards** — start from the desired conclusion and reason to the hypotheses.
7. **Auxiliary problem** — invent a related problem easier to solve.
8. **Auxiliary element** — add a construction (extra line in a figure, extra variable in an equation).
9. **Vary the problem** — relax a condition; tighten a condition.
10. **Symmetry** — exploit it; impose it.

## Pólya's implicit rubric for a "good solution"

A solution is good when, after the four steps:
- The reader understands not just *that* the result is true but *how it was found*.
- The method generalizes to neighboring problems.
- The "Look back" step has been visibly carried out — alternative paths considered, the result placed in context.
- The exposition mirrors the discovery (Pólya is opposed to "fully polished" presentations that hide the underlying ideas).

## Influence

Pólya's framework is the basis of:
- Schoenfeld's *Mathematical Problem Solving*.
- The MAA Putnam preparation tradition.
- Singapore-method primary mathematics.
- Most "problem-solving" undergraduate courses worldwide.
- Modern AI work on LLM reasoning chains, which often explicitly references Pólya.
