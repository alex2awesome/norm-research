# Mejía-Ramos & Weber: How Mathematicians Read Proofs

A two-study program (interviews with 9 mathematicians + survey of 118) on the actual cognitive practices of mathematicians reading published proofs. Provides an empirical rubric for what proof reading looks like and what proofs need to support.

## What Mathematicians Actually Do When Reading

The study debunks the formalist picture (read line by line, verify each step). Mathematicians read for different goals and use different strategies.

## The Three Reading Strategies

Mathematicians employ three general strategies:

1. **Authority appeal** — relying on the reputation of the author and the journal as evidence of correctness. The reader does not verify but accepts.
2. **Line-by-line reading** — checking each step against the previous ones (the formalist ideal, but rare in practice).
3. **Modular reading** — verifying that each section accomplishes its stated purpose, treating sections as black boxes whose internal details are skimmed.

## What Mathematicians Read Proofs FOR (Goals)

> Mathematicians read published proofs to gain different types of insight, **not primarily to check correctness**.

Specifically, they seek:

1. **Understanding the main idea** — what is the key insight?
2. **Understanding the technique** — what method is being used, and how?
3. **Identifying reusable lemmas** — what intermediate results might be useful elsewhere?
4. **Locating the load-bearing step** — where is the real difficulty?
5. **Comparing to alternatives** — how does this proof differ from known approaches?
6. **Extracting heuristics** — what general strategy does the proof embody?
7. **Adapting to new problems** — could this method work on a related problem?

## Specific Reading Behaviors (Survey Findings)

When reading proofs, mathematicians commonly:

- **Appeal to author/journal reputation** as a shortcut to trust.
- **Study how steps apply to specific examples** they have in mind.
- **Focus on overarching ideas and methods** rather than line details.
- **Shift between zoomed-out and zoomed-in views** (global ↔ local).
- **Engage in non-deductive reasoning** at every level (analogy, plausibility, "this should work because...").

## The Global ↔ Local Oscillation

A key empirical finding: mathematicians do not read top-to-bottom. They:

- **Skim for the big picture** first.
- **Zoom in on specific steps** that look interesting or suspicious.
- **Zoom out** when they get the local detail.
- **Re-skim with the new local insight** to update the global view.
- This **oscillation continues** through the reading.

A proof that supports this reading style scores higher than one that requires linear reading.

## Implied Rubric for Proof Quality (from the reader's needs)

A proof scores higher when it supports the goals mathematicians actually have:

- **States its main idea clearly** at the top (helps "understanding the main idea").
- **Names its technique** explicitly (helps "understanding the technique").
- **Highlights reusable lemmas** (helps "identifying reusable lemmas").
- **Marks the load-bearing step** (helps "locating the difficulty").
- **Acknowledges alternatives** (helps "comparing to alternatives").
- **Extracts the heuristic principle** (helps "extracting heuristics").
- **Discusses extensions and limits** (helps "adapting to new problems").
- **Has clear modular structure** (supports modular reading).
- **Has scannable section headings and signposts** (supports the global skim).
- **Has worked-example sections or remarks** (supports the local-example check).

A proof scores lower when it:

- Hides the main idea in midstream.
- Buries the technique inside calculations.
- Has no clear modular structure (forces line-by-line reading).
- Provides no signposts for skimming.
- Has no examples that show the method in action.

## On Author/Journal Authority

The fact that mathematicians **legitimately** rely on author reputation is empirically documented. This means:

- A rubric that pretends every proof must be evaluable independently of authorship is unrealistic.
- Conversely, a proof from an author with no reputation must be **more self-supporting** than one from an established author.
- The community's distribution of trust is part of the rubric, not a corruption of it.

## Skimming as a Method

Subsequent debate (Inglis & Alcock vs. Weber & Mejía-Ramos) established that **skimming is a primary mode of mathematical reading**, not a deficient form. A proof must be:

- **Skim-friendly** at the section/headline level.
- **Detail-rich** at the points where the reader chooses to zoom in.
- **Honest about which sections are routine** vs. critical.

## Anti-Patterns Detectable from the Reader's Side

A proof creates problems for readers when it:

- Has **no main idea statement** — the reader cannot extract the core insight.
- Has **homogeneous detail throughout** — the reader cannot tell which sections matter.
- Has **no signposts** — the reader cannot skim.
- Has **no example applications** — the reader cannot test understanding.
- Has **misleading section structure** — modular reading produces a false picture.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **Avigad's "abilities"**: this study identifies which abilities readers actually exercise.
- **Hamami & Morris (plan reconstruction)**: the reader's reconstruction is plan-recovery.
- **Selden & Selden (validation)**: validation is one specific goal among many; not the dominant one in research reading.
