Guidelines for Articles in the AMS Notices

Introduction

The Notices is the primary journal of record of your professional organization, and the AMS's membership wants to learn about topics that interest you. Writing for the Notices is your opportunity to reach a large audience, beyond the handful of readers of a typical journal research article. And even if you do not have the time or inclination to write something now, file the idea away for the future, while encouraging colleagues to share their interests with the wider mathematical community.

The Notices publishes many different types of articles; choose the one that best fits your story. Just keep in mind the following advice for writing a successful Notices articles:

Explain why your topic is fascinating.

Make your article a pleasure to read.

This page contains suggestions and guidelines to make your authoring job easier, including a description of the various types of articles that appear in the Notices.

General Guidelines

We start with general guidelines that apply to all articles in the Notices, and indeed many of these suggestions can be fruitfully applied to all mathematical writing.

  • You are writing for the general AMS membership, which is broader than you might think. Your audience is not colleagues who work in your field. Hook the non-specialists, who are eager to learn, by making your article comprehensible and compelling.
  • The first few paragraphs of your article should achieve two goals.
    • For the (many) readers who will not read past the introduction, leave them with answers to the following questions: What is your article about? Where is your subject coming from, where is it now, and where might it be going? Why is your topic interesting and exciting?
    • Convince the reader to continue past the introduction and get into the heart of your article. This is your chance to sell your topic — take advantage of the opportunity!
  • The majority of your readers will not read to the end, so do not hide your punchline there. Think more "newspaper article" and less "murder mystery." Describe the sizzle in the introduction, even if details only come later in the article.
  • Some key principles for you as an author:

    Know what you want to say.

    Understand your audience.

    Think and write clearly.

    Repeatedly read and polish.

  • Many people find that reading aloud helps improve readability, especially for complicated sentences or paragraphs
  • Avoid using jargon that is familiar to people in your field, but is not standard terminology for the general mathematical community.
  • Avoid phrases such as "Recall that X" or "It is well known that X," since they signal to the reader who has never run across "X" that there is little hope of understanding the rest of your article. Similarly, do not say things like "Clearly X is true," since if "X" is not immediately clear to the reader, then you've lost them.

Guidelines by Article Type

Articles in the Notices typically fall into one of the categories listed in the table here. We provide specific guidelines targeted to each type of article.

Research Feature Articles

As the name suggests, research feature articles describe interesting areas of current mathematical research. They should include an appropriate level of detail to be largely comprehensible to most AMS members, while also holding the interest of those with more background.

  • It is especially important to start gently for the non-specialists, since they form the bulk of the Notices' readership. The target audience for the introduction is (more or less) mathematicians who have never taken a graduate course in your specialty.
  • The first couple of paragraphs should sell your story, and at least the first couple of pages should be intelligible to the general AMS member.
  • The target length for feature articles is 8–12 pages, with 15 pages an absolute upper limit. Articles should include no more than 20 references that guide the reader to the literature, although with prior permission of the editor, survey articles may have a more comprehensive bibliography.

See the Features Collection.

Community Feature Articles

Articles concerning professional and social issues should be of interest to a substantial fraction of the AMS membership and should be written in a scholarly tone. It is inappropriate to use inflammatory language, sweeping generalizations unsupported by evidence, or personal attacks. However, well-argued and respectfully presented opinions on important issues that some may find controversial are welcome.

  • The target length for feature articles is 8–12 pages, with 15 pages an absolute upper limit, and no more than 20 references without the express permission of the editor.

Section top ^

Education Articles

Articles on education include research on educational methods, policy, and outcomes, descriptions of innovative educational techniques, and more generally, reports and analyses of activities that impact mathematical learning

  • The target length for education articles is 5–10 pages. There should generally be no more than 20 references; any excess will need justification and permission of the editor.

"What is…?" Articles

"What is…?" articles aim to briefly explain important mathematical concepts or topics to a broad mathematical audience. Clarity, an engaging presentation, and a sharp focus on conveying the significance of the subject matter are essential. A key to writing a good "What is…?" article is to aim for the shortest path that answers two questions:

What is X? Why is X Interesting?

See the Collection of "What is…?" articles, which should be 2–4 pages in length with a very short list of references, preferably at most 5.

Section top ^

Book Reviews

The Notices has a one-page Bookshelf Column wherein each month an associate editor reviews a couple of books. In addition, the Notices publishes longer submitted reviews of books of general interest. Such reviews include background material that puts the topic into context, as well as a detailed description of the book. They are typically 3–5 pages.

See the Reviews Collection.

Conversation Articles

The Notices features two types of conversation articles. The first is literally a conversation, consisting of an edited transcript of an interview or panel discussion. The second gives the author an opportunity to initiate a conversation within the mathematical community on a topic of interest.

  • For interviews and panel discussions, the author/interviewer's task is to use their raw source material and turn it into a compelling narrative.
  • Most conversation articles should be 3–5 pages in length, and they should have at most a small number of references.

Section top ^

Tribute/Memorial Articles

The AMS publishes short memorial articles that celebrate the lives, careers, and accomplishments of notable individuals from the mathematical community.

  • The primary aim of a memorial article is to tell a life story, and thus to inform the Notices' readership about this wonderful person and their contributions to our mathematical community.
  • Memorial articles should be 3–5 pages in length, with rare exceptions by permission of the editor.
  • Memorial articles should be written by one or two primary authors, who are responsible for gathering and organizing material from a wider cohort of colleagues, friends, and families.
  • A memorial article tells a life story and describes contributions to the mathematical community and/or contributions to mathematical research. Mathematical material should be aimed primarily at a general mathematical audience, with possibly a few paragraphs of a more technical nature aimed at people working in the field.
  • Memorial articles should include some pictures that illustrate the person's life and work.
  • Interesting reminiscences and quotes from friends and colleagues are encouraged, but they should be incorporated into the article, each should serve a purpose, and they should not be repetitive. The focus of reminisences should be on the person being memorialized.
  • In particular, memorial articles in the Notices should not be a compilation of eulogies or personal reminiscences. Phrases such as "X was a wonderful person, X had a great influence on my career, I remember when I first met X, I will miss X very much, etc." should not be included. The focus of the article is on X, not on X's friends and colleagues.
  • For mathematicians whose work had, and continues to have, far-reaching and long-lasting impacts, the Notices will consider publishing a companion article (possibly in a later issue) entitled "The Mathematical Legacy of X." Such articles should be 5–7 pages, with a significant portion accessible to a general mathematical audience. Authors who want to propose a Mathematical Legacy article should first consult with the editor-in-chief [email protected]. The Notices has a limited page budget and there are many calls on those pages, so not all such articles will be approved.

See the Memorials Collection.

Section top ^

History Articles

Articles on the history of mathematics should be written to interest a substantial proportion of readers of the Notices. They may cover any historical period, any geographical area, and any mathematical discipline. While not research articles, they should ideally be based on current or recent research. Topics could include internal mathematical developments, external analyses of such developments, biographical accounts, descriptions of developments within specific periods, special issues related to mathematics, or accounts of events that affected the evolution of mathematics.

  • Try to avoid technical language that is familiar to historians of mathematics, but is not standard terminology for the general (mathematical) community.
  • The target length for history articles is 8–10 pages, with 15 pages as an absolute upper limit. There should generally be no more than 20 references; any excess will need justification and permission of the editor.

Section top ^

Career Articles

This category encompasses articles about all aspects of a mathematical career, including articles in which the author gives advice on hard-won lessons from their own career and articles that describe research about the mathematics profession. We offer the following list of illustrative titles, but we stress that these are merely a few examples from among a wide range of possibilities: How to write a good math article; Navigating the grant and fellowship application process; Running a (math) department; Study tips to help your students succeed; Mathematical careers in industry/government; Participating in the wider mathematical community; Resources to help your career; Reflections on a career; Incorporating LLMs into curriculum and research.

See the Career Collection.

Section top ^

Letters to the Editor

Letters to the Editor are generally not refereed, but the editor-in-chief reserves the right to negotiate changes with the author before publication and to reject material that the editor-in-chief feels is beyond the limits of normal professional discourse.

Material Published by the Secretary

The AMS Secretary uses the Notices to publish official communications from AMS leadership to members and the larger mathematical community. This encompasses a wide variety of material, including:

  1. Calls for Nominations & Applications
  2. AMS Election Call for Suggestions & Results
  3. Biennial Overview of AMS Honors
  4. Annual List of Winners of AMS Prizes
  5. AMS CEO State of the AMS
  6. Annual Report of the Treasurer
  7. Backlog of Mathematics Research Journals

Send material to the AMS Secretary.

See the Secretary Collection.

Resources on Good Mathematical Writing

Articles in the Notices are expected to be written to a high standard of exposition. There are numerous guides to good mathematical writing, including for example the books of N. Higham, S. Krantz, and J. Trzeciak.

References

Higham, Nicholas J. Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences. 3rd ed. Society for Industrial and Applied Mathematics (SIAM), Philadelphia, PA, 2020. ISBN: 978-1-611976-09-0.

Krantz, Steven G. A Primer of Mathematical Writing. 2nd ed. American Mathematical Society, Providence, RI, 2017. ISBN: 978-1-4704-3658-2.

Trzeciak, John. Writing Mathematical Papers in English: A Practical Guide. European Mathematical Society, Cambridge 1995. ISBN: 978-0-521-55993-5.
