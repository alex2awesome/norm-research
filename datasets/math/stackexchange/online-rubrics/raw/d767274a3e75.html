Abstract

This study focuses on students who are registered for the University of Pretoria’s academic development programme, the Four-year Programme (FYP). The programme was introduced as a gateway for students who are under-prepared but have the potential to succeed. This programme helps them to then continue their studies in mainstream science programmes. Our research focuses on measuring the change in the academic maturity of these students. In the theoretical framework that we developed, academic maturity is subdivided into two components, namely, non-subject based maturity, and subject based maturity (mathematical maturity). A mathematics test was also administered twice (at the beginning of the year and after the first semester) and was used to measure the subject based maturity of students. The results of the pre- and post-tests were compared to measure the improvement in students’ mathematical skills. The results showed that in all of the topics and constructs, there was an improvement in students’ mathematical abilities. The study also shows that students still struggle with the fundamentals of some mathematics topics, even after a semester of tuition in the FYP.

Introduction

In recent years, there have been reports from the South African Department of Basic Education that learners opt not to take mathematics as a subject in the final years of secondary school (Campbell & Prew, 2014). This led to a decrease in the number of Grade 12 learners writing mathematics examinations from 2009 to date. Moreover, the number of learners qualifying for science and engineering degree programmes has declined (Engelbrecht et al., 2015). To the country's detriment, those who qualify were found to lack the necessary mathematical skills required to be placed in or to succeed in university-level mathematics courses (Jacobs & Pretorius, 2016). The alarming number of under-prepared students entering universities is not a predicament faced by South Africa alone. Similar concerns have been reported in countries such as the United States (Moran, 2008), the Philippines (Amanonce, 2020), and Australia (Robinson et al., 2019), to name a few. In an attempt to address the shortage in these fields and to improve the mathematical skills of students, the South African Higher Education Department devised a strategy to increase the number of students in these fields by introducing funding for access programmes, known as academic development programmes, at universities around the country. These programmes aim to address incoming university students' shortfalls in mathematics and science and their general unpreparedness for university study (Engelbrecht et al., 2015). Other countries worldwide also embarked on new programmes to curb the ‘gap’ between high school and university mathematics. Most tertiary institutions in South Africa now offer some form of academic development for science degrees and/or diploma programmes. These offerings can be categorised as extended, foundation or augmented programmes (Engelbrecht et al., 2015). In extended programmes, the foundation modules and modules from the first academic year of a BSc degree are integrated and distributed in a coherent manner over two years and the extra time is used for academic support and skills development. Foundation programmes typically consist of a one-year curriculum of preparatory science and academic literacy modules after which a student enters mainstream BSc. In the augmented model first-year modules are spread over two years with concurrent support in the form of extra tutorials and/or practicals (CHE, 2013). The programme that is reported on in this article is an extended degree programme.

This study investigates the extent to which an academic development programme fosters growth in the academic maturity of students in mathematics in the first semester, in one of the universities in South Africa. The study was conducted at the University of Pretoria's extended programme, called the Four-Year Programme (FYP). Students registered in this programme, attend lectures at the Mamelodi campus, one of the satellite campuses of the University of Pretoria. Although the study was designed mainly to measure the academic maturity of students in an extended programme in mathematics in South Africa, we believe this model could be used in any educational environment worldwide. Moreover, similar investigations could be extended to the students’ development of the entire first year at university, and interviews could be conducted to add more value to the results.

The Four-year Programme (FYP) at the University of Pretoria

The background of the Bachelor of Science Four-Year Programme (FYP) programme is described extensively in Engelbrecht et al. (2015) and is summarised here. The programme was initiated in 2008 as an access programme in the fields of mathematical, physical and biological sciences (Potgieter et al., 2015). The FYP is designed for students with academic potential but whose secondary education did not prepare them adequately for studies at tertiary level. Students in the programme are from different schooling backgrounds; however, a large percentage are from historically previously disadvantaged groups.

The programme is designed in two phases in which students develop academically and socio-psychologically. During the first phase, students do a semester of academic and social bridging. In the second phase, they do a two-semester programme at a slower pace than the mainstream programme. After three semesters, students then join the mainstream programme for their second semester (see Fig. 1).

The Grade 12 examination in South Africa is probably one of the most important stages in any students academic career. Students are selected for university study on the grounds of their Grade 12 results. The admission requirements for students in the FYP are lower than for the mainstream BSc and different for each field of study. A minimum Grade 12 mathematics mark of 40% is required for students in the BCom stream, while the other streams (BSc, Multimedia and Information Technology) require 50% in Grade 12 mathematics. To be considered successful in the first-semester mathematics module of the FYP, a student has to obtain a mark of 50% or more. For admission to mainstream degrees, the entrance requirements are substantially higher. The first-semester module of the FYP is taught at a significantly slower pace than the normal mainstream programme, to accommodate the variety of student backgrounds.

Academic Maturity

According to Althoff (2010), academic performance goes hand in hand with academic maturity, that is, students with high levels of academic maturity show better academic achievement than other students. Engelbrecht and Harding (2006) understand academic maturity as encompassing areas such as educational, organisational, and social maturity. The academic maturity definitions of Althoff (2010) and that of Engelbrecht and Harding (2006) (discussed in detail in the literature review) assisted us in classifying academic maturity into cognitive (mathematical) and non-cognitive (social) maturity. The objective of our larger study is to measure the growth in academic maturity of students in the FYP by measuring the growth in content based academic maturity (mathematical maturity) as well as in non-content based academic maturity (social maturity).

In this paper, however, we report on measuring the results on content based academic maturity (mathematical maturity). The non-cognitive results were discussed in Yani et al. (2019). To recall, these results show that, over the first semester, in all dimensions, there is a surprising decline in students’ perceptions regarding their abilities. The Dunning-Kruger effect (Dunning, 2011) was used to explain this unexpected decline, in that students seem to develop a more realistic view of their maturity, which in itself can be seen as growth in academic maturity.

Literature Review

The under-preparedness of first-year students for university mathematics is a worldwide concern (Heck & van Gastel, 2005). The number of students entering colleges without the necessary skills in mathematics is disturbing (Nihan Er, 2018; Robinson et al., 2019). Moreover, transitioning from high school to university is challenging (Akoojee & Nkomo, 2007; Clark & Lovric, 2008). In general, adjusting from high school to university is one of the most demanding experiences that many students have to endure in their lifetime (Feldt et al., 2011) their academic success could be influenced by whether they adjust positively or negatively to the university environment. D'Souza and Wood (2012) believe that the fact that first-year students are poorly prepared for university could be the main reason why the transition from high school to university is experienced as difficult for students. Issues such as not being continuously monitored at university, as they were in secondary school, are among other causes of students experiencing difficulty in adjusting to the university environment (Jones et al., 2008).

Clark and Lovric (2008) argue that the transition from high school to university is a challenging process. Moreover, this transition, in mathematics specifically, is considered to be difficult, ranging across a variety of issues (Clark & Lovric, 2008). Engelbrecht (2010, p. 143) supports this view "… the transition from high school to university or from calculus to analysis is experienced as traumatic by many students.” Although this issue is also visible in other scientific disciplines, the transition from one mathematics level to the next is seen as more challenging globally (Niss, 2003). Thomas et al. (2010) investigated the factors that cause students to experience difficulty when transitioning from high school to tertiary mathematics. Their results showed that though students are affected differently, the transition process changed their motivation for studying mathematics and their confidence declined during the transition.

Whilst academic development was flourishing earlier, its capacity to contribute to the transformation and development of South African higher education has been questioned by some researchers (Boughey, 2007). This includes large-scale commissioned reports by the Council on Higher Education (CHE, 2013) and Inyathelo (2014). An analysis of foundational provision in South Africa by Dhunpath and Vithal (2012) reports of success stories in foundation/extended programmes published by the Foundation Special Interest Group of HELTASA (Higher Education Learning and Teaching Association of South Africa) (HELTASA, 2010).

Universities use different criteria to evaluate general undergraduate programmes, mainly focusing on the achievement of pre-set learning objectives; quality of teaching staff members; quality of graduate output; student success; employment placement after completion of the programme; student personal growth; as well as the articulation of the programme with other programmes within the university and at other leading institutions (Inyathelo, 2014; SUNY, 2012).

Completion rates are documented as one measure of success for academic development programmes (Engelbrecht et al., 2015; HELTASA, 2010). WITS (University of the Witwatersrand, Johannesburg) reports that over the period 1992 – 2005 the BSc Four-Year degree programme showed a completion rate of just under 50% outperforming the WITS BSc three-year programme that showed a completion rate of 42% over the same period (Ndaba, 2005). As yet another measure of the success of AD programmes, the Tshwane University of Technology (TUT) reports a disappointingly high attrition rate during and directly following the first foundational year, but an impressive success rate after that, on par with regular programme students. Some other reports on the success of bridging programmes refer to short-period programmes that are non-compulsory and are conducted during summer holidays prior to starting with university academic lectures (Ashley et al., 2017; Bradford, et al., 2021; Eitemuller & Habig, 2020). Grayson (1997) measured the success of an academic development programme on qualitative factors, sourced through interviews and questionnaires with students, including changes in attitude to learning and learning behaviour, development of practical and cognitive skills, transfer of learning between different contexts, development of academic communication skills, self-awareness and other life skills.

Murray (2015) examined the effectiveness of two bridging (BSc-Augmented and BSc-4-year Foundation) programmes using regression adjustment and propensity score matching methods to control for a possible bias selection that can occur with observational studies. In the BSc 4-year Augmented programme, students are allowed to take two years to complete, what for normal entry students would be their first year of study, with special classes and extra tuition material being provided to help them cope with the mainstream courses that they are taking. In the BSc 4-year Foundation programme, students spend their first year doing a suite of non-credit bearing courses that have been designed specifically to help prepare them for their following year of study when they will be doing the mainstream courses that an ordinary first-year student entering the faculty would have to do. He reports that the results showed students performed better after attending these programmes than if they did not attend.

In an attempt to address issues directed at improving students’ performance in mathematics, Niss (2003) suggested eight competency strands. According to Niss (2003), mastering mathematics involves the skill of comprehending, applying, evaluating and implementing mathematical theory. In her study on formative assessment feedback on the mathematical competence of students, Bothma (2014) developed an instrument for a first-year mathematical module that would give proper feedback on mathematical skills in finite mathematics. In her taxonomy, she identified five dimensions that can be used as a measure of mathematical competence: knowing mathematics; communicating mathematics; doing and reasoning mathematics; representing mathematics, and modelling and solving.

The reviewed studies show a number of factors influencing the difficulty experienced by students when transitioning from high-school to university mathematics – which alternately influences students’ performance in the subject. These factors are related to students’ lack of conceptual knowledge in mathematics and in applying the basic mathematical skills needed to succeed at tertiary level.

The present study uses these theoretical foundations to build a framework that will measure students’ conceptual knowledge and basic mathematical skills as contributing to their mathematical maturity, that encapsulates some of these factors.

Confidence and Misconceptions

Researchers have shown an interest in self-reported feedback in conjunction with answers (Stankov & Lee, 2014). This is an approach that measures the confidence that students have in the correctness of an answer provided in an assessment. In many cases, students’ confidence does not reflect their academic potential. Thus, self-assessment on confidence can help students address misguided self-perceptions and help educators to identify students with strongly held misconceptions (Morony et al., 2014).

Engelbrecht et al. (2005) conducted a study that compared students’ performance and confidence in procedural and conceptual mathematics in the first year. Their findings were that students were more confident in their ability to grasp theoretical problems than in grasping procedural problems. The study also showed that students have more misconceptions regarding procedural knowledge than with conceptual understanding.

Hasan et al. (1999) define misconceptions as firmly held intellectual structures that are different from the correct concepts of a specific field. The authors emphasise that in order to identify the presence of misconceptions in science, students’ self-reported certainty of responses could be used in conjunction with their answers.

Misconceptions are believed to interfere with the learning of new skills and knowledge (Hasan et al., 1999). In mathematics, science, and geography, it is believed that the fundamentals of education are that a student is not an empty vessel; hence, the vital part of teaching is to determine what the student already knows (Jansen, 2009). Therefore, lecturers of first-year university students need to be well informed about what type of knowledge and understanding students possess from high school. This may contribute towards a smooth transition for students between school and tertiary education (Potgieter et al., 2010).

Hasan et al. (1999) used the certainty of response index or confidence index (CI) in multiple-choice questions for first-year undergraduate students studying classical mechanics. The method involves a six-point confidence scale from zero to five. The degree of certainty is low for a CI of zero to two and high for a CI of three to five.

In order to identify the participating students’ possible misconceptions and lack of knowledge, we used Hasan et al.’s (1999) decision matrix model. Their model uses confidence of response (CI) in conjunction with the correctness or incorrectness of an answer to distinguish between a lack of knowledge and the presence of misconceptions.

A low CI in conjunction with a correct or incorrect answer is an indication of a lack of knowledge, which means a student could have guessed the correct answer. A high CI in conjunction with the correct answer is an indication of the presence of knowledge. This shows that the student probably used the proper procedure to arrive at their solution. A high CI given with an incorrect answer is an indication of the presence of misconception; that is, the student believes that the procedure of arriving at the solution is correct. Mathematical Maturity

There is no formal definition that clearly describes mathematical maturity. Steen (1983, p. 99) states that defining mathematical maturity is as futile an exercise as trying to define “ love or justice”. On the other hand, Lew (2019) states that mathematicians use the term informally to describe undergraduate students who achieved a combination of mathematical skills such as technical skills, habits of investigation, persistence, and conceptual understanding of the subject. However, Moursund (2007) believes that, mathematical maturity is an expression used to describe the abstract part of mathematics, such as proofs, logical reasoning, critical, creative reasoning and advanced thinking in higher education. It includes the capacity to generalise from a specific example to a broad concept, handle increasingly abstract ideas, communicate mathematically by learning standard notation and acceptable style and make a significant shift from learning by memorisation to learning through understanding. Faulkner et al. (2019), found engineering faculty members synthesising mathematical maturity with modelling, extracting meaning from symbols and appropriate use of computational tools.

The colloquial use of mathematical maturity is reserved for a certain stage in a person's intellectual growth that marks the transition from routine, elementary modes of thought to subtle, complex patterns. This transition ordinarily occurs, if at all, sometime during the college years (Steen, 1983). Garrity (2014) illustrated mathematical maturity in stages from pre-school to doctoral degree; he also supports the notion of mathematical maturity being a mark of maturity reached at individual stages of learning mathematics. Steen (1983) sees mathematical maturity at two levels – competency in procedural skills and knowledge (undergraduate level) and understanding of the depth that links mathematics and the world (postgraduate level). Steen (1983) alludes that although it might not be easy to define mathematical maturity, there are characteristics that only mathematicians can identify. Examples that are relevant to this study include:

  • to use and interpret mathematical notation and symbols

  • to model and express real-world phenomena in mathematical form

  • to see and exploit relationships among various ways of representing mathematics to read and understand mathematical writing

  • to see patterns and apply the appropriate principles.

So, mathematical maturity can be seen as reaching a particular level of competency in procedural skills as well as the ability to conceptualise different mathematical theories and the knowledge to link them to real-life situations, on the one hand, but along with the mathematical competency (content knowledge) there should also be an element of confidence in your mathematical ability. So, we view mathematical maturity as consisting of two components – mathematical competency (content knowledge) and the confidence that what you are doing is correct.

Theoretical Framework

The theoretical framework for the non-content based maturity in this study was discussed in detail in Yani et al. (2019) and is summarised here. The framework's structure was motivated by the definitions of academic maturity suggested by Althoff (2010) and Engelbrecht and Harding (2006). To these authors, academic maturity encompasses areas such as educational, organisational and social maturity. These definitions and interpretations by Althoff (2010) and Engelbrecht and Harding (2006) are explained in their studies to demonstrate that academic performance at university is not only affected by a student’s cognitive excellence, but also by non-cognitive traits. This study measures the academic maturity of students in an extended programme, where both behavioural and cognitive traits could influence students’ success in the programme. Thus the two definitions prompted the framework structure of classifying behavioural traits into organisational, social and personal maturity for the non-content maturity, and into mathematical competency and mathematical confidence for the content based academic maturity, as discussed below. In this paper, we focus only on measuring the content based academic maturity.

Bothma’s (2014) work features prominently in this study, where she developed an instrument on mathematical competency. Some of the competency strands that she suggested are discussed below with additional views and examples from different authors.

Knowing Mathematics

Bothma (2014) describes knowing mathematics as a way to recognise and comprehend when to apply specific mathematical concepts successfully and efficiently. This ability to identify and understand, manage and apply specific mathematical concepts and boundaries involves, what Niss (2003) calls, mathematical thinking. This dimension measures the proper usage of mathematical notations and the use of the notations, being able to identify the connections between symbols and knowing their limitations and strengths.

Mathematical Thinking

Mathematical thinking is also seen as an essential goal of schooling as it supports other scientific fields (Stacey, 2007). It is the ability to respond to the ‘how’ and the ‘why’ in mathematics, as well as the understanding of restrictions when applying mathematical theories (Sáenz, 2009). “Mathematics is, however, not only about knowing and doing, it is about analysing, reasoning, communicating, posing formulating, solving and interpreting” (Bothma, 2014, p. 17).

Communicating mathematics refers to the correct use of mathematical symbols and language, and the ability to write down the solution to problems using a precise mathematical method (Bothma, 2014). It is also the appropriate use of mathematical symbols and language, and the ability to construct solutions in a universally approved mathematical form (Niss, 2003). This includes a mathematical dialogue of challenging students to communicate mathematics, both orally and in writing that develops students’ understanding of mathematics. Training students to explain the steps taken and to answer questions on how they solved a mathematical problem, helps students to understand the material better (Wichelt, 2009). In so doing, students’ skills in communicating mathematics are developed, which gives students the confidence to participate openly during lectures (Wichelt, 2009).

This dimension measures the ability to perform a mathematical procedure and the proper use of mathematical tools.

Representing mathematics refers to a student’s ability to use of similar mathematical formulas and concepts, and being able to interpret, convert, and identify the different mathematical techniques.

This dimension measures the ability to understand and transform real-life issues into a mathematical formula and then solve them.

The fifth strand (measuring communication skills) suggested by Bothma (2014) was not used, partly because the format of the measuring instruments does not allow for measuring this skill. For the purpose of this study, as a working definition for mathematical maturity (content based maturity), we include the two components: mathematical competence and mathematical confidence. Research Questions

Our study investigates the following research questions.

  • What is the level of mathematical maturity of students entering the programme?

  • To what extent, does the mathematical maturity change during the course of the first semester mathematics course of the academic development programme?

To measure mathematical maturity, we measure and compare (following our working definition of mathematical maturity) students’ content knowledge (mathematical competency) and their confidence. To measure the growth in mathematical maturity, we do this comparison before the programme and again at the end of the programme.

As mentioned earlier, the non-content based dimension of academic maturity was investigated in Yani et al. (2019). In this part of the study, we attempt to provide more information on the content based (mathematics) maturity.

Research Design

Instrument

A mathematics diagnostic test was administered twice to the 2015 cohort of first-year students in the FYP during the first-year university orientation at the start of a new academic year and again at the end of the first semester. The questions used in the test were extracted from the Mathematics Achievement Test obtained from the Alternative Admission Research Project (AARP) of the University of Cape Town (Yeld & Haeck, 1997). The University of Pretoria has been collaborating in developing these tests. The test is a structured question paper that measures the subject-based maturity associated with the related skills. Every question is followed by a self-evaluation question that seeks clarity regarding students’ confidence in the answer provided. The pre- and post- tests were identical. The study was comparative in nature since the data from before and after the students were taught in the first semester of the FYP, were compared.

The diagnostic mathematics test used in the study consisted of questions covering various mathematical topics (see the Appendix for the instrument). These were classified into six different mathematical topics by a team of experts: algebraic expressions, algebraic equations, inequalities, functions and graphs, trigonometry and logic. The topic choices were determined by the type of questions included in the test, and the classification of items resulted from the methods that needed to be applied while finding the solution. In some instances, items could be categorised in more than one of the topics. The team of experts decided on which topic is the most dominant in the solution of the question. For example, question 17: \(xy=1\) is an equation of a hyperbola \(y=\frac{1}{x}\) and also could be viewed as a function. However, the method of answering this question depends on logical reasoning; hence the item is classified under the logic topic.

Algebraic Expressions (4 items)

Algebraic expressions can be thought of as a group of terms that comprise numbers and variables combined using mathematical operations (\(+, -, \times , \div\)). Questions of this form typically involve the writing of an expression in a simplified form. Students would be asked to simplify, factorise, and/or find values that are permitted to be substituted into the expression.

Algebraic Eqs. (1 item)

This is another form of a group of terms that consist of numbers and variables combined using mathematical operations (\(+, -, \times , \div\)) and an equals sign that equates the expression on the left to those on the right. Such questions involve finding a solution for the variable appearing in the equation.

Inequalities (2 items)

These items involve two mathematical expressions separated by a sign that implies that one side is either smaller or larger (\(<, >\)) than the other expression.

Functions and Graphs (4 items)

Items involve functions represented by either algebraic formulas or graphs.

Trigonometry (3 items)

Items involve the basic trigonometric functions, the sine, cosine, and tangent functions.

Logic (2 items)

These are questions that include translating real-life problems into mathematical equations and explaining the relationship between variables given as an equation.

A second categorisation of the items in the test was done according to the taxonomy, as explained in Sect. 5 (the theoretical framework). Items were grouped into the four mentioned dimensions of mathematics competency, with nine items in the doing and reasoning mathematics dimension, two in representing mathematics, four in knowing mathematics, and one in modelling and solving mathematical problems. Grouping of these items resulted from the methods that needed to be applied while finding the solution synthesised with the dimension definitions outlined in the theoretical framework (see Sect. 5 above). In some instances, items could be categorised in more than one dimension; however, the same procedure as that of topic classification was followed. That is, where the team of experts had to decide on which topic was the most dominant in the solution of the question.

On completion of each of the items, the student had to indicate how confident s/he was about their answer to the question, indicating his/her confidence on a four-point scale from 1 (for not confident) to 4 (confident).

Reliability and Validity

Data source triangulation was conducted to enhance reliability (credibility), in that the test was first administered twice (before and after the first semester) to the 2014 cohort of the FYP before it was used with the 2015 cohort of students in the study. A comparison of the analysis of the correlation between the two cohorts’ results showed sufficient similarities.

In order to attend to face validity (representative coverage of the mathematics content) and construct validity (relating to the theoretical knowledge needed to be measured) (Muijs, 2011), a team of experts classified the items into the different mathematical topics and dimensions, according to the definitions of the topics and dimensions as in the taxonomy (Sect. 5, theoretical framework).

Participants

The participants in the study are students registered for the first mathematics module in the FYP at the Mamelodi campus of the University of Pretoria (WTW133). The module is designed as a bridging module, revising the fundamentals of algebra and trigonometry and gradually introducing new mathematics concepts, such as absolute values. A total of 739 students participated in the pre-test. While 592 participated in the mathematics post-test, only 385 students participated in both the pre- and post- tests, and these students were analysed to study their possible change in mathematics maturity. Data containing incomplete student information, such as incomplete student numbers, were not considered. Of the 385 participants in the study, 64% (248) were students from disadvantaged backgrounds, most of whom came from rural areas in KwaZulu Natal, Eastern Cape and the Limpopo province. These students attended high schools where the quality of teaching is sometimes considered as below standard. The study excludes demographical data such as gender, age, family size, nationality, religion, etc. Such information is not included in the analysis of the findings.

Data Analysis

The data analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel (version 2302), and SPSS (version 28.0.1.0) software. Descriptive statistics were obtained to explore the data, which included the population parameters of frequency, mean and correlation p-value. The pre and post- tests consist of 16 mathematics questions and confidence responses. Each question has a mark of one; the total for each test is 16 marks. The confidence responses were rated from 1 (not confident) to 4 (confident). A total for each question (item) across the students’ responses was calculated to obtain an average for the question. In addition, each student's performance score and confidence index value were calculated as an average of the scores obtained by the student in all 16 questions over the mathematics questions and the confidence responses, respectively.

The averages of the confidence scores against average performance test scores were analysed and compared using several methods. Firstly, item averages (in percentage) of the pre-test and post-test CI vs PS were plotted on the scatter diagram. We compared items being represented in each quadrant in the pre-test to those in the post-test. Since 50% is the general pass mark for university courses in South Africa, a test mark of 50% and above is regarded as a high mark and an average confidence of 2.5 and above is regarded as high confidence, resulting in four different quadrants referred to by name as follows: Quadrant 1: adequate knowledge quadrant (high confidence, high mark), Quadrant 2: misconception quadrant (high confidence, low mark), Quadrant 3: lack of knowledge quadrant (low confidence, low mark) and Quadrant 4: the guessing quadrant (low confidence, high mark). This was done to ascertain whether items changed position in the post-test as compared to the pre-test.

Secondly, for each item, the difference between the confidence level values of the pre-test and the post-test were calculated using a statistical t-test. This was conducted between the pre- and post- tests’ confidence level values per item, for each mathematical topic, and for each mathematical taxonomy dimension.

Thirdly, for each item, the correlation between the confidence index value and the performance score was analysed using Pearson's correlation coefficient with a 95% confidence interval. In addition, Hasan et al.s’ (1999) model was used to determine if inadequate problem-solving in mathematics resulted from misconceptions or a lack of adequate knowledge. For the sample size of this study, the correlation coefficient's critical value was \(r\le 0.1\). A Pearson correlation coefficient of \(r\le 0.1\) indicates a non-linear relationship between the variables. Also, \(0.11\le r\le 0.29\) indicates a weak correlation, a moderate correlation was seen if \(0.31\le r\le 0.49\), a strong correlation was seen if \(0.51\le r\le 0.69\), and a very strong correlation was identified if \(r\ge 0.71\).

Lastly, with all comparisons, we calculate the data effect size Cohen’s d, to obtain an indication of the effect that the change may have. Effect size gives an indication of how meaningful the relationship between variables or the difference between groups is, and indicates the practical significance of a research outcome. A commonly used interpretation is to refer to effect sizes as small (d = 0.2), medium (d = 0.5), and large (d = 0.8) based on suggested benchmarks. A large effect size means that a finding has practical significance, while a small effect size indicates limited practical implication (Thompson, 2007).

Limitations

This academic maturity study was conducted during the first semester on a group of students registered at the University of Pretoria’s AD programme. Due to time constraints, it was not possible to extend the study for a longer period. Moreover, comparing each student’s progress to a higher level of mathematics was not done as it was not the focus of the study. However, this procedure would have added more value to the findings of this study.

Since one of the (secondary) objectives of the programme is to assist students to make a decision about their future by exposing them to science subjects, some students leave the course to enter different study fields or realise that they do not want to continue university studies. This contributes to a fairly high drop-out rate. Students who dropped out, or did not complete both studies, might have skewed results somewhat. With the large sample size, however, this issue would probably not have had a serious impact on the results.

Results

Overall Results

There is a general increase in students’ performance in the post-test as compared to the pre-test results for all mathematical topics covered in the test. That is, 287 (74.55%) of students obtained a mark of 50% and above in the post-test, compared to 166 (43.11%) in the pre-test.

A paired samples t-test was conducted to determine the difference between students’ test performance scores on the pre- and post- tests. The results indicate a significant difference between pre-test score (\(\mathrm{M}=45.65\); \(SD (\mathrm{standard\;deviation})=14.75\)) and post-test score (\(\mathrm{M}=57.21\); \(\mathrm{SD}=15.19\)); (\(\mathrm{t}(\mathrm{N}=384)=13.66\), \(p< 0.01\)). The 95% confidence interval of the difference between the means (\(\mathrm{M}=11.56\)) ranged from \(9.89\mathrm{ \;to\; }13.22\) and indicates that the difference between the means is statistically significant. With a high Cohen’s d of 0.81, the results show a strong effect, contributing to the conclusion that the FYP successfully improved students' mathematical skills.

In addition, a paired sample t-test was conducted to determine the difference between students’ confidence levels on the pre- and the post- CI. The post-CI level mean (\(\mathrm{M}=3.17\); \(\mathrm{SD}=0.53\)), is slightly higher than the mean for the pre-CI level (\(\mathrm{M}=3.08\); \(SD=0.50\)). These results were statistically significant (\(p<0.01\)). However, the difference of only 0.1 between the results is so small that we hesitate to conclude that being in the FYP for six months, significantly improves students’ confidence levels when doing mathematics.

Overall, we conclude that initially, students’ mathematical abilities were poor, partially addressing our first research question. However, regarding mathematics performance there was a significant improvement throughout the semester, contributing to answering the second research question.

Discussion and Conclusions

Mathematics is experienced as a subject in which students find it challenging when they are faced with complex problems (Zaini & Amhad, 2010).

Regarding our first research question, our results clearly indicate that our students arrive at university with low mathematical maturity. This is shown by the performance in the pre-test—a large number of students had not obtained the required mathematical skills level when coming to university (results of the pre-test); as well as by the high level of misconceptions that exist at that stage.

The improvement in performance in the post-test results can be viewed as noticeable progress and growth in mathematical maturity. Fewer misconceptions exist and students have a better idea of their own mathematical abilities. These results positively address our second research question.

The positive results cannot conceal the conceptual difficulties and misconceptions that were observed from the items that remained positioned in the misconception quadrant in the post-test. The three topics (inequalities, trigonometry and functions) represent the types of concepts taught in high school and yet, remain a problem for some students. This may be a clear indication where the continual existence of misconceptions impedes the learning of proper concepts; or, this might be the result of students making numerical errors in procedural problems (Engelbrecht et al., 2005). It may be true that high confidence can cause students not to think carefully and deeply about the procedures chosen in problem-solving.

The purpose of administering the pre- and post- tests was to measure if there is a change in students' mathematical maturity in different topics. The findings show an improvement in mathematical ability for most topics. Nonetheless, it is clear that there still is work to be done. The results show that the majority of students seem to have difficulties with the inequalities and the logic topics, even after the intervention. Students may experience difficulty in mathematics due to poor basic mathematical background, unconventional mathematical thinking and poor memory (Allen, 2007). The findings seem to indicate that students need to review their conceptual understanding of word problems in formulating real-life problems into mathematical equations. The challenge in such mathematical concepts, i.e. word problems, is evident in the findings for both the mathematics topics and the mathematics dimensions. In both the topics and the dimensions' findings, we observe the low scores depicted by the results, especially for the logic topic and the modelling and solving mathematical problems dimension, where scores were low in both the pre-test and the post-test. The programme could also be blamed for the low scores in modelling since only a small portion of such problems are taught in the first semester. However, it appears that many universities around the world do not seem to provide the required time for teaching "word problems" (Ferri & Blum, 2009).

The overall dimension results show more students obtaining 50% and above in the post-test compared to the pre-test in all dimensions. Based on these findings, we can positively conclude that the majority of students' mathematical abilities have improved.

In the first semester, there was no individual remediation introduced in the programme, and the same teaching methods were used for all students; also there was no method used to distinguish between students with no knowledge and those who showed the presence of misconceptions. However, this study shows that this cohort of students are academically more mature with regards to the specific content matter despite their weak foundation in mathematics and the challenging social and personal issues they were exposed to when entering university.

The pre-test results show that these students entered the FYP with weak mathematical skills and that they overestimated their own performance abilities. Alternatively, the post-test results showed students whose basic mathematical skills had improved, along with an improvement in their ability to judge their knowledge of the content. The investigation on determining the relationship between confidence and performance was used to measure students' improvement in mathematical skills and their confidence in these skills. This model was also used to identify students' inadequate problem-solving skills in mathematics due to the presence of misconceptions or a lack of knowledge.

According to our working definition of mathematical maturity – a combination of students’ mathematical skills and their confidence—we can therefore conclude that the programme positively impacted on the mathematical maturity of students.

The study also revealed that the students' inadequate problem-solving skills in mathematics resulted from either misconceptions or a lack of adequate knowledge. Our results support those of Sarwadi and Shahrill (2014), who showed that even after being taught for some time, students showed difficulty with some basic mathematical skills.

The statistical method used to compare the students' improvement in mathematical competency in the four dimensions adopted from Bothma's (2014) competency tool showed a significant difference between the pre- and post- tests’ results. The results were positive, showing a general increase in students' scores for all four dimensions, namely, representing mathematics, knowing mathematics, doing and reasoning mathematics, as well as modelling and solving mathematical problems. A noteworthy finding is that, in all four dimensions, there were more students whose mathematical skills improved than those whose skills decreased.

The transition process to advanced mathematical thinking is experienced as traumatic by many students (Engelbrecht, 2010). Students’ experiences of school mathematics differ greatly from what is expected from them at university in that success in school mathematics means application of different methods to obtain an answer. Students are not familiar with logical deductive reasoning, required in advanced mathematics. Tall (2007) calls this a significant transition, moving from describing to defining, and from convincing to proving in a logical manner. So, we teach mathematics differently at university, in that pedagogical and epistemological issues associated with the nature of mathematics change quite dramatically. We fully acknowledge that these factors may also contribute to the differences in maturity that this study has identified.

To summarise, our study shows that many of our students arrive at university not prepared for what they will be confronted with. The transition, mathematically as well as socially, is experienced as very steep and universities that are seriously concerned about students’ success, need to plan carefully to introduce measures to address this transition. Many of these students do not even realise that they need assistance and it is not good enough for university lecturers just to “be available” for student enquiries – they have to realise that many of these students need external guidance to adapt, both cognitively and socially.
