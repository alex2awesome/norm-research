# The New York Times — Guidelines on Our Integrity
SOURCE_URL: https://www.nytimes.com/editorial-standards/guidelines-on-integrity.html
DOMAIN: journalism

At a time of public suspicion about the accuracy and integrity of journalism, The Times requires standards that ensure nothing erodes readers' confidence in its news columns. Falsifying any part of a news report results in disciplinary action up to termination.

## Other people's reporting (attribution of claims sourced from elsewhere)

When The Times uses facts gathered by any other organization, it attributes them. This applies to newspapers, magazines, books, broadcasts, and news agencies including The Associated Press (e.g., "the Senator told The Associated Press") — even though AP is a co-op that The Times belongs to, its reporting is not treated as The Times's own. When writing from a pool report without having witnessed the events directly, the pool reporter is credited. In a roundup, a generic phrase like "reports from news agencies and New York Times bureaus" may be used.

**Preference for independent verification:** When time and distance permit, The Times does its own reporting and verifies another organization's story — in which case attribution of the underlying facts is not required (though credit for breaking an exclusive is still given as a matter of courtesy).

**Attribution does not launder unreportable claims:** Attribution to another publication cannot serve as a license to print rumors that would not meet The Times's own reporting standards. Rumors must independently satisfy The Times's standards of newsworthiness, taste and plausibility before publication, even when attributed to someone else. The need to attribute a claim to protect against liability is itself a cue to consult with the department head about whether publishing it is warranted at all.

Stories should distinguish in print between information the reporter personally witnessed and information from personal interviews vs. telephone/email interviews vs. written statements, whenever that distinction matters.

## Fact-checking

Writers are their own principal — often only — fact checkers. Concrete facts (distances, addresses, phone numbers, titles) must be verified with standard references (directories, official websites); more obscure checks may go to the research desk. If deadline pressure forces skipping a check, editors must be flagged explicitly (e.g., "desk, please verify"), and the writer should ideally double back after filing.

## Corrections

The Times recognizes an ethical responsibility to correct all factual errors, large and small. Any complaint about accuracy should be relayed to a responsible supervising editor and investigated quickly; if warranted, a correction should be published immediately. In cases of reasonable doubt or disagreement about the facts, a statement may be acknowledged as "imprecise" or "incomplete" even without certainty that it was fully wrong.

## Rebuttals (response-seeking as verification/fairness step)

The Times seeks and publishes a response from anyone criticized in its pages. When the criticism is serious, there is a special obligation to describe the scope of the accusation and let the subject respond in detail — no subject should be taken by surprise by the published piece, or feel there was no chance to respond.

## Anonymity and its devices (the core claim-attribution test)

**The use of unidentified sources is reserved for situations in which the newspaper could not otherwise print information it considers newsworthy and reliable.** When possible, reporter and editor should discuss any promise of anonymity *before* it is made, or before reporting begins on a story likely to result in such a commitment (some beats — criminal justice, national security — may carry standing authorization to grant anonymity).

Attribution rule: tell readers as much as possible about the placement and known motivation of the source. The Times avoids automatic boilerplate phrases about a source having "insisted on anonymity," and instead tries to state tersely what kind of understanding was actually reached between reporter and source, especially where the source's reasons can be shed light on. The Times does not dissemble about sourcing — it does not, for example, refer to a single person as "sources" (plural), and does not say "other officials" when quoting someone already cited by name elsewhere in the piece. There is no fixed formula, but the attribution must be literally truthful, not coy.

## Fictional devices (concealment must be disclosed, never fabricated)

The Times refrains outright from assigning fictional names, ages, places or dates to protect an identity, and strictly limits other concealment devices. If shielding an identity is unavoidable (e.g., a hospital or school governed by privacy rules), the preferred solution is to omit the name and explicitly explain the omission to the reader. Where several shielded identities must be distinguished in a complex narrative, options in order of preference are: given names with last initials; given names alone; generic descriptions (the lawyer; the psychotherapist); or, as a rare last resort with senior-editor consultation, coined single initials. Whatever device is used, the article must gracefully indicate that a device is in use and explain the reason.

## Masquerading

Reporters do not actively misrepresent their identity to get a story. They may passively remain silent about their identity to observe institutional behavior (e.g., a public rally, an interaction with the public), but sustained, systematic deception (e.g., taking a job under false pretenses to observe a business from the inside) requires consultation between a department head and masthead editors before being employed.
