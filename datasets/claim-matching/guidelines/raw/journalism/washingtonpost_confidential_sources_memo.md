# The Washington Post — Memo on the Use of Confidential Sources (February 2004)
SOURCE_URL: https://accountablejournalism.org/ethics-codes/usa-washington-post-note-on-use-of-confidential-sources
DOMAIN: journalism

The Washington Post is committed to disclosing to its readers the sources of the information in its stories to the maximum possible extent, so reporting is as transparent as possible about how and where information was obtained.

## Confidential sources — general standard

Granting anonymity to a source should not be done casually or automatically. Named sources are vastly preferred to unnamed sources; reporters should press sources to go on the record — persistent pushing often works. If a source refuses to allow identification, the reporter should consider seeking the information elsewhere.

Editors have an obligation to know the identity of unnamed sources used in a story so editors and reporters can jointly assess whether using them is appropriate. A source may not insist the reporter withhold their identity from editors — if this happens, the reporter should tell the source that information obtained that way cannot be published; the source of anything printed will be known to at least one editor.

## Minimum corroboration requirement

**The Post prefers at least two sources for factual information that depends on confidential informants, and those sources should be independent of each other.** Sources with first-hand or direct knowledge are preferred. A relevant document can sometimes serve as a second source.

**Single-source exception:** There are situations where information from a single source will be published, but only after deliberations involving the executive editor, the managing editor, or the appropriate assistant managing editor (AME). The judgment to rely on one source depends on the source's reliability and the basis for the source's information.

## Describing sources to readers

The Post must tell readers as much as possible about *why* an unnamed source deserves confidence — avoiding bare "sources" or "informed sources" in favor of concrete descriptions (e.g., "sources familiar with the thinking of defense lawyers in the case," "sources on the governor's staff who disagree with his policy"). When sources refuse identification, the story should show that an attempt was made and explain why it failed (e.g., "spoke only on condition that he or she not be named" rather than merely "asked not to be identified" — asking alone is not sufficient grounds for anonymity). In rare cases where no useful descriptive information about a source can be given, approval from the relevant AME, managing editor, or executive editor is required before printing information from a totally unidentified source.

Spokespersons, by virtue of their role, should be on the record for briefings; if they decline to be quoted by name in that context, the Post should protest, seek a publishable explanation, and tell readers what happened if appropriate.

## Attribution must be literally true

Attribution must be truthful: if a story refers to "sources" (plural), there must genuinely be multiple sources; if the story says someone "declined to comment," that must be literally true. The Post will not misrepresent that a source "refused to talk" as a way of protecting that source's identity. Any risk that a formulation might mislead the reader about the sourcing must be discussed with an editor before publication.

## Attribution for facts not obtained by original reporting

Facts and quotations in a story that were not produced by the Post's own reporting must be attributed, so readers can distinguish between what the reporter directly witnessed and what came from other sources (wire services, pool reporters, email, websites, etc.). If a reporter was not present at a described scene, the story must make that clear; a narrative describing events as recounted by witnesses must include attribution for that reconstruction. Collective/summary attribution (e.g., "This account is drawn from interviews with 19 Somalis... and 30 U.S. Army soldiers...") is acceptable for takeouts/investigative pieces but not routine news stories, and should be discussed with an editor first; it must be honest, full, and appropriate whenever used, including noting when accounts could not be independently verified.

## Ground rules and definitions of attribution

- **On the record:** Quotation attributable to the source by name.
- **On background / not for attribution:** Information attributable to a general, unnamed category (e.g., "a police department official"); reporters should push for maximum specificity in this description, prioritizing the reader's ability to evaluate the claim over the source's preferred vagueness.
- **Deep background:** Information usable in the story but with no attribution at all — discouraged because readers get no way to judge where it came from, though it can guide further reporting.
- **Off the record:** Information that cannot be used in the paper or in further reporting at all; the Post is careful to clarify this term because many sources misuse it to mean "not for attribution to me."

Ground rules must be established at the start of an interview (ideally on tape), not renegotiated retroactively — once a quote is on the record, it stays on the record. The Post does not promise sources it will refrain from additional verification efforts.

## Dealing with sources

Statements quoted must be put in context; potentially controversial statements by public figures should be quoted in a complete sentence or paragraph where possible, including what question was being answered. The Post does not publish ad hominem quotations from unnamed sources — sources wanting to attack someone must do so by name. Blind quotations added only for color should be avoided. No pseudonyms are used, and readers are never misled about identities; any decision to use less than a full name requires editor involvement and an explanation to readers of why.
