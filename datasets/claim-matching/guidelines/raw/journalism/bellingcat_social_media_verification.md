# A Beginner's Guide to Social Media Verification (Bellingcat)
SOURCE_URL: https://www.bellingcat.com/resources/2021/11/01/a-beginners-guide-to-social-media-verification/
DOMAIN: journalism

Bellingcat is an open-source investigation outfit whose verification methodology has become a de facto standard for checking claims made via social-media images, video, and posts. This guide lays out the practitioner-level tests it uses to decide whether a piece of online content actually supports the claim being made about it. The guide's throughline is a five-factor check that should be run on essentially any viral image, video, or post before it is treated as evidence: **originality, source, location, time, and motivation.**

## The five core verification checks

**1. Originality — has this content been recycled?**
Before anything else, determine whether the media is actually new/contemporaneous to the event it is being used to illustrate, or whether it has been reused from an earlier, unrelated context. The primary tool for this is reverse image search (Bing Images, Yandex, Google Images, TinEye — run against still frames extracted from video, not just standalone photos). Example given: a viral video purporting to show Covid-era panic buying at an Aldi store was, via reverse image search, traced back to a 2011 German promotional video — the content was genuine but its claimed context (date and event) was false. This is the single most common failure mode the guide warns about: content that is not fabricated, but is *misattributed* to the wrong event.

**2. Source — who posted this, and are they credible?**
Investigate: which platform did it appear on; who is the poster; what is their track record (do they have a history of sharing conspiracy content or hoaxes); what other content do they typically post (their thematic profile); do their hashtags, followers, and posting history plausibly connect them to the event they claim to be depicting. Example: a video of a container ship (the "Ever Given") was traced to a poster who was a mining-equipment enthusiast with no plausible connection to the trafficking narrative later attached to the clip by others — a mismatch between the source's evident interests/expertise and the claim being made about their content is itself a red flag.

**3. Location — does the depicted place match the claimed place?**
Use geolocation techniques: match visible buildings, terrain, signage, and other fixed features against satellite imagery, Street View, and other geo-referenced sources to confirm (or refute) that the event happened where it is claimed to have happened. Bellingcat's broader methodology (referenced elsewhere in its toolkit) treats geolocation as fundamentally an investigator's skill, not a tool's output: **"Tools don't geolocate photos; investigators do."** The recommended method is to identify the single most visually distinctive feature in the frame, search specifically for that feature, use it to narrow the candidate region, then triangulate using one or more secondary features to confirm the match, documenting each step so that the chain of reasoning is reproducible by someone else — directly paralleling the IFCN's "readers should be able to replicate the work" standard.

**4. Time — does the depicted time match the claimed time?**
Chronolocation techniques establish when the image/video was actually captured, as distinct from when it was posted or claimed to depict. Tools such as SunCalc allow an investigator to compute the expected position of the sun and resulting shadows at a candidate date/time/location and compare that to the shadows visible in the media — if the sun/shadow geometry is inconsistent with the claimed date and location, the claim fails this check regardless of how convincing the image otherwise looks.

**5. Motivation — why was this posted, and by whom, with what interest?**
Even once content is confirmed authentic, correctly located, and correctly timed, the guide instructs investigators to still ask why the poster shared it and what bias or agenda might shape the framing — a technically accurate piece of content can still be deployed to support a misleading claim if its motivation/framing is not accounted for.

## Practical techniques cited

- **Keyword/phrase search:** extract any distinctive spoken or written phrase from the content and search it directly. Example: searching the phrase "French police throw down their handcuffs" surfaced the true origin of a viral clip — the footage was real but predated the Covid-19 lockdown protests it was later attached to by roughly nine months, again illustrating the recycled-content failure mode.
- **Detailed frame analysis:** zoom in on visible logos, labels, and text within the shot. Example: a shipping-container video showed "MineARC" and "mineSAFE" branding that, once searched, identified the object as mining safety equipment rather than the shipping container it was claimed to be — a case where close reading of in-frame detail directly refuted the claim.
- **Cross-platform / cross-outlet corroboration:** search for independent coverage of the same purported event across multiple platforms and established news outlets. Example: BBC reporting was used to confirm that French police protests in the relevant clip occurred in June 2020 over policing practices generally, not in response to Covid lockdowns as a viral captioning claimed — official/mainstream corroboration was used to pin down the true context.
- **Manipulation detection:** look for visual tells of digital alteration, including mirrored/reversed images (e.g., text appearing backwards, or a uniform patch appearing on the wrong side of a body). Example given: purported comparison images of Derek Chauvin were shown to be manipulated because arm patches appeared reversed and image quality was inconsistent with an unaltered photo.

## Consolidated red-flag list

The guide's cumulative red flags for treating a piece of content's claimed context with suspicion:
- The posting account has a documented history of sharing conspiracy or hoax content.
- The content lacks any clear attribution or traceable original source.
- The footage appears to be older material being recirculated as breaking/current news.
- Visual anomalies (mirroring, inconsistent lighting, patch/logo placement errors) suggest deliberate or accidental manipulation.
- There is a large, unexplained gap between the content's true original posting date and the date it went viral under a new claim.
- No established, independent news outlet has corroborated the claimed event.
- An existing fact-check organization has already investigated and contradicted the claim.

## Summary checklist form

Bellingcat frames the whole process as a checklist to run, in order, on any piece of social content before it is used as evidence for a claim: confirm originality (not recycled) → identify and vet the source → verify location → verify time → assess motivation → cross-reference against independent news coverage → screen for manipulation/misattribution. A claim is only as well-supported as the weakest link in that chain — e.g., authentic, unmanipulated footage from the correct location but the wrong date still fails to support a claim about a specific current event.
