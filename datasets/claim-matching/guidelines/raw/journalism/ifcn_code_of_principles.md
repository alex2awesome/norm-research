# IFCN Code of Principles (International Fact-Checking Network)
SOURCE_URL: https://ifcncodeofprinciples.poynter.org/the-commitments
DOMAIN: journalism

The International Fact-Checking Network (IFCN), housed at the Poynter Institute, maintains a Code of Principles that signatory fact-checking organizations must follow. Applications from organizations wishing to become verified signatories are assessed by independent assessors against 31 specific criteria derived from the five core commitments below, and that assessment is reviewed by an IFCN advisory board to ensure fairness and consistency across the network. The Code is the operative rubric fact-checkers use to certify that their claim-verification process is trustworthy; it is less a style guide than a set of testable process requirements.

## Commitment 1: A commitment to non-partisanship and fairness

Signatory organizations must fact-check claims using **"the same standard of evidence and judgement for equivalent claims"** regardless of who makes the claim or what position it supports. Concretely, this commitment requires:

- Not concentrating fact-checking efforts on any one side of a debate — coverage should not systematically target one political party, movement, or institution while ignoring equivalent claims from others.
- Following the same verification process for every single fact-check, so that the rigor applied does not vary based on whether a finding is likely to embarrass a particular actor.
- Letting the evidence — not the fact-checker's own views — dictate the conclusion reached.
- Not advocating or taking policy positions on the issues being fact-checked, even on issues where the factual finding is clear-cut.
- Disclosing any conflicts of interest, in sources used or in the fact-checking organization's own affiliations, that could color the assessment of a claim.

This is the criterion an auditor of a claim-matching system would apply first: does the matching procedure (the "standard of evidence") stay constant across claims, or does it flex depending on the claim's source or political valence?

## Commitment 2: A commitment to transparency of sources

This is the most directly actionable principle for claim verification. Signatories must:

- Provide **"enough information for readers to be able to replicate their work themselves"** — meaning every fact-check must disclose exactly which sources were consulted, not merely assert a conclusion.
- Prioritize **primary sources** over secondary reporting on those sources — e.g., a government dataset over a news article citing the dataset, an original court filing over a summary of the filing.
- Verify the **key elements of a claim against more than one named source** whenever more than one relevant, independent source exists. Single-sourcing is acceptable only when no second source is available, and this limitation should be disclosed.
- Name sources whenever it is safe and ethical to do so, rather than relying on unnamed or vague attribution ("experts say"), so that the reader (or a downstream auditor) can independently check the chain of evidence.
- Disclose when a source could not be reached for comment or when the claimant refused to provide substantiating evidence — an evidentiary gap must be surfaced, not silently smoothed over.

For a claim-matching task, this principle operationalizes to: (a) does the matching decision cite specific, named, checkable evidence, rather than an unsupported assertion of match/no-match; (b) is there more than one independent corroborating source for the central factual element being checked; (c) is the source a primary document (data, legal text, transcript) or a secondary account, and is that distinction reflected in how much weight the match is given.

## Commitment 3: A commitment to transparency of funding and organization

Organizations must:

- Disclose all funding sources that account for **5% or more of total revenue**.
- Explain the organizational and ownership structure of the fact-checking outlet.
- Publish a professional biography for anyone who plays a significant role in the editorial output (i.e., anyone whose judgment materially shapes which claims get labeled true/false), so that readers can assess potential bias in the verifier itself.

While this commitment is about the institution rather than the individual claim, it matters for claim-matching because it establishes that the credibility of a verdict is partly a function of the verifier's own disclosed incentives — an evaluator auditing a "match" decision should be able to ask who made the call and whether they had a stake in the outcome.

## Commitment 4: A commitment to transparency of methodology

Signatories must:

- Publish a general, standing methodology statement explaining, in the abstract, how they select claims and how they evaluate them — this should exist independent of any single fact-check, so that the process can be scrutinized before any particular verdict is issued.
- Select claims for checking **"based primarily on the reach and importance of the claim"** rather than on how easy or hard it would be to "win" a fact-check.
- For each individual fact-check, explain the specific methodology used for that piece — the steps taken, not just the conclusion.
- Present **relevant evidence that appears to support the claim as well as relevant evidence that appears to undermine it** — i.e., an explicit accounting of both corroborating and contradicting evidence, rather than a one-sided presentation optimized to justify the final verdict. This is a direct instruction against selectively reporting only the evidence that fits a predetermined rating.
- Make clear whether the piece is checking a literal statement, or the impression/gist that a statement leaves, since these can require different evidentiary standards.

This is the closest analogue in the Code to a formal claim-matching rubric: a verdict is only defensible if the write-up shows both sides of the evidentiary ledger and explains why the balance tips the way it does.

## Commitment 5: A commitment to open and honest corrections

- Organizations must have a corrections policy that is **"easily visible and accessible."**
- When an error is found, the organization must correct it "openly and transparently, seeking as far as possible to ensure that users of the original see the correction" — i.e., a correction is not complete merely because a private edit was made; the original audience must be reachable.
- The correction should explain what was wrong, why, and what has changed, mirroring the transparency-of-methodology requirement applied retroactively.

## Practical implication for claim-matching / verification tasks

Read together, the Code implies a fact-checker (human or automated) should be able to answer, for any claim-evidence pair under evaluation:
1. Was the same evidentiary bar applied here as would be applied to a claim on the "other side"?
2. Which sources were consulted, are they named, and is at least one of them primary?
3. Was more than one independent source used to corroborate the key factual element, where more than one existed?
4. Does the resulting verdict explicitly weigh both supporting and undermining evidence, rather than cherry-picking?
5. Is the reasoning chain transparent enough that an outside party could replicate the check and arrive at the same verdict?

These five questions form a fairly direct checklist against which a claim-matching decision (i.e., "does this evidence support/contradict/fail-to-address this claim") can be scored for rigor, independent of whether the eventual label is correct.
