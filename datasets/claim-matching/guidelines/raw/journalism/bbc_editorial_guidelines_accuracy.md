# BBC Editorial Guidelines — Section 3: Accuracy (Principles and Guidelines)
SOURCE_URL: https://www.bbc.co.uk/editorialguidelines/guidelines/accuracy/guidelines
DOMAIN: journalism

## 3.1 Introduction

The BBC is committed to achieving "due accuracy" in all its output. "Due" means the accuracy must be adequate and appropriate to the output, taking account of the subject and nature of the content, likely audience expectation, and any additional information the BBC provides that may influence that expectation. The due accuracy required of drama, entertainment or comedy is not the same as for most factual content, and can even vary within a genre — e.g., factual entertainment vs. historical documentary vs. current affairs vs. news.

## 3.2 Principles

- All BBC output, as appropriate to its subject and nature, **must be well sourced, based on sound evidence, and where possible corroborated.** Content producers should be honest and open about what they don't know and avoid unfounded speculation.
- **Claims, allegations, material facts and other content that cannot be corroborated should normally be attributed** (i.e., stated as someone's claim, not as established fact).
- The BBC must not knowingly and materially mislead its audiences: no distorting known facts, no presenting invented material as fact.
- Serious factual errors should normally be acknowledged and corrected quickly, clearly and appropriately.
- In news and current affairs, achieving due accuracy is more important than speed.

## 3.3 Mandatory Referrals

Any proposal to rely on a single unnamed source making a serious allegation, or to grant anonymity to a significant contributor making a serious allegation, **must be referred** to the Director of Editorial Policy and Standards and Programme Legal Advice before publication.

## 3.4 Practices (detailed guidance)

### Gathering material
Accuracy is not simply getting facts right — relevant opinions as well as facts may need to be weighed to get at the truth. Where appropriate and possible, content makers should:
- gather material using first-hand sources;
- check facts and statistics, identifying important caveats and limitations;
- **validate the authenticity of documentary evidence and digital material**;
- **corroborate claims and allegations made by contributors**;
- weigh, interpret and contextualise claims, including statistical claims.

Content producers should try to witness events and gather information first-hand; where not possible, talk to first-hand sources and, where practicable, corroborate evidence. **Content producers should be reluctant to rely on a single source.** If they do rely on a single source, it should be credible, and a named, on-the-record source is always preferable. Research interviews with sources making serious allegations should, wherever practicable, be recorded; where recording might inhibit the source, full contemporaneous notes should be made. Information, facts, documents and digital material must be checked and verified to achieve due accuracy — **if content producers have been unable to verify material, they should usually say so and attribute the information.**

### Accuracy in live content
Achieving due accuracy live is challenging because there is little opportunity to verify claims in real time. Where an issue is controversial, risks should be identified in advance and mitigated (sufficient challenge/context prepared, other contributors available to add challenge). Significant inaccuracies that arise live should be corrected quickly.

### Reporting statistics and risk
The same scepticism applied to sourcing should be applied to statistics — they should not be taken at face value. Numbers should be explained, contextualised, weighed, interpreted and, where appropriate, challenged; caveats and limitations should be explained; a range of evidence should be used to judge magnitude and importance; wrong or misleading claims should be challenged.

### User-generated content, social media and other online material
It should not be assumed that user-generated material is accurate — it may need verification and must always be clearly identified. Care is needed with content supplied by anyone with a vested interest, not just disinterested bystanders. Even apparently reliable internet sources may not be accurate or complete — it may be necessary to check who runs the site or confirm authenticity with an individual/organization. Fact must be distinguished from rumour, especially on social media, where misinformation may be deliberate. **Additional scrutiny may be necessary if material from a social media site or other internet source is being used to corroborate a fact.** Material the content producers did not gather themselves should be attributed.

### Material from third parties (including video/audio news releases)
Third-party material — including from other news agencies — needs appropriate caution based on the reputation of the source. The BBC should normally only rely on an agency report if it can be substantiated by one of its own journalists, or if it is sourced from a reputable news agency; such material should only be used if credible and reliable, with appropriate credit/attribution. Content makers should be reluctant to use video/audio news releases or material from parties with vested interests (companies, government agencies, campaigners, charities, etc.) — this can be a route to avoid the scrutiny of an interview and may be inaccurate; any such use must be clearly labeled with who supplied it and the circumstances of production.

### Note-taking
Accurate, reliable, and wherever possible contemporaneous notes of all significant research must be taken and records kept (documents, background notes, emails, social messages), stored so they can be double-checked. **When the BBC broadcasts serious allegations made by an anonymous source, full, timely notes must be kept of the interviews, conversations and information underlying the story.**

### Sources
Sources of information and significant contributors should normally be identified, with credentials made clear so audiences can judge their status. Whenever anonymity is agreed, journalist and source must both understand the scope of the anonymity commitment. When quoting an anonymous source making serious allegations, content makers must protect their identity but give the audience whatever information they can about the source without materially misleading about the source's status. The relevant editor (including the Director-General as editor-in-chief) has the right to know a source's identity in such cases and must keep it confidential.

**Mandatory referral test** for relying on a single unnamed source making a serious allegation, or granting anonymity to a significant contributor making a serious allegation — considers:
- whether the story is of significant public interest;
- whether the source is of proven credibility and reliability and in a position to have sufficient knowledge of the events featured;
- whether a serious allegation was made or substantiated off the record;
- whether a response to the serious allegations has been sought;
- legal issues, safety concerns (e.g., for whistleblowers), and sensitive/personal issues.

The reporting of allegations made by an anonymous source should be scripted carefully to explain (a) the nature of the allegation and (b) that it is being made by an anonymous source, not by the BBC. **When allegations have not been independently corroborated, journalists should consider whether it is appropriate to inform the audience of that fact.** Live unscripted two-ways should not normally be used to report allegations — that decision belongs to the editor.

### Links to third-party websites
Links should normally lead to credible sites; credibility should be assessed before linking, even where editorially justified to link to a significant site in a current story.

### Correcting mistakes
Serious factual errors should be acknowledged and corrected promptly, clearly and appropriately, setting out what was wrong as well as the correction. In online/on-demand content, serious breaches of editorial standards must be corrected in a timely manner (with possible removal in exceptional cases); any mistake in online text content that alters editorial meaning should be corrected and the amendment made clear.
