# Writing your report | Nature Portfolio (Nature Communications reviewer guidelines)
SOURCE_URL: https://www.nature.com/ncomms/for-reviewers/writing-your-report
DOMAIN: academic

This guidance is published by Nature Portfolio for reviewers of Nature Communications and is materially identical across the Nature-branded journals (Nature Methods, Nature Biotechnology, Nature Medicine, Nature Ecology & Evolution, Nature Sustainability, Scientific Reports). It sets out the criteria editors use reviewer reports to assess, and the required structure of a reviewer report.

## Criteria for publication

Editors ask reviewers to keep three factors in mind when writing a report:

- **The quality of the data** — whether the data are technically sound, obtained with appropriate techniques, analysed and interpreted carefully, and presented in sufficient detail.
- **The level of support for the conclusions** — whether sufficiently strong evidence is provided for the authors' claims and all appropriate controls have been included.
- **The potential significance of the results** — whether the results will be important to the field and advance understanding in a way that will move the field forward. (Note that posting of preprints and/or conference proceedings does not compromise novelty.)

The stated primary purpose of review is "to provide feedback on the soundness of the research reported." Reviewers are explicitly told they are not required to make a publication recommendation, only to set out arguments for and against publication if they wish — the evidentiary assessment (does the data support the claim) is separated from the up/down editorial call.

## Elements of a reviewer report

Reviewers are asked to comment on each of the following, in this order:

**Key results** — an overview, in the reviewer's own words, of the key messages of the study, highlighting what the reviewer finds significant or notable. Usually summarized in a short paragraph.

**Validity** — "Your evaluation of the validity and robustness of the data interpretation and conclusions. If you feel there are flaws that prohibit the manuscript's publication, please describe them in detail." This is the core claim-vs-evidence judgment: reviewers are asked directly whether the interpretive leap from data to conclusion is sound.

**Significance** — "Your view on the potential significance of the conclusions for the field and related fields. If you think that other findings in the published literature compromise the manuscript's significance, please provide relevant references." This ties novelty/significance claims to an explicit check against the existing literature — a reviewer who believes a claimed contribution is undercut by prior work is expected to cite it.

**Data and methodology** — assessment of the validity of the approach, the quality of the data, and the quality of presentation, including supplementary information. Reviewers are told: "We ask reviewers to assess all data, including those provided as supplementary information." Reviewers also have "the right to view the data and code that underlie the work if it would help in the evaluation, even if these have not been provided with the submission," and should contact the editor if essential data are not available before submitting the report — i.e., a claim cannot be evaluated, let alone accepted, if its underlying evidence is inaccessible.

**Analytical approach** — assessment of the strength of the analytical approach, "including the validity and comprehensiveness of any statistical tests."

**Suggested improvements** — suggestions for additional experiments or data that would strengthen the work, explicitly scoped: "Suggestions should be limited to the present scope of the manuscript; that is, they should only include what can be reasonably addressed in a revision and exclude what would significantly change the scope of the work."

**Clarity and context** — "whether the results have been provided with sufficient context and consideration of previous work." Reviewers are explicitly told not to comment on language/grammar issues here.

**References** — "Your view on whether the manuscript references previous literature appropriately." This is treated as a distinct evaluative dimension from "clarity and context," signaling that citation appropriateness (are prior claims cited, and cited accurately/fairly) is its own checkable criterion.

**Your expertise** — reviewers are asked to flag any part of the manuscript, data, or analyses that falls outside their expertise or that they were unable to assess fully — an explicit scope-of-review disclosure so editors know which claims went unchecked.

## Providing constructive feedback

Reviewers are asked to approach review "with a sincere intention to help the authors improve their manuscripts," to be direct but respectful, and to give specific, actionable feedback rather than vague criticism. Nature states a strict non-censorship policy: "we do not censor the content of reviewer reports; any comments that were intended for the authors are transmitted, regardless of what we may think of the content," except to remove offensive language or information that reveals confidential matters.

## Confidential comments to editors

Confidential comments to the editor are reserved for sensitive information (e.g., potential ethical issues, prior interactions with the manuscript at another venue) and must never contradict the comments given to the authors — i.e., a reviewer cannot privately flag an unsupported claim to the editor while telling the authors the claim is fine.

## Practical implication for claim-matching

The Nature Portfolio structure explicitly separates four distinct checks relevant to claim-evidence matching: (1) whether the *data itself* is sound ("quality of the data"), (2) whether the *inference from data to conclusion* is valid ("Validity"), (3) whether the *significance/novelty claim* survives comparison to the existing literature ("Significance" + "References"), and (4) whether *sufficient evidence/controls* back the specific claims made ("level of support for the conclusions"). A reviewer flagging a claim-evidence mismatch is expected to locate it within this four-part decomposition rather than as an undifferentiated "I don't believe this."
