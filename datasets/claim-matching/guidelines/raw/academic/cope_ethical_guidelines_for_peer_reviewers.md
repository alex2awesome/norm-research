# COPE Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers (COPE Council, Version 2, September 2017)
SOURCE_URL: https://publicationethics.org/guidance/guideline/ethical-guidelines-peer-reviewers
DOMAIN: academic

COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics) guidelines are the cross-publisher ethical baseline most journals point reviewers to (PLOS, Elsevier, and others explicitly defer to COPE on ethics questions). Unlike the journal-specific reviewer guides, COPE's document is oriented mainly around reviewer conduct and integrity rather than a technical criteria checklist — but it contains explicit, directly relevant instructions on how claims must be substantiated in a review report, and on scoping additional-evidence requests to specific claims.

## Conducting a review — initial steps

"Read the manuscript, supplementary data files and ancillary material thoroughly (e.g., reviewer instructions, required ethics and policy statements), getting back to the journal if anything is not clear and requesting any missing or incomplete items you need." Reviewers should understand the scope of review expected before starting (e.g., whether review of raw data is expected) — i.e., verifying claims may require access to underlying data, and reviewers should ask for it if it is missing.

## Preparing a report — substantiating your own claims about the manuscript

This is COPE's most direct claim-evidence instruction, but aimed reflexively at the reviewer's own report: "Be objective and constructive in your review, providing feedback that will help the authors to improve their manuscript. For example, **be specific in your critique, and provide supporting evidence with appropriate references to substantiate general statements**, to help editors in their evaluation." A reviewer asserting that a claim is unsupported is itself expected to substantiate that assertion with specifics (line numbers, contradicting citations, etc.), not merely assert disbelief.

"Be professional and refrain from being hostile or inflammatory and from making libellous or derogatory personal comments or unfounded accusations."

## Appropriate feedback

"Bear in mind that the editor requires a fair, honest, and unbiased assessment of the strengths and weaknesses of the manuscript... Ensure your comments and recommendations for the editor are consistent with your report for the authors; most feedback should be put in the report that the authors will see." Any accept/revise/reject recommendation "should be congruent with the comments provided in the review" — i.e., a reviewer cannot privately flag an unsupported claim while publicly endorsing the paper's conclusions.

## Suggestions for further work — scoping evidence requests to specific claims

"It is the job of the peer reviewer to comment on the quality and rigour of the work they receive. If the work is not clear because of missing analyses, the reviewer should comment and explain what additional analyses would clarify the work submitted. It is not the job of the reviewer to extend the work beyond its current scope. **Be clear which (if any) suggested additional investigations are essential to support claims made in the manuscript under consideration and which will just strengthen or extend the work.**" This is an explicit instruction to distinguish "this evidence is required to justify what you are claiming" from "this would be a nice addition" — a load-bearing distinction for any claim-matching rubric that wants to separate necessary from merely-desirable evidence.

## Accountability in citation suggestions

"Refrain from making unfair negative comments or including unjustified criticisms of any competitors' work that is mentioned in the manuscript. Refrain from suggesting that authors include citations to your (or an associate's) work merely to increase citation counts or to enhance the visibility of your or your associate's work; suggestions must be based on valid academic or technological reasons." — citation-related feedback (including asking for additional citations) must itself be substantively justified, not self-interested.

## Bias and competing interests (relevant to fair claim assessment)

"It is important to remain unbiased by considerations related to the nationality, religious or political beliefs, gender or other characteristics of the authors, origins of a manuscript or by commercial considerations." Reviewers who suspect their own competing interest may color their assessment of a manuscript's claims should notify the journal.

## Suspicion of ethics violations

If a reviewer suspects "that misconduct occurred during either the research or the writing and submission of the manuscript, or you may notice substantial similarity between the manuscript and a concurrent submission to another journal or a published article," they should contact the editor directly rather than investigate independently — relevant where a claim-evidence mismatch might indicate fabrication/falsification rather than ordinary overclaiming, which is a matter for the editor rather than the reviewer to pursue.

## Models of peer review (context)

COPE's guidelines note that peer review varies along independent axes — timing (preprint / pre-publication / post-publication), identifiability (double-blind / single-blind / open), mediation, publication of the review itself, and ownership — which affects how directly a reviewer's claim-evidence assessment becomes part of the public record (e.g., open, published, signed review vs. confidential single-blind review).

## Scope note

This document is process/ethics-oriented rather than a technical review checklist; it is included here because journal-specific guides (PLOS, Elsevier) explicitly incorporate it by reference for ethics questions, and because its "substantiate general statements" and "essential to support claims" language are the clearest cross-publisher articulation of the standard a reviewer's own claim-evidence critique must meet.
