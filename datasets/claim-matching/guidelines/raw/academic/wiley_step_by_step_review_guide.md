# Step by Step Guide to Reviewing a Manuscript | Wiley
SOURCE_URL: https://authors.wiley.com/Reviewers/journal-reviewers/how-to-perform-a-peer-review/step-by-step-guide-to-reviewing-a-manuscript.html
DOMAIN: academic

Wiley's reviewer guide is one of the most granular publicly available walkthroughs of how a reviewer should check a manuscript section by section, with explicit sub-criteria for whether conclusions match evidence and whether citations actually support the statements attached to them.

## First read-through: key considerations

- **Research relevance and originality**: "Ask yourself if the main question addressed by the research is interesting, relevant, and original. How does the manuscript contribute to the field compared to existing studies?"
- **Clarity and quality**: "Evaluate whether the authors' conclusions are well-supported by the evidence presented. If the author disagrees significantly with the current academic consensus, do they have a substantial case? If not, what would be required to make their case credible?" — this frames disagreement-with-consensus claims as needing a higher evidentiary bar, and asks the reviewer to specify the missing evidence rather than just reject the claim.
- **Data presentation**: assess whether tables/figures/images "effectively support the manuscript's findings or if they seem unnecessary."

## Identifying major flaws

Explicitly listed flaw category: **"Conclusions contradicting the evidence"** — "Are the conclusions supported by the data, or do they contradict the author's own statistical or qualitative data?" Other major-flaw categories: outdated/unreliable methods, and "overlooking key processes" known to influence the area under study.

For experimental design, reviewers check: sampling methods, sufficiency of control experiments, precision of process data, regularity of sampling in time-dependent studies, and (for qualitative work) whether "the data analysis [is] being done systematically" and whether the research "extends beyond the author's opinions, with sufficient descriptive elements and appropriate quotes."

Major flaws in the data itself: "Insufficient data," "Unclear data tables," "Contradictory data that either are not self-consistent or disagree with the conclusions," and "Confirmatory data that adds little, if anything, to current understanding — unless strong arguments for such repetition are made."

## Section-by-section guidance

**Introduction — originality and novelty claims**: "Originality and topicality can only be established in the light of recent authoritative research. For example, it's impossible to argue that there is a conflict in current understanding by referencing articles that are 10 years old." A claim that "this gap hasn't been addressed" is only credible if backed by recent citations — though citing older seminal papers for genuinely foundational methodology is fine. Reviewers should check that stated research aims are not "a surprise" relative to the introduction — i.e., the claimed contribution should be foreshadowed, not sprung on the reader.

**Materials and Methods**: research should be *replicable* (sufficient controls, repeated analyses, adequate sampling "to confirm that observed trends are not due to chance" — otherwise "statistical analyses will not be sound"), *repeatable* (enough procedural detail for others to redo it), and *robust* (sufficient data points; unaddressed biases should be flagged). "Where research is not replicable, the paper should be recommended for rejection."

**Results and discussion**: good reporting pattern is (1) describe in simple terms what the data show, (2) reference the statistical analyses (significance, goodness of fit), (3) "evaluate the trends observed and explain the significance of the results to wider understanding. This can only be done by referencing published research" — i.e., a significance claim requires an explicit tie to the literature, not just internal statistics. "The outcome should be a critical analysis of the data collected."

**Conclusions**: "should reflect upon the aims — whether they were achieved or not — and, just like the aims, should not be surprising. If the conclusions are not evidence-based, it's appropriate to ask for them to be re-written." This is the guide's most direct claim-matching instruction: a conclusion untethered to the presented evidence should be sent back for revision, not merely criticized.

**Images, graphs and data tables**: reviewers should check that "the trends you can see support the paper's discussion and conclusions" and that "there is sufficient data" (e.g., enough data points across a time series to support the described trend). Reviewers should also watch for undisclosed image editing/manipulation that would misrepresent what the data show, flagging this confidentially to the editor.

## References: accuracy, adequacy, balance

This is the guide's most explicit citation-verification checklist, directly relevant to whether "cited references actually support the statements they're attached to":

- **Accuracy**: "Where a cited article is central to the author's argument, you should check the accuracy and format of the reference." (Full exhaustive reference-list checking is the editor's job, not the reviewer's.)
- **Adequacy**: "Assess the adequacy of the references by determining if they adequately support the manuscript's claims and if there are any significant studies, both similar and opposing, that should be cited but are missing. A reference list that is too sparse or relies heavily on a limited number of sources may indicate that additional references are needed, though avoid judging solely by quantity."
- **Balance**: "Check for a well-balanced list of references that is helpful to the reader, fair to competing authors, and not over-reliant on self-citation. The references should recognize the initial discoveries and related work that led to the work under assessment." Reviewers are told they "should be able to evaluate the balance of the references without looking up every single citation but should raise any concerns if certain perspectives seem underrepresented."

## Plagiarism / undisclosed prior work

If a reviewer identifies a very similar prior paper the authors overlooked, "you may advise the author how to emphasize the novel aspects of their own study, to better differentiate it from similar research" or ask them "to discuss their aims and results, or modify their conclusions, considering a similar article." If overlap is severe enough to make the work unoriginal, rejection may be the only option.

## How to structure the report — major issues checklist

- "Are there any major flaws? State what they are and what the severity of their impact is on the paper."
- "Has similar work already been published without the authors acknowledging this?"
- "Are the authors presenting findings that challenge current thinking? Is the evidence they present strong enough to prove their case? Have they cited all the relevant work that would contradict their thinking and addressed it appropriately?" — an explicit instruction to check that contrary evidence is engaged with, not omitted.
- Minor issues include: "Are the correct references cited? If not, which should be cited instead/also? Are citations excessive, limited, or biased?" and "Are there any factual, numerical or unit errors?"

## Recommendation categories

Accept / Revise (major or minor) / Reject, with the instruction that revision requests should state specific required changes so authors can respond point by point, and rejection should still include constructive, non-personal feedback on the research (not the author).
