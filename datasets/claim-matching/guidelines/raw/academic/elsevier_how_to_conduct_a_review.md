# How to conduct a review | Elsevier
SOURCE_URL: https://www.elsevier.com/reviewer/how-to-review/checklist
DOMAIN: academic

Elsevier's reviewer guide walks through a structured, section-by-section evaluation of an "original research article" submission, with explicit instructions to justify judgments by reference to the data/evidence rather than opinion, and to check conclusions against study design.

## Structuring the review: the core instruction

"Providing insight into any deficiencies is important. You should explain and support your judgement so that both editors and authors are able to fully understand the reasoning behind your comments. **You should indicate whether your comments are your own opinion or are reflected by the data and evidence.**" This is an explicit meta-instruction: reviewers must mark which of their criticisms are evidence-grounded claim-mismatches versus personal/stylistic preference.

## Section-by-section evaluation checklist (original research articles)

**Title**: "Evaluate whether the title accurately and sufficiently describes the content." For certain study types (e.g., RCTs), the study type should be named in the title.

**Abstract**: "Assess whether the abstract accurately reflects all essential aspects of the study, including all major results and limitations." — a direct instruction to check that the headline claims in the abstract are actually borne out in (and not overstated relative to) the body of the paper.

**Introduction**: background/literature should be up to date and relevant; "the introduction should clearly explain the scientific background and rationale of the study," and the primary/secondary objectives should be clearly stated.

**Methods**: sufficient detail "to enable replication and reproducibility"; check the experimental design type (RCT, case-control, cohort, cross-sectional), patient population and sample-size estimation, and a "literature-backed explanation for the minimal clinically important difference (MCID)." Statistical methods (controls, sampling mechanism, p-values, CIs, effect sizes) "should be appropriately described."

**Results**: verify the presentation (tables/figures) is appropriate to visualize the findings, and examine whether additional sub-analyses or statistical measures (CIs, effect sizes, sensitivity analyses) are needed to support what is being claimed.

**Discussion** — the core claim-matching checkpoint: "**The interpretation of the results and study conclusions should be supported by the data and study design.**" Reviewers should also "assess whether the authors have clearly emphasized the limitations of their study, including the theory, methods, and argument" — i.e., appropriate hedging/scope-limiting is itself a checked criterion, not merely a courtesy.

**References**: "Scrutinize whether the references used in this study are the most up-to-date and relevant for the topic at hand."

**Overall**: "state if this paper presents research that is novel or important [in some other aspect e.g., exhibits reproducibility] for the journal or for the field."

## Review-article-specific criteria (relevant to synthesis claims)

For review articles, Elsevier asks reviewers to separately evaluate: whether the rationale for writing the review is justified, whether the review is a comprehensive overview of the field's historical development and future directions, whether the reference list adequately covers "the most relevant and recent advances in the field, including coverage of all or most relevant research groups," and — where meta-analysis/meta-regression is used — whether the statistical methods are appropriate and clearly reported (p-values, 95% CIs), with an explicit suggestion that a statistician co-reviewer may be warranted.

## Data and visualization checks

"Once you are satisfied that the methodology is sufficiently robust, examine any data in the form of figures, tables, or images." Critical/major flaws in research data are defined as: "insufficient data points, statistically non-significant variations and unclear data tables."

## Best-practice DO / DON'T list (most directly reusable as a checklist)

DO:
- "Summarize the article in a short paragraph" to demonstrate understanding.
- "Give your main impressions of the article, including whether it is novel and interesting, has a sufficient impact and adds to the knowledge base."
- "Assess whether the article conforms to the journal-specific instructions."
- "Does the title accurately reflect the content? Is the abstract complete and stand-alone?"
- "Keep your comments strictly factual and don't speculate on the motives of the author(s)."
- "Carefully review the methodology, statistical errors, results, conclusion/discussion, and references."
- "Consider feedback on the presentation of data in the article, the sustainability and reproducibility of any methodology, the analysis of any data, and **whether the conclusions are supported by the data**."
- Raise suspicion of plagiarism or research falsification with the editor, with supporting detail; consult COPE guidelines.
- Be alert to one's own unconscious bias.

DON'T:
- Comment on spelling/grammar/layout if the underlying research is sound (recommend language editing to the editor instead).
- Make ad hominem comments.
- "Dismiss alternative viewpoints or theories that might conflict with your own opinions on a topic; when reviewing, maintain an open perspective." — a direct guard against a reviewer's own priors substituting for an evidence-based claim check.
- Suggest citations to the reviewer's own work "unless for genuine scientific reasons and not with the intention of increasing citation counts."

## Generative-AI policy (relevant to how claim-checking is expected to be done)

Elsevier's policy states plainly that "the critical thinking and assessment required for peer-review are outside the scope of generative AI and AI-assisted technologies, and there is a risk that the technology will generate incorrect, incomplete or biased conclusions." Reviewers may not upload manuscript content to AI tools; AI may be used only in a supportive capacity (e.g., improving report language, background literature search) with disclosure, and never to perform the substantive evidence-claim assessment itself.

## Decision recommendation categories

Reject, Accept without revision, or Revise (major/minor) — reviewers are told that if recommending revision, they should expect and agree to a follow-up review of the authors' response to their specific comments.
