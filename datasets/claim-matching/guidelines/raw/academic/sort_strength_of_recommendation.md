# Strength of Recommendation Taxonomy (SORT): A Patient-Centered Approach to Grading Evidence in the Medical Literature
SOURCE_URL: https://www.aafp.org/afp/2004/0201/p548
DOMAIN: academic

SORT (Ebell et al., American Family Physician / JABFM, 2004) is a taxonomy jointly adopted by U.S. family medicine journals (American Family Physician, Journal of Family Practice, BMJ USA) for grading how strongly a clinical recommendation is supported by evidence, built around a single organizing question that generalizes well beyond medicine: **does the evidence being cited actually measure the outcome the claim is about, and is it good enough, consistent enough, and abundant enough to support the strength of the claim being made?** Unlike frameworks that grade evidence primarily on study design, SORT's first and most consequential filter is the *kind of outcome measured*, before even considering design or quality.

## Step 1: Patient-oriented vs. disease-oriented outcomes (the outcome-type filter)

- **Patient-oriented outcomes**: "outcomes that matter to patients and help them live longer or better lives, including reduced morbidity, reduced mortality, symptom improvement, improved quality of life, or lower cost."
- **Disease-oriented outcomes**: "intermediate, histopathologic, physiologic, or surrogate results (e.g., blood sugar, blood pressure, flow rate, coronary plaque thickness) that may or may not reflect improvement in patient outcomes."

This distinction is load-bearing: **a recommendation based solely on disease-oriented (surrogate) evidence is automatically capped at the lowest strength grade (C), regardless of how rigorous, large, or consistent the underlying studies are.** The taxonomy's worked example is doxazosin, which reliably lowered blood pressure (a disease-oriented outcome) in trials, yet increased mortality (the patient-oriented outcome that actually matters) — illustrating why surrogate-outcome evidence, however well-supported on its own terms, cannot license a strong claim about patient benefit.

## Step 2: Individual study evidence levels (1, 2, 3)

Once outcome type is established, individual studies (or reviews of studies) are leveled:

- **Level 1**: Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), or systematic reviews of RCTs, with consistent findings, measuring patient-oriented outcomes.
- **Level 2**: Cohort studies, case-control studies, systematic reviews of these designs, or lower-quality/limited RCTs — still measuring patient-oriented outcomes.
- **Level 3**: Case series, cross-sectional studies, case reports, or **any study design (including RCTs) if it measures only disease-oriented/surrogate outcomes**. Note that a study's design alone does not guarantee a level above 3 — measuring the wrong (surrogate) outcome demotes it regardless of design rigor.

## Step 3: Combining quality, quantity, and consistency into a recommendation grade (A, B, C)

SORT explicitly names three elements that must be jointly assessed to grade the *strength of a recommendation* (not just an individual study):

1. **Quality** — validity of the studies, i.e., how well they minimize bias.
2. **Quantity** — the number of studies and total number of subjects.
3. **Consistency** — how similar the findings are across the available studies on the same question.

These combine into three recommendation grades:

- **Grade A** — "Recommendation based on consistent and good-quality patient-oriented evidence." Requires either multiple consistent Level 1 studies, or a single large, well-designed Level 1 study conducted in a sufficiently diverse population.
- **Grade B** — "Recommendation based on inconsistent or limited-quality patient-oriented evidence." Applies when the evidence is consistent Level 2–3 studies, or Level 1 studies that disagree with each other (inconsistency), or when there is simply a limited quantity of otherwise good-quality evidence.
- **Grade C** — "Recommendation based on consensus, usual practice, opinion, disease-oriented evidence, or case series (for studies of diagnosis, treatment, prevention, or screening)." This grade is a catch-all floor for: evidence that is only Level 3; evidence based exclusively on disease-oriented/surrogate outcomes (irrespective of quality); or claims resting on expert consensus without a systematic evidentiary base.

## The critical caveat (outcome type trumps quality)

The taxonomy is explicit that a recommendation resting on disease-oriented evidence — however excellent its internal quality and however consistent across studies — **cannot exceed Grade C**. This is the single most actionable, transferable rule from SORT for claim-evidence matching: before asking "how good is the evidence," first ask "does the evidence actually measure the outcome the claim asserts," because a well-supported claim about a *surrogate* is not equivalent to (and cannot license) a strongly-worded claim about the *outcome that actually matters*. The doxazosin example generalizes directly: any body of evidence, no matter how large, well-designed, or unanimous, that supports claim X (surrogate) does not thereby strongly support a distinct claim Y (patient-relevant outcome) unless outcome Y was itself what was actually measured.

## Actionable takeaways for claim-evidence matching

1. **Check outcome identity first**: does the cited evidence measure the same outcome the claim asserts, or a proxy/surrogate for it? A claim about outcome Y evidenced only by data on proxy Z is capped at the lowest support grade regardless of study count or quality.
2. **Then check design**: is the evidence RCT-level (Level 1), observational (Level 2), or merely descriptive/case-based (Level 3)?
3. **Then check quantity and consistency**: do multiple studies exist, and do they agree? A single well-powered Level 1 study can support a strong grade; several small or disagreeing Level 1 studies cannot.
4. **Only after all three checks** (outcome type → design level → quantity/consistency) assign the overall strength grade (A/B/C) — treating any one of these three as sufficient on its own is the taxonomy's core cautionary lesson (illustrated by drugs that "worked" on the disease-oriented metric but harmed patients on the outcome that mattered).
