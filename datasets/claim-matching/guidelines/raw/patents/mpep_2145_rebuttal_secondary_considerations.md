# MPEP 2145 — Consideration of Applicant's Rebuttal Arguments and Evidence
SOURCE_URL: https://www.uspto.gov/web/offices/pac/mpep/s2145.html
DOMAIN: patents

## Overview

Once a prima facie case of obviousness is established (MPEP 2142), the burden shifts to the applicant to rebut it with arguments and/or evidence. MPEP 2145 catalogs the recurring rebuttal arguments examiners see, explains which succeed and which fail as a matter of law, and sets out the treatment of secondary considerations. This is the doctrinal counterpart to the rationales in MPEP 2143 — it tells you what actually **defeats** a claim-vs-prior-art obviousness match once the prima facie mapping has been made.

## I. Argument Does Not Replace Evidence

"An assertion of what seems to follow from common experience is just attorney argument and not the kind of factual evidence that is required to rebut a prima facie case of obviousness." Attorney argument alone, without evidentiary support (declarations, data, etc.), cannot overcome a rejection where evidence is required (e.g., claims of unexpected results).

## II. Additional Advantages or Latent Properties

Recognizing a latent property or extra advantage that "flows naturally" from following the prior art's suggestion does not make an otherwise-obvious invention nonobvious. Merely noticing a benefit the prior art would have produced anyway is not patentable contribution.

## III. Physical Combinability of Prior Art Devices

Obviousness does not require that the prior art devices be capable of *actual physical* bodily combination — the question is whether the *teachings* would have rendered the claim obvious. The one limit: the proposed combination cannot change the principle of operation of the primary reference or render it inoperable for its intended purpose.

## IV. Arguing Against References Individually

"One cannot show nonobviousness by attacking references individually where the rejections are based on combinations of references." If a rejection combines Reference 1 (showing limitations A, B) with Reference 2 (showing limitation C), an argument that "Reference 2 doesn't show A or B" is not responsive — the correct attack is that Reference 2 doesn't show C, that Reference 1 doesn't show A or B, or that there is no reason to combine them.

## V. Number of References Combined

The sheer number of references combined, standing alone, does not weigh against obviousness. Courts have upheld rejections based on many combined references where each contributes an articulated element and reason.

## VI. Arguing Unclaimed Limitations

Claims are construed by their own express language; an applicant cannot rely on advantages or features disclosed in the specification but not actually recited in the claims to distinguish over the prior art.

## VII. Economic Infeasibility

That a combination "would not be made by businessmen for economic reasons" does not establish technical non-obviousness — economic impracticality is not the same as technological incompatibility, and a PHOSITA is assumed to make the combination regardless of whether it would be commercially attractive.

## VIII. Age of References

The mere age of a reference does not establish nonobviousness. It becomes probative only when combined with evidence that the art tried and failed to solve the problem despite having long known of the reference (a species of the "failure of others" secondary consideration).

## IX. Nonanalogous Prior Art

An applicant may argue a cited reference is not "analogous art" (see MPEP 2141.01's two-prong test — same field, or reasonably pertinent to the problem). This is a legitimate rebuttal but requires a fact-specific showing, not a bare assertion that the reference comes from a "different industry."

## X. Improper Rationales for Combining References — What the Examiner Must Avoid

- **(A) Impermissible hindsight.** Some hindsight reconstruction is inherent in any post-hoc obviousness analysis and is not automatically improper — the touchstone is whether the reasoning draws only on knowledge that was within ordinary skill *at the time of invention* (not knowledge gained from the applicant's own disclosure) — an explicit written motivation in the prior art is not mandatory.
- **(B) Obvious to try.** Valid where the record shows a finite number of identified, predictable solutions with a reasonable expectation of success (see MPEP 2143.I.E); invalid where it would require trying "numerous" alternatives with no guidance on likelihood of success.
- **(C) Lack of an explicit suggestion to combine.** TSM in the prior art is one valid rationale (Rationale G) but not the only one — common-sense reasoning consistent with the Graham factors can substitute for it.
- **(D) Teaching away.** A reference that actually teaches away from the claimed subject matter weighs significantly against obviousness, and proceeding contrary to accepted wisdom in the art is itself evidence of nonobviousness. But **mere disclosure of more than one alternative is not, by itself, a teaching away** from any of them — teaching away requires actual criticism, discredit, or discouragement of the specific option claimed.
- **(E) KSR applies across all technologies.** Examiners should not withdraw an obviousness rejection solely because the technology is traditionally viewed as "unpredictable" — *KSR*'s flexible rationales apply in unpredictable arts too, though the predictability finding itself will naturally be harder to sustain there.

## Secondary Considerations and the Nexus Requirement

Rebuttal evidence may include objective indicia of nonobviousness ("secondary considerations"): commercial success, long-felt but unsolved need, failure of others, unexpected results, copying by competitors, licensing activity, praise, and skepticism by experts.

**Nexus is required.** For any secondary consideration to carry substantial weight, the applicant must show a legally and factually sufficient connection between the evidence and the *claimed* invention specifically — not to unclaimed features, and not to external market factors unrelated to the claimed advance. A presumption of nexus arises when the product both **embodies** the claimed invention and is **coextensive** with it (i.e., is not merely one embodiment among many broader unclaimed features driving the success). The evidence must be reasonably commensurate in scope with the claim; showing superior properties across the entire claimed range is not always required — a persuasive showing within a representative portion may suffice if a PHOSITA could reasonably extend the probative value across the range.

## Weighing Rebuttal Evidence — The Final Balancing

- Examiners must consider **all** timely-submitted rebuttal evidence, though not necessarily give it equal weight; the guidance explicitly warns against giving *no* weight to submitted evidence except in rare circumstances.
- The final determination applies a **preponderance-of-the-evidence** standard: an obviousness rejection should be made or maintained **only if** the evidence of obviousness *outweighs* the evidence of nonobviousness on the whole record.
- The mere presence of *some* credible rebuttal evidence does not automatically require withdrawing a rejection — a strong prima facie case (well-articulated rationale, closely mapped elements, clear predictability finding) can outweigh even substantial secondary-considerations evidence if the nexus is weak or the prima facie showing is otherwise compelling.
- "Mere recognition of latent properties in the prior art does not render nonobvious an otherwise known invention" (restating Section II above as a general weighing principle).

## Practical Takeaway for Claim-Matching

When scoring or reconstructing an obviousness determination, the relevant rebuttal-side checklist is: (1) is the reference combination attacked as a whole, not piecemeal; (2) is any claimed advantage actually recited in the claim language; (3) is there a genuine teaching-away, not just disclosure of alternatives; (4) is any secondary-considerations evidence tied by nexus to the specific claimed delta, not to unclaimed or generic market factors; and (5) does the totality of the record — prima facie showing versus rebuttal — favor obviousness or nonobviousness on a preponderance standard.
