# MPEP § 2112 — Requirements of Rejection Based on Inherency; Burden of Proof
SOURCE_URL: https://www.uspto.gov/web/offices/pac/mpep/s2112.html
DOMAIN: patents

This section governs the standard for when a claim limitation that is *not expressly stated* in a prior art reference may nonetheless count as disclosed because it is **inherent** in what the reference does disclose. Inherency is one of the two channels (express + inherent) recognized by MPEP § 2131's "each and every element" test, so it is central to deciding whether a reference "matches" a claim limitation it never states outright.

## Doctrinal framework: inherent disclosure supports both § 102 and § 103

Express, implicit, and inherent disclosures of the prior art may all be relied upon to reject claims under either 35 U.S.C. 102 (anticipation) or 35 U.S.C. 103 (obviousness). Whether a limitation is inherently taught by a reference is a **question of fact**:

> *In re Napier*, 55 F.3d 610, 613, 34 USPQ2d 1782, 1784 (Fed. Cir. 1995).

## I. Discovery of a new property in an old thing does not create patentability

If the prior art already discloses a structure/composition/process, later discovery of a previously-unappreciated property or scientific explanation of *why* it works does not make the old thing patentably new — the property was always inherently there, whether or not anyone had noticed:

> "[T]he discovery of a previously unappreciated property of a prior art composition, or of a scientific explanation for the prior art's functioning, does not render the old composition patentably new to the discoverer." — *Atlas Powder Co. v. IRECO Inc.*, 190 F.3d 1342, 1347, 51 USPQ2d 1943, 1946 (Fed. Cir. 1999).

Similarly, claiming a new *use* of an old structure, where the "new" use is simply an inherent, previously-unknown property of that old structure, does not necessarily confer patentability. *In re Best*, 562 F.2d 1252, 195 USPQ 430 (CCPA 1977).

## II. Contemporaneous recognition by skilled artisans is NOT required for inherency

This is a critical and often counter-intuitive matching rule: a characteristic can be treated as inherently disclosed by a reference even if no person of ordinary skill at the time would have recognized that the reference possessed it. The test is whether the characteristic is **in fact** necessarily present — not whether anyone at the time understood that it was:

> *Schering Corp. v. Geneva Pharm. Inc.*, 339 F.3d 1373, 1377, 67 USPQ2d 1664, 1668 (Fed. Cir. 2003): "the discovery that a claim limitation is inherent in the prior art is not the same as the recognition of the limitation by a person of ordinary skill in the art at the relevant time... the fact that a characteristic is a necessary feature or result of a prior-art embodiment ... is enough for inherent anticipation, even if that fact was unknown at the time of the prior invention."

**Matching implication:** for anticipation purposes, "did the reference in fact necessarily produce/possess X" is the question — not "would a reader of the reference have realized it produced/possessed X."

## III. Concurrent § 102 / § 103 rejections are permitted

Where a prior art product or process appears structurally/procedurally substantially identical to the claimed one, but the reference is silent as to whether it explicitly discloses the claimed function/property, an examiner may make concurrent anticipation and obviousness rejections without inconsistency:

> "There is nothing inconsistent in concurrent rejections..." — *In re Best*, 562 F.2d 1252 (CCPA 1977).

## IV. The examiner must give a reasoned basis for an inherency finding — mere possibility is not enough

A finding of inherent disclosure cannot rest on speculation that the claimed characteristic *might* be present in the prior art. The examiner must articulate "a basis in fact and/or technical reasoning" that reasonably supports the conclusion that the allegedly inherent characteristic **necessarily** flows from what the reference teaches:

> *Ex parte Levy*, 17 USPQ2d 1461, 1464 (Bd. Pat. App. & Inter. 1990).

The Federal Circuit has emphasized that inherency doctrine must be applied narrowly, particularly in the obviousness context — the limitation must necessarily be present, or be the natural/inevitable result of combining the explicitly disclosed elements, not merely a possible or probable result:

> *PAR Pharmaceutical, Inc. v. TWI Pharmaceuticals, Inc.*, 773 F.3d 1186, 1195-96 (Fed. Cir. 2014) (inherency's "use ... must be carefully circumscribed in the context of obviousness"; the limitation "necessarily must be present, or the natural result of the combination of elements explicitly disclosed by the prior art").

## V. Structural/procedural identity as a proxy for inherency; burden-shifting

Where the reference and the claim describe **substantially identical structures/products/processes**, courts and examiners may presume the inherent performance of the claimed function without independent proof, because identical structures necessarily behave identically:

> *In re Schreiber*, 128 F.3d 1473, 1477-78, 44 USPQ2d 1429, 1431 (Fed. Cir. 1997) (conical dispensing tops for popcorn/oil containers — the prior art dispenser inherently was capable of the claimed dispensing function because its structure was substantially identical).

**Once a reference disclosing a substantially identical product/process is identified**, and the examiner supplies evidence or sound technical reasoning supporting inherency, the burden of proof shifts to the applicant to show the prior art product does *not* necessarily possess the claimed characteristic:

> *In re Best*, 562 F.2d 1252, 1255, 195 USPQ 430, 433-34 (CCPA 1977) ("Where the Patent Office has reason to believe that a functional limitation ... may, in fact, be an inherent characteristic of the prior art, it possesses the authority to require an applicant to prove that the subject matter shown to be in the prior art does not possess the characteristic relied on."); see also *In re Spada*, 911 F.2d 705, 708, 15 USPQ2d 1655, 1658 (Fed. Cir. 1990) ("When the PTO shows a sound basis for believing that the products of the applicant and the prior art are the same, the applicant has the burden of showing that they are not.").

This burden-shifting framework parallels the treatment of product-by-process claims. *In re Fitzgerald*, 619 F.2d 67, 205 USPQ 594 (CCPA 1980).

## § 2112.01 — Composition, product, and apparatus claims

**Identical/substantially identical structure ⇒ presumed identical properties.** Where the claimed and prior art products (or the processes that produce them) are identical or substantially identical in structure/composition, a prima facie case of anticipation/obviousness is established without the examiner needing to separately prove every recited property:

> *In re Best*, 562 F.2d 1252 (CCPA 1977).

For chemical compositions specifically: "Products of identical chemical composition can not have mutually exclusive properties." Identical chemical structure necessarily entails identical properties as a matter of chemistry — a claimed "new" property cannot distinguish an otherwise chemically identical prior art composition.

> *In re Spada*, 911 F.2d 705, 709, 15 USPQ2d 1655, 1658 (Fed. Cir. 1990).

**Printed matter carve-out.** Nonfunctional printed matter (e.g., an instruction label) that is not functionally related to the substrate/product does not distinguish an otherwise identical prior art product from the claim — otherwise, patentees could perpetually re-patent an identical product merely by adding new printed instructions:

> *In re Ngai*, 367 F.3d 1336, 1339, 70 USPQ2d 1862, 1864 (Fed. Cir. 2004) ("If we were to adopt Ngai's position, anyone could continue patenting a product indefinitely provided that they add a new instruction sheet.").

## § 2112.02 — Process claims

**Device performing the claimed process in normal operation ⇒ anticipation.** If a prior art device, in its ordinary/normal mode of operation, necessarily carries out the steps of a claimed process, the process claim is anticipated — even absent an explicit description of that mode of use in the reference. Where the claimed apparatus for practicing a process is identical to a prior art apparatus known to perform that process, a presumption of anticipation arises.

> *In re King*, 801 F.2d 1324, 231 USPQ 136 (Fed. Cir. 1986).

**"New use" doctrine.** Discovery of a genuinely new use for an old structure, grounded in a property that was not previously known and is not simply an inherent, automatic byproduct of using the structure as before, may be patentable. *In re Hack*, 245 F.2d 246, 114 USPQ 161 (CCPA 1957). But where the claim is merely directed at achieving an inherent result/property that necessarily occurs whenever the old structure/process is used as before, that claim is anticipated. *In re May*, 574 F.2d 1082, 197 USPQ 601 (CCPA 1978). The line between these two turns on whether the "new" outcome is a necessary, inevitable consequence of the prior art's normal operation (inherent, hence anticipated) versus a genuinely separate, previously unknown mode of operation/use.

## Actionable matching criteria for a claim-matching / anticipation metric

1. **Two channels of disclosure**: express statement in the reference, or inherency — a limitation counts as "matched" under either.
2. **Inherency test = necessity, not recognition**: ask whether the limitation is a necessary feature/result of what the reference discloses, not whether a contemporary reader would have noticed it.
3. **Reasoned-basis requirement**: an inherency claim needs technical/factual grounding that the property *necessarily* flows from the disclosed structure/process — mere possibility, likelihood, or "could have" language is insufficient, especially in obviousness contexts (per *PAR Pharmaceutical*).
4. **Structural-identity shortcut**: if the reference's structure/composition/process is identical or substantially identical to the claimed one, inherent possession of claimed functional properties may be presumed without independent proof; burden shifts to the patentee/applicant to rebut with evidence the prior art lacks the property.
5. **Printed matter and process-of-use don't rescue an otherwise-identical apparatus/product claim** from anticipation — non-functional labeling or the "manner of intended use" is not itself a distinguishing element (see also § 2114/2115 on apparatus claims).
6. **Process claims**: normal/ordinary operation of an identical prior art device that necessarily performs the claimed steps anticipates the process claim, even without explicit description of that operation.
