# Peer Review: A Flawed Process at the Heart of Science and Journals (Richard Smith 2006)

## Background

Author: Richard Smith — editor of the BMJ and chief executive of the BMJ Publishing Group for 13 years.

Published: Journal of the Royal Society of Medicine, 99(4), 178-182.

## Core Argument

Smith argues that "peer review is a flawed process, full of easily identified defects with little evidence that it works."

He notes: "people have a great many fantasies about peer review, and one of the most powerful is that it is a highly objective, reliable, and consistent process."

## Identified Defects

Smith catalogs problems with peer review:
- **Slow and expensive** — manuscripts often take months for review
- **Inconsistent** — agreement between reviewers little better than chance
- **Bias** — gender, nationality, institutional affiliation, against minority/non-English speakers
- **Easily abused** — can be used to suppress competitors
- **Anti-innovation** — tends to favor incremental, expected results over surprising findings
- **Doesn't detect fraud** — undetected manipulated data routinely passed peer review
- **Doesn't detect errors** — major errors slip through
- **Reviewer effects** — interaction effects between reviewers and editors

## Famous Quote

Smith compares peer review to democracy: "a system full of problems but the least worst we have."

## Implications for Reviewers

The article suggests that reviewers should:
- Recognize the limitations of their own judgment
- Be cautious about claims of objectivity
- Look beyond expected results — be open to genuinely innovative work
- Take fraud and error detection seriously
- Be transparent about reasoning and biases

## Influence

This article is widely cited in discussions of peer review reform and motivated experiments like:
- Open peer review
- Post-publication peer review
- Pre-registration / registered reports
- Multiple-stage review processes
