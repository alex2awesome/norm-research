# An Introduction to Writing a Peer Review (Small Pond Science)

Terry McGlynn's how-to for ecology / biology early-career reviewers.

## Three-Section Structure

1. **Summary** (1–3 sentences): overview of the paper's main argument, quality assessment, and key shortcomings.
2. **Major issues**: substantial problems with design, interpretation, analysis, argumentation, writing — and praise where merited.
3. **Minor remarks**: typos, citation errors, non-fatal issues, often as bullets.

## Essential Checklist

- Read the journal's reviewer instructions first — different venues want different things.
- Find something genuinely praiseworthy, even in a bad paper.
- Contact the editor with questions; that's their job.
- Respond promptly to invitations — click "no" immediately if you can't commit.
- When declining, suggest alternative reviewers, especially doctoral candidates and postdocs.
- Address the editor, not the authors directly.
- Avoid prescriptive language — explain problems and *why* solutions work, don't issue orders.
- Be culturally sensitive about writing quality, particularly with non-native English speakers.

## Tone

- Polite language while staying honest about shortcomings.
- Phrase suggestions gently in main reviews; reserve unvarnished assessment for editor-only sections.
- Don't pile on about poor writing; brief, carefully chosen remarks are enough.
- Skip detailed reviews if comprehension is genuinely impossible; tell the editor.

## For Early-Career Reviewers

Constructive, specific feedback is community service — particularly important for authors with limited peer-review access before submission.
