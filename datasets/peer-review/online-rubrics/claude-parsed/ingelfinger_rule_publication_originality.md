# The Ingelfinger Rule — Reviewer Criteria for Manuscript Originality and Disclosure

Promulgated in 1969 by NEJM Editor-in-Chief Franz J. Ingelfinger and reaffirmed by Arnold S. Relman in 1981 ("Definition of 'Sole Contribution'"), the rule defines the originality criterion that medical reviewers and editors apply to submissions. It has been adopted by most major medical journals (JAMA, Lancet, Annals of Internal Medicine, BMJ in modified form).

## The Rule (verbatim)

"Articles are accepted for consideration with the understanding that they are contributed for publication solely in this journal."

The journal will not publish findings that have:
- already been published elsewhere in substantive form, OR
- been promised to another journal, OR
- been disseminated in popular media before peer-reviewed publication (with defined exceptions).

## Reviewer Operational Criteria Derived From Ingelfinger

Reviewers (and editors) at journals adopting the Ingelfinger rule must check:

1. **Substantive overlap** with the authors' own prior publications — does the manuscript present materially the same data or analysis already published?
2. **Salami slicing** — has the dataset been split into multiple thin publications?
3. **Duplicate publication** — has the manuscript been submitted simultaneously elsewhere?
4. **Pre-publication media disclosure** — have the findings been disclosed to lay press in a way that compromises peer-reviewed publication?
5. **Conference proceedings** — does prior conference dissemination cross the threshold of "substantive prior publication"? (Most journals now permit conference abstracts but not full papers.)
6. **Translation duplication** — is the manuscript a translation of a paper already published in another language?

## Recognised Exceptions (Modern Codification)

- **Public-health emergencies**: findings that should be communicated immediately for patient safety (e.g., emerging epidemic data).
- **Regulatory or court-required disclosure**.
- **Press releases by sponsors** preceding publication where authors had no control.
- **Preprints** (since ~2017, most journals including NEJM accept preprinted submissions; the rule has been narrowed to peer-reviewed publication only).

## Norms for Reviewers Confronting Ingelfinger Issues

- If you suspect duplicate or prior publication, **flag to the editor** with specific citation rather than proceeding to substantive review.
- Do not penalise the manuscript merely for prior **preprint posting** (if the journal accepts preprints).
- Treat **conference abstract** prior publication as expected, not as duplicate publication, unless the conference proceedings include a full paper.
- Where overlap exists with the authors' prior work, evaluate whether the **incremental contribution** is sufficient for a new publication.

## Significance for Rubric Design

The Ingelfinger rule is the historical anchor for the "originality" criterion that appears on virtually every medical-journal review form ("Is the work original? Has it been published or submitted elsewhere?"). Its modern codification — including preprint-friendly modifications — defines the operational scope of "originality" that reviewers must enforce.
