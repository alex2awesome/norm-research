# Patterns of Evaluation in Science: The Referee System (Zuckerman & Merton 1971)

A foundational sociology-of-science article that introduced the systematic study of peer review and articulated the institutional functions and evaluative criteria of refereeing.

## Functions of the Referee System

Zuckerman and Merton characterise refereeing as a mechanism of *institutionalised quality control* in science. They identify multiple, intertwined functions:

1. **Quality control / gatekeeping** — Filter what enters the archive of certified knowledge.
2. **Allocation of recognition and esteem** — Awards of "publication credit" calibrate symbolic rewards in science.
3. **Allocation of journal space** — A scarce resource that referees ration.
4. **Reduction of error** — Detection of mistakes in evidence, reasoning, and presentation before publication.
5. **Improvement of manuscripts** — Constructive criticism that raises the level of accepted work (the "developmental" function).
6. **Status-judgments on contributors** — Cumulative evaluations that shape careers.
7. **Reinforcement of cognitive and methodological norms** — Refereeing socialises authors into the disciplinary standards of evidence, exposition and citation.

## Evaluative Dimensions Zuckerman & Merton Document Referees Using

Drawing on samples of editorial correspondence and referee reports (notably from the Physical Review), they document referees attending to:

- **Originality / novelty of contribution** (priority claims and incremental advance).
- **Validity of method** (experimental design, statistical treatment, theoretical derivation).
- **Soundness of evidence** (adequacy of data to claims).
- **Logical coherence of argument**.
- **Significance / importance** to the discipline (whether the paper "advances the field").
- **Clarity and economy of exposition**.
- **Adequacy of citation to relevant literature** (situating the work in the cognitive map of the field).
- **Appropriateness for the specific journal** (audience and scope fit).
- **Priority and originality verification** (whether claims duplicate prior work).
- **Ethical conduct in attribution** of credit.

## Stratified Evaluation

A central empirical claim is that referees' judgments are stratified by:
- the **rank of the journal** (more prestigious journals demand stricter standards),
- the **rank of the contributor** (with the "Matthew effect" giving high-status authors cumulative advantage), and
- the **status of the referee** (eminent referees tend to be more critical and less variable).

## Sources of Disagreement Among Referees

They explicitly tabulate sources of inter-referee disagreement, anticipating later empirical bias literature:
- Differing cognitive standards across schools of thought.
- Substantive theoretical disagreement.
- Differential salience attached to method versus substance.
- Differences in tolerance of speculative versus consolidative work.
- Personal/social biases (institution, school, nationality).

## Normative Prescriptions for Referees

Zuckerman & Merton draw out the *normative* expectations the institution places on referees:
- **Disinterestedness** — judgments must not be coloured by personal interest.
- **Universalism** — claims judged by impersonal criteria, not who advances them.
- **Communality** — sharing of knowledge required by referee-author exchange.
- **Organised scepticism** — every claim subject to critical scrutiny.
- **Humility** — recognition that the referee may be wrong.
- **Confidentiality** of the manuscript and review process.
- **Timeliness** of response (recognised even in 1971 as a chronic failure).

## Significance for Rubric Design

This article is the seminal academic source articulating *why* referees evaluate, not merely *how*. Its enumeration of (i) referee functions, (ii) evaluative dimensions, and (iii) Mertonian normative obligations on referees forms the implicit backbone of nearly every modern reviewer-instruction document.
