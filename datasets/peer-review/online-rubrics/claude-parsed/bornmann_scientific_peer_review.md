# Scientific Peer Review (Bornmann 2011)

## Author Background

Lutz Bornmann is a researcher at ETH Zurich/Max Planck who has worked on peer review and quality assurance since the late 1990s.

## Major Empirical Findings

### Inter-Rater Reliability

In a meta-analysis of 48 studies, Bornmann et al. report 'a low level of inter-rater reliability'. However, Bornmann does not necessarily regard a low level of agreement as undesirable, since it may result from different peer reviewers bringing in different types of expertise and focusing on different aspects of a manuscript.

### Predictive Validity (Angewandte Chemie study)

Bornmann presented an approach for determining the usefulness of peer reviews for manuscript selection at Angewandte Chemie International Edition. Results show that peer review is useful: **78% of submissions accepted by AC-IE are correctly accepted for publication when the editor's decision is based on peer review.**

### Bias Findings

Bornmann's work has documented:
- Gender bias in some peer review processes
- Geographic / institutional bias
- Cronyism in panel review
- Selection bias against innovation

## Theoretical Framework

Bornmann analyzes peer review through the lens of sociology of science theories:
- Mertonian norms (CUDOS — Communalism, Universalism, Disinterestedness, Organized Skepticism)
- Bourdieu's concept of scientific field
- Latour and Woolgar's lab studies
- Kuhn's paradigms

## Quality Dimensions

Bornmann's research suggests that peer review quality should be evaluated on:
1. **Reliability** — agreement between reviewers
2. **Validity** — predictive accuracy of decisions
3. **Fairness** — absence of bias
4. **Efficiency** — speed and cost
5. **Predictive power** — correlation with later citations/impact

## Implications

Reviewers should:
- Recognize that disagreement may reflect legitimate diversity of expertise
- Provide explicit criteria for their judgments
- Be aware of potential biases
- Aim for both reliability AND validity
