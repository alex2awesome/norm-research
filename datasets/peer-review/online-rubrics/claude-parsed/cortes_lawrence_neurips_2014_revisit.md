# Inconsistency in Conference Peer Review: Revisiting the 2014 NeurIPS Experiment

## The 2014 NeurIPS Experiment

The experiment randomly selected 10% of submissions and had them reviewed by two independent committees. 170 papers were selected from the conference for dual reviewing. The goal was to test consistency in peer review by examining what would happen if submissions were reviewed independently.

## Key Original Finding

The experiment found that approximately **25% of decisions were inconsistent between the two committees** for borderline papers. (For papers that were clear accepts or clear rejects, agreement was higher.) For borderline papers, the disagreement rate was effectively a coin flip.

## 2021 Revisit (Cortes & Lawrence)

A comprehensive follow-up study with seven years of additional data determined that **50% of the variation in reviewer quality scores was subjective in origin.**

### Quality of Decisions

The reviewing process for the 2014 conference was:
- **Good for identifying poor papers**
- **Poor for identifying good papers**

For accepted papers: **no correlation** between quality scores and impact (citation count).
For rejected papers: **correlation** between quality scores and impact.

This suggests review committees can filter out clear weakness but cannot reliably distinguish among strong submissions.

## Implications for Peer Review

1. **Subjectivity is unavoidable** — reviewers should disclose their reasoning
2. **Borderline papers are essentially random** — area chairs should not over-trust narrow score margins
3. **Discussion matters more than scores** — qualitative reviewer comments contain more signal than ratings
4. **The system filters out poor work but cannot reliably select the best** — implies need for more inclusive acceptance models
5. **Community attention is the real signal** — citation counts reveal what reviews cannot predict

## Quality Dimensions for Better Reviews

Based on these findings, reviewers should:
- Justify scores explicitly with concrete criteria
- Distinguish "this is wrong" from "I don't find it interesting"
- Engage with author rebuttals to update on evidence
- Acknowledge their own areas of uncertainty
