# Hagstrom 1965 — The Referee Role and the Norms of Evaluation

Hagstrom's monograph (based on 1960–61 interviews with American physicists, molecular biologists, and theoretical physicists) is one of the foundational sociology-of-science treatments of the *normative obligations* of evaluators in science. The book predates Zuckerman & Merton 1971 and articulates a number of rubric-relevant principles.

## The Core Exchange Hagstrom Documents

For Hagstrom, science is a **gift exchange**: scientists "give" findings (via publications) in exchange for **recognition** awarded by the community. The referee is the institutional gatekeeper of that recognition. Refereeing therefore implicates several normative dimensions:

1. **Disinterestedness** — the referee gains no personal recognition, must not be motivated by competition with the author.
2. **Universalism** — judgements based on the merits of the contribution alone, not on the author's identity, school, or status.
3. **Communality** — knowledge is to be shared; refereeing must not be used to suppress findings the referee dislikes.
4. **Organised scepticism** — every claim subject to systematic critical scrutiny.
5. **Independence** — a "norm of independence" Hagstrom particularly emphasises: scientists must reach their own judgements free of authority pressure. This norm extends to refereeing, where reviewers must not defer to senior authors or to the journal editor's expected verdict.

## Referee Functions Hagstrom Identifies

- **Quality verification** — checking that empirical claims are supported by evidence.
- **Originality verification** — checking that priority claims are not duplicated.
- **Relevance assessment** — determining whether the work addresses a question the field considers important.
- **Audience screening** — fitting the manuscript to the scope of the journal.
- **Status calibration** — implicit ranking of the contribution's importance through differential journal placement.

## Tensions Hagstrom Identifies (now incorporated into modern reviewer guidelines)

- **Specialisation vs generalist judgement** — narrow specialists may be most competent but most biased; generalists are more disinterested but less competent. Both are needed.
- **Originality vs solidity** — referees must trade off the value of risky, novel claims against the importance of consolidative, careful work. Hagstrom argues that most disciplines err toward conservatism and reject too much truly original work.
- **Recognition allocation vs error correction** — these can pull in opposite directions; a referee who emphasises "did this advance the field?" may overlook errors, and vice versa.

## Operational Norms Drawn From Hagstrom

- The referee should evaluate the manuscript **as if the author were anonymous** to enforce universalism.
- The referee should recuse where personal stakes (priority competition, school disagreement, career rivalry) would compromise disinterestedness.
- The referee should explicitly weigh **whether the contribution merits the recognition** the journal would confer (a senior journal confers more recognition; a higher bar of significance applies).
- The referee should **report potential priority conflicts** (where the referee or others might have prior claim to the result).
- The referee should recognise that they are not deciding *truth* but *whether the community should grant recognition* — a more limited and humble judgement.

## Significance for Rubric Design

Hagstrom is rarely cited explicitly in modern reviewer-instruction documents but his framing has been absorbed: the Mertonian norms (disinterestedness, universalism, communality, organised scepticism) appear nearly verbatim in COPE's *Ethical Guidelines for Peer Reviewers* and in many publisher-level reviewer codes. The "gift exchange" framing — that the referee is a custodian of the community's recognition system — is the implicit moral premise of every modern call to "review for others' work as you would want them to review yours."
