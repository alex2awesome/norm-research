# Polanyi 1958 — Tacit Knowing and the Structure of Personal Judgement in Peer Review

Polanyi's *Personal Knowledge* and the later *Tacit Dimension* (1966) are foundational philosophical sources for understanding what reviewers actually do when they "evaluate." The work argues that scientific evaluation cannot be fully algorithmised; it inescapably involves personal judgement informed by tacit knowledge. This has rubric-relevant implications.

## Core Claims Bearing on Peer Review

### "We can know more than we can tell"
The judgement that a piece of work is good, important, valid, or original cannot be wholly reduced to explicit criteria. The expert reviewer brings tacit knowledge — pattern recognition, taste, sense of style — that informs the rating but resists full articulation.

### Personal commitment is logically necessary to evaluation
The reviewer is not (and cannot be) a dispassionate calculator applying a rule. Polanyi argues that personal commitment is constitutive of the judgement; the reviewer **stakes** their judgement on the evaluation.

### The republic of science is structured by mutual evaluation
Scientists evaluate each other's contributions through a network of overlapping competences — no single reviewer is competent across the whole map; reviewers form a "polycentric" evaluative community whose judgements aggregate.

## Polanyi's Implicit Criteria for Good Evaluation

Drawing from *Personal Knowledge* and *The Tacit Dimension*, Polanyi treats good evaluators as exhibiting:

1. **Connoisseurship** — the trained capacity to recognise quality in the discipline. Acquired through apprenticeship, not through rules. Cannot be replaced by checklists alone.
2. **Submission to the standards of the discipline** — acceptance of the field's existing standards as a precondition for criticism (not blind obedience but commitment to the framework).
3. **Recognition of valid problems** — the ability to distinguish a genuine scientific question from a pseudo-question.
4. **Intellectual responsibility** — willingness to take a definite position and defend it.
5. **Integrity** — honesty about the limits of one's own knowledge.
6. **Originality of reception** — the capacity to recognise novel contributions whose value is not yet articulated.

## Tensions Polanyi Identifies (taken up by modern reviewer training)

- **Rule-based vs tacit evaluation** — explicit rubrics are necessary but not sufficient; reviewers who apply only the explicit rubric miss the value of work that does not fit established categories. The Center for Open Science Registered Reports system is explicitly designed to *limit* tacit judgement to a few permitted moments because it can be abused; Polanyi's view is that some tacit judgement is irreducible.
- **Conservatism vs originality** — connoisseurship trained on the existing field tends to undervalue genuinely novel work. Reviewers must self-check for this conservatism.
- **Individual vs collective judgement** — each reviewer is a "polycentric" evaluator whose limitations require multiple reviewers and multiple modes of evaluation.

## Operational Norms for Reviewers Drawn From Polanyi

- A reviewer should explicitly **declare the tacit standards being invoked** — "in my field, work of this type is expected to do X" — even if these cannot be wholly justified by explicit rules.
- A reviewer should **acknowledge the limits of their own competence** rather than pretend universality.
- A reviewer should **not reject a manuscript merely because it does not fit existing categories** — connoisseurship trained on the past tends to misjudge the genuinely new.
- A reviewer should **stake their judgement** — provide a definite recommendation and a defence of it, rather than an evasive list of "concerns."
- The community should rely on **multiple polycentric reviewers** rather than treat any single reviewer's judgement as authoritative.

## Significance

Polanyi is the philosophical anchor of the (otherwise sceptical) recognition that some elements of peer review are irreducibly tacit and personal. His framing legitimates the reviewer's narrative summary as bearing real epistemic weight, even where it cannot be reduced to a numeric score, while also providing the diagnostic vocabulary (conservatism, connoisseurship-bound) for the failures of tacit judgement.
