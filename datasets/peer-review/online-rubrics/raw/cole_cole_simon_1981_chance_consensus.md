# Cole, Cole & Simon 1981: NSF Reviewer Evaluation Criteria

This study re-refereed 150 NSF proposals (Chemical Dynamics, Solid State Physics, Economics) with new reviewers and showed that "getting a research grant depends to a significant extent on chance." For our purposes, the key contribution is the explicit set of NSF evaluation criteria the authors operationalised and the implicit rubric that emerged from their inter-rater analyses.

## NSF "Mail Reviewer" Evaluation Form (the operational rubric)

Reviewers were asked to rate each proposal on a 5-point scale (Excellent / Very Good / Good / Fair / Poor) on the following dimensions, and to supply a written justification:

1. **Probable scientific significance / importance of the proposed research** — would the work, if successful, advance the field?
2. **Originality of the research** — does it open new ground rather than incremental refinement?
3. **Adequacy of the research design / methodology** — soundness, feasibility, technical specification.
4. **Scientific competence of the principal investigator** — track record and demonstrated ability.
5. **Adequacy of facilities and resources** — institutional capacity to execute.
6. **Effect of the grant on the scientific infrastructure** (training of students, instrumentation, etc.).
7. **Overall recommendation for funding** with one of: Must Fund / Should Fund / Could Fund / Should Not Fund.

## Inter-rater Reliability and Implicit Norms

Cole, Cole & Simon's results crystallised several normative claims that have been carried forward into modern reviewer training:

- The **modal Pearson correlation between independent reviewers' ratings was ~0.25** (around 0.25 across fields). Reviewers should therefore treat their own judgement as one noisy estimate, not a verdict.
- **Disagreement is endemic and largely substantive**, not random — it reflects genuine differences over what counts as good science. Reviewers should articulate the substantive grounds of their evaluation rather than offer summary judgements alone.
- **Halo effects from PI reputation** explain a large share of reviewer agreement; reviewers should explicitly try to evaluate the proposal independently of the investigator's prior eminence.
- **Field heterogeneity** matters: criteria such as "originality" carry different weights across disciplines; reviewers should make their weighting explicit.

## Their Recommendations to Funding Panels (operational rubric for panels)

- Use **multiple independent reviewers** (at least 4–6) and treat their average not as truth but as a reliability-bounded estimate.
- Require reviewers to **separate scores by dimension** so that disagreement can be diagnosed (e.g., disagreement on significance versus on methodology).
- **Discount the marginal predictive value of the PI's reputation** when the proposal itself is informative.
- Recognise an inevitable "**zone of indifference**" near the funding cut-off where lottery-like effects dominate; reviewers should not pretend false precision in that zone.

## Long-Run Significance for Reviewer Norms

The paper's finding — that approximately half of competitive NSF outcomes are attributable to which reviewers happened to be drawn — became the empirical anchor for later procedural reforms (multiple reviewers, calibration meetings, lottery-component proposals at the SNSF and NIH). It is therefore both a foundational empirical study and an implicit prescriptive document on how reviewers and panels should structure their judgements.
