Clarifying the questions regarding minor revisions

Megan Bryant
April 18, 2025
Read Time - 5 minutes

Revisions give you an opportunity to improve your paper in light of the comments you receive, so show the editor and reviewers plenty of evidence that you have done so.

Author support

What is expected in a minor revision? How should I respond to reviewer comments effectively? How can I ensure my revised manuscript is ready for final acceptance?

As a journal editor, these are among the many questions we often receive from authors. In this blog, I’ll guide you through some common queries that authors have when they receive a ‘minor revision’ notice. While specific policies may vary across journals at Sage, this general guide can help you to understand the key details to look out for when processing a minor revision.

Common reasons for a ‘minor revision’ decision

A reviewer may request ‘minor revisions’ when only a few changes are required prior to acceptance. This generally means that the manuscript needs some refinement, rather than a whole reconstruction.

Minor revisions typically address:

  • Ambiguities in the text, requiring re-writing to address specific areas where the manuscript requires clarity.
  • Adjusting citations that could be limited, excessive, out of date, or require reformatting in line with the journal’s reference style.
  • Correcting simple factual or numerical errors, which are easily resolved.
  • Formatting issues with tables and figures i.e., incorrect labelling, missing arrows, enhancing figure quality, etc.
  • Minor language edits such as repetitive statements, typos, subject-specific formatting conventions, spelling errors, etc.

Following resubmission, the manuscript may undergo another review to ascertain whether the recommended changes have been made. If the editor is satisfied, the manuscript is then accepted for publication.

5 tips for effectively addressing minor revisions: 

1. Craft a clear response document and address each comment systematically.

Why this matters: Your response document is almost like a conversation with the reviewer. You’re not just making changes, you’re explaining why you made them. A well-organised document shows careful consideration of reviewer feedback and also makes the editor’s job easier!

How to do it: Don’t just say “done”. Point to the exact location of your revision and explain how those address their suggestions. 

For example:

Reviewer comment: Please clarify the methodology in Section 2."

Author Response: "We’ve added a sentence in Section 2 to clarify the methodology, explaining the sampling process in more detail."

Correction: Page 3, lines 5-10.

2. If something is unclear, seek clarification from the journal editor

Why this matters: Don’t waste time guessing! If a reviewer comment is ambiguous, please reach out to the journal editor to avoid your manuscript having to go back for another round of revisions. 

How to do it: Be polite and specific.

For example:

Dear Editor,

Reviewer 2 asked for ‘more detail on the control group’. Please could you clarify if they are looking for information on the group’s demographics or the specific experimental procedures.

3. Check journal-specific guidelines

Why this matters: Each journal has specific formatting per article type and reference style. Not adhering to this is often a common reason for delays. Ensure the acknowledgements, declaration of conflicting interest, funding statement and ethical approval and informed consent are present (if applicable) and correct.

How to do it: The journal submission guidelines are usually present on the journal website. Navigate to the ‘preparing your manuscript for submission’ section. Tip: If you use EndNote to manage references, you can download the Sage EndNote output file to format your references in the correct style.

4. Aim for a timely resubmission (but communicate if you can’t)

Why this matters: Adhering to the journal’s deadlines ensures that the publication process keeps moving. Delays can sometimes lead to complications or even withdrawal from consideration.

How to do it: Set a deadline for yourself. If you are unable to submit your revision on or before the deadline, please get in touch with the journal editor.

5. Thoroughly proofread your manuscript

Why this matters: A polished manuscript enhances clarity.

How to do it: Conduct a final review for grammar, spelling, and formatting accuracy.

In summary

When you receive a revision ‘verdict’ of any kind, you are also given instructions for what/how to revise. Revisions give you an opportunity to improve your paper in light of the comments you receive, so show the editor and reviewers plenty of evidence that you have done so. As always, the journal editors are here to help, so please do get in touch if you have any questions at any point in the publication process.
