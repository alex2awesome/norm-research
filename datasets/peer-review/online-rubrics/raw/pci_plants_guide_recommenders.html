1. What you should know

PCI recommenders manage the evaluation of articles in the same way as associate editors do at journals, with a few differences (see below). PCI only accepts (“recommends”) preprints of high scientific quality that are methodologically and ethically sound and that adopt Open Science practices, such as open data and open code. PCI wishes to promote scientific reproducibility and reliability, to improve the overall robustness and integrity of the scientific conclusions drawn. 

You are responsible for editorial decisions. If you have co-recommenders, you share this responsibility with them. The Managing Board then validates your decisions or asks you to modify them before validation.

If no recommender decides to handle a submitted article within a certain amount of time (usually 20 days), the article is automatically desk-rejected.

If you start the preprint evaluation process, you will play a similar role to that of a journal editor: finding at least two reviewers, collecting reviews, and making editorial decisions based on reviews. Just like peer review at a journal, the evaluation process can require more than one review/decision/author response cycle. 

After each round of review, you can recommend the preprint, reject it, ask the authors for revisions, and begin another round of review. As a recommender, you will sign all decisions sent to the authors. Reviewers may choose to remain anonymous or to sign their reviews. Reviewers are typically expected to write their reviews within 3 weeks, and once the reviews are completed, you are expected to make your decision within 10 days. 

There is no required acceptance/rejection rate. All articles handled by a recommender that are of high scientific quality and methodologically and ethically sound should be accepted. Articles that do not achieve these standards should be rejected at any point in the evaluation procedure. The importance or impact of the results should not be a criterion for acceptance. 

If you reject the article, the reviews and decision will be sent to the authors, but will not be published or publicly released by PCI. They will be safely stored in our database, which is accessible only to the Managing Board.

If you decide to accept and recommend the article, PCI publishes the evaluation correspondence (peer reviews, editorial decisions, and authors' answers), with reviewers retaining the right to choose whether to sign their reviews or remain anonymous. 

When PCI publishes your recommendation, it signifies that PCI considers that the article is scientifically valuable. Recommendations and associated articles can be cited in the literature. The authors are free to publish recommended articles in traditional journals if they wish. 

Authors can notably choose to have PCI-recommended preprints published in open access in Peer Community Journal (PCJ) with no fees or further peer review. 

Several PCI-friendly journals use the PCI evaluation to replace, accelerate or complement their evaluation procedure. The list of journals that are relevant to this PCI can be found in this webpage and the complete list of PCI-friendly journals.

2. What you should verify/request from the authors when making your editorial decisions

You should verify that the article meets the following criteria when making you editorial decisions. If it does not, please ask the authors to revise their article accordingly. If the necessary revisions are not made, please reject the article:

  • The research question/hypothesis/prediction must be clearly presented.
  • A clear justification of sample sizes must be provided for empirical studies (see Lakens, 2022: https://doi.org/10.1525/collabra.33267), especially with respect to the following, as relevant: statistical power, Bayes factor thresholds, or estimate precision (quantitative studies) and information power or saturation thresholds (qualitative studies).
  • The results must adequately support the conclusions, and the analysis's interpretation must not be overstated.
  • Raw data must be available and reusable, except where there are ethical restrictions (e.g., when availability would pose a risk to humans, animals or the environment). Hence, the metadata and accompanying text must accurately describe the data. 
  • Details concerning the quantitative analyses (e.g., data treatment and statistical scripts in R, bioinformatic pipeline scripts, etc.) and simulations (scripts, codes), must be available to the reader in the text or through a correctly versioned deposit in an open repository, such as Zenodo, Dryad or an institutional repository (see Directory of Open Access Repositories) with a DOI or another permanent identifier (such as a SWHID of Software heritage). Note that Git URLs are not permanent. Information on how to assign a DOI for a GitHub repository is given at https://docs.github.com/en/repositories/archiving-a-github-repository/referencing-and-citing-content. 
  • Scripts or code must be carefully described so that another researcher can reuse them.
  • Details on experimental procedures must be given in the text.
  • In fields requiring approval from an ethics committee or institutional review board, the authors must ensure that the proposed research has received all necessary approvals before submission.

3. What you should do

3.1. When you are invited to act as a recommender

Reply rapidly to the invitation to handle an article on the website by accepting or declining. 

You must handle a manuscript evaluation only if (see also the Tips to help you decide to handle the evaluation of a preprint):

  • You are willing to do so; you should not feel obliged to take charge of an evaluation.  
  • You find the preprint interesting. 
  • You have enough relevant expertise to evaluate the preprint.
  • You have time to handle the preprint throughout the whole process, from submission to recommendation.
  • You have no actual, potential or perceived conflict of interest as specified in the PCI Code of conduct​.

Major flaws within a preprint may only become clear after a deeper examination, after you have decided to handle the article, but before sending invitations to review it. If, after careful consideration, you conclude that the preprint is so flawed as not to be worth sending out for full peer review, it may be “desk-rejected” at this point. The assigned recommender (you) will write a detailed decision letter explaining the reasoning behind the decision and send it to the Managing Board. Note, however, that you are expected to have read the article carefully before deciding to handle it. Desk rejection after handling should therefore remain extremely rare. Most desk rejections should be due to no recommenders choosing to handle the submitted preprints.

3.2. Once you agree to handle a preprint

You commit to ensuring that the evaluation process is conducted in a timely manner. If necessary, remind reviewers to meet their deadlines and ensure you also meet yours.

Start inviting reviewers (without conflict of interest with the authors or with the content of the article) as soon as you agree to handle the manuscript or as soon as you receive the revised version of an article that you intend to evaluate again. Send invitations to multiple potential reviewers within 48 hours. Since not all reviewers will accept, you may need to send as many as 5-10 initially before sending new invitations until you find at least two reviewers willing to review the preprint. This process of finding reviewers should, ideally, take no more than one week.

When inviting reviewers to evaluate a preprint, consider a broad mix of scientists with different scientific perspectives, institutional affiliations, career stages, and personal characteristics (e.g., geographic origin, ethnicity, gender, etc.).

Try personalising your invitation letters as much as possible to maximise response rates.

Search for additional reviewers if the reviews received are too light and insufficient to enable you to reach an editorial decision.

3.3. When you need to make an editorial decision

Be kind, respectful, and constructive in your decisions. You must adhere to the PCI Code of conduct, avoiding abusive or discriminatory language in your comments. Editorial decisions that violate the code of conduct may be returned to you for editing or discarded.

Be open to new approaches.

Try to keep to the deadlines at each step of the process, as it shows respect towards the authors. Inform the Managing Board if there is likely to be any delay or if you are unexpectedly unable to carry out your recommender duties. 

Make a decision based on at least two good-quality reviews. Making editorial decisions is your responsibility, not the reviewers'.​

Cite all the information and comments you have used to reach your decision, in addition to the peer reviews you have invited.

Do not use negative results as grounds for rejecting an article.

If you require a revised version, pay attention to any kind of error in the text of the article (typography, grammar, etc.).. If the language is poor, mention it to the authors and suggest they check for typographical errors and/or have the text reviewed by a language professional.

You do not need to check the format of the references (but you can if you want).

Post your editorial decision or write your recommendation text within 10 days of receiving the reviews or the revised version of the preprint.

You should not copy and paste the reviewers' comments in your decision (as the reviews are automatically attached). Feel free to reject an article at any step of the evaluation process (even after several rounds of review) if scientifically sound reasons justify this rejection.

Your editorial decision should summarise the most relevant comments of the reviews and your own points of criticism. 

State whether you disagree with some of the points raised by the reviewers.

Inform the Managing Board if you suspect scientific misconduct.

You must absolutely not use AI to generate your decisions. If you do so, you risk having your text rejected by the Managing Board and being banned from PCI. You can, however, use AI to translate, correct and improve your texts.

3.4. If you decide to recommend the preprint

If you decide to recommend the preprint, you must write a recommendation text. This text is a short article, similar to a News & Views piece. It has its own title, contains 300-1500 words, describes the context, and explains why you find it interesting and why you decided to recommend it. This text also contains references (at a minimum, the reference to the recommended article) (see Tips to help you write your recommendation text). Do not hesitate to read some past recommendations in your relevant PCI.

You must absolutely not use AI to generate your recommendations. If you do so, you risk having your text rejected by the Managing Board and being banned from PCI. You can, however, use AI to translate, correct and improve your texts.

4. Tips to help you decide whether to handle the evaluation of a preprint

Here are questions to help you determine if you should handle the evaluation of a preprint

1- Do you have a specific interest in the scientific question asked by / the object studied in the article?

2- Do you have a specific interest in the methodology, the robustness, the results or the discussion of the article?

3- Besides the above points, are there any other reasons that could make you accept the recommender role for this article? 

4- As a recommender, are there any points that you particularly appreciated in the work?

5- Do you know colleagues who have relevant expertise that could act as reviewers? Or do you know where to find experts?

6- Do you have the time to handle this preprint?

If you answered YES to most or all of these questions, then you should agree to handle the preprint. But if your answer to all questions is NO, you should decline.

As a reminder, the goal of PCI is not to evaluate and recommend all submitted preprints. Articles that do not meet high scientific standards or are not methodologically or ethically sound should be rejected at any stage of the evaluation process.

5. Tips to help you write your recommendation text

Here are questions to help you write your recommendation text:

1- What is the general context of the study in the field?

[The general context of the study is ...]

2- What is your specific interest in the scientific question asked by / the object studied in the article?

[I'm particularly interested in ...]

3- What is your specific interest in the methodology, the robustness, the results or the discussion of the article?

[I evaluated this article because the methodology/robustness/results/discussion are ...]

4- Besides the above points, is there any other reason you decided to accept the role of recommender for this article? (You should make it clear it was due to one or both of the above points if not).

[I decided to accept the role of recommender for this article because ...]

5- Is there a particular way the authors decided to tackle these questions? What was notable?
[The authors used a new experimental design; new software; …]

6- Very concisely, what are the main results of the study? (Note that this is not meant to reiterate the abstract -- a succinct high-level summary is generally best)

[The authors used X and Y to show that Z is consistent over…]

7- Were any notable study aspects improved due to feedback from the reviewers/recommender? Especially in scientific content, such as new analyses that resolved ambiguity.

[One reviewer noted an issue with the author’s original method, which was improved in a subsequent version by…]

8- What were the points that the reviewers positively evaluated in their review?

[During the peer-review process, the reviewers particularly appreciated ...]

9- What were the points that you, as a recommender, particularly appreciated in the work?

[I found tool Y/finding X particularly interesting, because … ].
