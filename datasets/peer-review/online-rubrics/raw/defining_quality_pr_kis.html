Abstract

This study examines the challenge of defining quality in peer-review reports, a crucial yet underexplored aspect of academic publishing. Reviewers are vital gatekeepers of scientific knowledge, but unclear skills and a lack of standardized guidelines have led to inconsistent and subjective practices, weakening the overall efficacy of the peer-review process. To address this issue, the primary objective of this paper is to answer the research question: How has literature addressed guidance for producing quality peer-review reports? A scoping review was conducted, utilizing Scopus, Web of Science, SpringerLink, ScienceDirect, PubMed, and SAGE databases to search for records using keywords related to guidelines for scientific peer reviewing. The review identified 111 primary studies offering recommendations on how to review scientific articles. Extracted data were analysed thematically, focusing on approaches to reviewing articles, manuscript evaluation criteria, and report-writing guidelines. The findings revealed six key categories of review criteria for evaluating scientific manuscripts: structural components, research approach, style, ethical conduct, scientific value, and overall suitability. Additionally, the review provides 70 actionable recommendations for writing peer-review reports and highlights eight essential quality features expected in review texts: constructive, specific, fair, thorough, courteous, consistent, objective, and readable feedback. This study contributes to developing a standardized guide for scientific reviewing, with a particular emphasis on supporting early-career reviewers. The findings encourage academic publishers, journal editors, and professional organizations to adopt the proposed guidelines to enhance consistency, reduce bias, and improve the peer-review process. They also provide a foundation for developing new tools to support the reviewing.

1 Introduction

The current publication system depends on reviewers to carry out the peer-review process, where they assess the research and assist editors in making publication decisions. However, despite their important role as gatekeepers of scientific knowledge [1], the skills required for effective review are not well defined or formally established [2]. In other words, there are no standardized rules for writing review reports. This lack of standardization exists for several reasons: reviewing is generally voluntary work [1], review reports are not published or cited [3], and there is a common belief that reviewers should have the freedom to conduct their evaluations as they see fit [4].

Differences in the style and quality of peer reviews reveal uncertainties within the review process and indicate that reviewers would benefit from more clearly defined guidelines [5]. While the existing literature offers numerous publications on how to review manuscripts grounded in extensive theory and guidelines, some studies argue that these guidelines are often too generic and lack specificity [6]. Alternatively, some studies suggest that these guidelines are overly tailored to specific research fields [7]. In summary, the current guidelines for scientific reviews do not address some of the essential criteria required to ensure high-quality evaluations [8]. Consequently, reviewers tend to establish their own criteria for evaluation, shaped by their personal experiences and backgrounds [9]. This approach results in an uneven review pattern, with some reviewers submitting detailed analyses and criticism pages and others submitting only a few superficial sentences [3].

Reviewers' personal traits are also a factor because they vary greatly in their attitudes, practices, and viewpoints [10]. Some reviewers are more lenient, whereas others are harsher [11]. As an information product resulting from a process, a review report lacks standards, references, and requirements that define its quality. It is irrefutable that peer reviewer feedback is at the core of peer-reviewed journals [12]. The quality of the review matters significantly to the authors and editors for three reasons. First, peer review serves as the primary quality control mechanism for validating research and disseminating its findings through journals and conferences. Second, it enhances the quality of the manuscripts by identifying areas for improvement. Finally, it provides essential input to guide the editor's decision on whether a manuscript is suitable for publication. Therefore, when a review report is vague, superficial, or biased, it helps neither the authors nor the editor [13].

Research on peer review is not particularly well developed [14], especially with regard to the analysis of peer review text. Since 1999, when Van Rooyen introduced the Review Quality Instrument [15], there has been a growing interest in analyzing the quality of peer review reports. With advances in Natural Language Processing (NLP) that have proven to be a powerful tool for assessing text quality [16] and the availability of public datasets of peer review reports, such as the PeerRead dataset [17], recent studies on scientific peer review have focused on linguistic analysis. However, the lack of a standardized definition of general quality in the literature has led researchers to create study-specific definitions, raising concerns about the rigor of their findings. Studies that present guidelines for reviewers can reduce the bias inherent in the heuristic peer review process. Therefore, it is pertinent to analyze studies that propose peer-review guidelines and assess how reviewers can effectively apply them to enhance the consistency and quality of their evaluations.

Therefore, this study employs a scoping review approach, without restrictions on time or discipline, to explore the approaches used for reviewing scientific articles, manuscript evaluation criteria, and report-writing guidelines discussed in the literature. The primary objective is to answer the research question: How has literature addressed guidance for producing quality peer-review reports? Answering this question provides insights into the quality features a peer-review report should include and how these can be achieved. As well as offer a conceptual framework for defining the quality features of peer-review reports, which can serve as a basis for automated analyses of review quality.

This article is structured as follows: Sect. 2 provides the background and context for review reporting. Section 3 outlines the methodologies adopted in this study. Section 4 presents key findings and analysis. Section 5 discusses limitations and proposes future research directions.

2 Quality of peer review reports

Scientific publishing relies on peer review, where journal editors use reviewer reports to assess a manuscript's suitability for publication. However, inconsistencies among reviewers lead to variable review quality [18]. Some provide unprofessional, overly harsh [19], or unconstructive feedback, negatively affecting authors and research quality [20].

Editors use various mechanisms to monitor peer-review quality, with review assessment playing a key role in evaluating submitted reviews. These evaluations help determine whether a reviewer remains in the pool for future assignments. Peer-review quality has been assessed through diverse metrics and methodologies. Van Rooyen et al. (1999) introduced a foundational tool based on eight criteria: research question importance, originality, methodology, presentation, constructive feedback, substantiation, and result interpretation [15]. Since then, studies have identified 24 tools and 23 scales using between 1 and 26 criteria to assess review quality. Notably, four of ten tools rely on a single-item measure asking editors to rate a review's "overall quality," yet none provide a comprehensive definition of peer-review report quality. However, these methods are prone to editorial bias and often lack a solid theoretical foundation, raising concerns about validity and reliability [21].

Assessing peer reviewer comments is inherently subjective [22], with quality perceptions varying by stakeholder. Authors evaluate reviews based on their usefulness in improving manuscripts but may be biased by how well the feedback aligns with their interests [23]. Editors, on the other hand, prioritize how effectively reviews support their decisions [24]. Understanding the nuances of peer review texts and reviewer intent is crucial for quality assessment [25]. With no universally accepted definition of peer-review report quality [14, 21, 26], it is essential to examine how guidelines shape review reports and define expected quality, aiming to establish a broadly accepted standard.

Interest in analyzing peer-review texts is growing, with NLP and Machine Learning (ML) techniques increasingly used for automation, primarily through text classification [27, 28]. Review quality has been assessed by identifying key features such as criticism, recommendations, and question-framed requests [29] as well as praise and criticism [30]. Other studies have examined aspects like analytical tone, authenticity, clout, sentiment, and morality in review comments [31]. Ghosal (2022) proposed five NLP tasks as indicators of review thoroughness, aiding automated quality assessment: aligning comments with paper sections, detecting paper aspect categories, predicting review statement roles, assessing statement significance, and generating meta-reviews [25]. Researchers have also developed methods to measure review informativeness, scoring section relevance, aspect coverage, and reviewer-centric uncertainty in feedback [32].

Recent studies have explored generative AI, particularly OpenAI's GPT models, to assess peer review helpfulness by generating text from input prompts [33]. Tools like ChatGPT also support writing, as seen in ReviewWriter, which aids in drafting clearer, higher-quality reviews [34]. The use of large language models (LLMs) in peer review is increasingly encouraged, with international committees advocating for ethical, transparent implementation and context-specific policies [35]. The Guidelines for Ethical Use and Acknowledgment of Large Language Models in Academic Writing propose a framework for responsible LLM use in research [36]. Leading journals now support their ethical application, though LLMs remain limited, often producing inconsistent or inaccurate results [37]. Classifying review quality accurately can be time-consuming and inefficient without a clear standard for high-quality reviews.

This study examines the criteria and requirements for writing high-quality peer reviews. To achieve this, we analyze the scientific literature on the peer-review process and summarize key findings to answer: How has the literature addressed guidance for producing quality peer-review reports? The insights gained aim to inform future research, including the development of automated tools leveraging NLP and generative AI. The findings aim to inform future research, including developing automated tools utilizing NLP and generative AI.

3 Methods

This scoping review followed the framework and principles established by Arksey and O’Malley (2015) and was conducted in five key stages: identifying the research question, identifying relevant studies, study selection, charting the data, and collating, summarizing, and reporting the results [38]. To enhance the quality and transparency of reporting, this review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses for Scoping Reviews (PRISMA-ScR) guidelines (Online Resource 1) [39].

3.1 Data sources and search strategy

In the identification phase, relevant articles were retrieved through searches conducted in the Scopus, ScienceDirect, Web of Science Core Collection, PubMed, SAGE, and SpringerLink databases. These databases were chosen for their extensive topic coverage and advanced filtering capabilities. Grimaldo et al. (2018) highlighted that 49.84% of publications on peer-review topics are found in medical journals [40]. Consequently, PubMed was included as it is widely used for biomedical and life sciences research and is freely and openly accessible.

Our search was performed for all available database records from its inception to May 27, 2024. The search query used was: ("scientific" OR "academic" OR "scholarly") AND "peer review") AND ("reviewer" OR "referee") AND ("guideline" OR "guidance" OR "how to" OR "guide" OR "recommendation" OR "tip" OR "advice" OR "orientation" OR "suggestion"). For ScienceDirect, the query used was: ("scientific" OR "academic" OR "scholarly") AND "peer review" AND ("reviewer" OR "referee") AND ("guideline" OR "guidance" OR "how to"). This adjustment was necessary because ScienceDirect limits the number of Boolean connectors to a maximum of eight per field. No filters were applied to ensure the review was as comprehensive as possible. Details of the search strategies for each database are presented in Online Resource 2.

3.2 Process selection

Inclusion criteria covered all types of journal publications—such as editorials, primary studies, and commentaries—from any scientific field, as long as they were written in English and aimed to provide guidance on peer review. In contrast, secondary research and articles addressing other topics related to peer review (e.g., peer review models, research grant evaluations, or educational contexts) were excluded. Publications offering guidelines for systematic literature reviews, clinical trials, or other study designs not classified as original research articles, and those focused on statistical guidance for reviewers were also excluded.

Publications were selected during the screening phase based on predefined criteria. Two reviewers (A.S. and A.L.) independently assessed all titles and abstracts to determine their alignment with the research objectives. Articles approved during screening underwent full-text evaluation, and those meeting the eligibility criteria were included in the review. Full-text copies of potentially eligible publications were obtained for further evaluation. These full texts were independently assessed, and any disagreements were resolved through discussion or, if necessary, by involving additional reviewers (A.R. and L.P.). The finalized set of selected studies was added to Mendeley,Footnote 1 a bibliography management tool used to organize search results from each database and remove duplicates. The reviewer (A.S.) manually screened the titles and abstracts of all references from the selected studies, applying exclusion criteria to refine the selection. Finally, the studies were imported into NVivo 12Footnote 2 for quantitative and qualitative analysis. The results of this process were documented using a flowchart.

3.3 Methodological quality assessment

Two independent reviewers (A.S., A.L.) assessed the methodological quality of eligible papers using the JBI’s Narrative, Opinion, and Text Assessment and Review Instrument (NOTARI) to evaluate the quality of discussion and opinion papers [41]. This tool comprises seven questions evaluating the source, expertise in the field, references to existing literature, and alignment with prior research. Editorials that did not explicitly state undergoing peer review before publication were marked as "not applicable," as most such publications are not peer-reviewed. For the remaining six questions, each was scored as follows: Yes = 2, No = 0, Unclear = 1, with a maximum possible score of 12, later converted to a percentage. Scores of 0–50% indicated low quality, 50–70% medium quality, and above 70% high quality. Regardless of quality, all papers were included in the review. Disagreements on inclusion were resolved by a third reviewer (A.R or L.P.).

3.4 Data extraction and coding

During the data extraction process, a cyclical coding approach was implemented, following Saldana’s guidelines [42]. In the first coding cycle, the publication characteristics were identified, including publication type (e.g., original research, editorial, perspective, commentary), year of publication, journal name, journal field, and the journal's quartile ranking according to SCImago.Footnote 3 Additional codes captured aspects related how guidelines were presented, such as the format of the content (text, checklist, bullet list, or questions), target audience (general or specific), review criteria (e.g., introduction, methodology, results), and whether specific elements were addressed, including approaches to reading the manuscript, writing the review report, providing review examples, differentiating comments for editors versus authors, and defining the concept of review quality. The second coding cycle focused on extracting key concepts from the studies. In the third cycle, these concepts were grouped into broader themes, such as criteria for scientific review, approaches to reading the manuscript, writing the review report, and defining review quality. Themes were refined by merging or excluding similar codes until code saturation was achieved. Patterns were then categorized, and preliminary conclusions were drawn based on the content. To ensure credibility, all themes underwent cross-validation by the three researchers, allowing for thorough comparison and analysis. The full record of the extracted data can be found in Online Resource 2.

To analyze the data from studies included in this review, methods for synthesizing qualitative evidence were applied. Two primary techniques were used: content analysis and narrative summary. Content analysis categorizes data and identifies the frequency of different categories, facilitating systematic organization and quantitative assessment of themes [43]. In contrast, narrative summary synthesizes content by structuring it into a coherent narrative. This process involves ordering, chronicling, and selecting key elements to create a comprehensive summary of the evidence [43].

Each category was carefully analyzed to address the research question, including its citations and detailed descriptions. The content was then structured to form a cohesive theoretical framework. Connections between criteria were identified, particularly when concepts shared similar contextual definitions, such as "originality" and "novelty." Categories were developed by grouping criteria with common characteristics, creating an organized and practical framework for interpreting the findings. The results are presented according to the approach to reading the manuscript, reviewing the manuscript—criteria for peer review, and the approach to writing the review.

4.3 Synthesis of results

An analysis of the available literature revealed that preparing a high-quality peer review report involves four key aspects: (1) the approach to reading the manuscript, (2) reviewing the manuscript, (3) writing the report, and (4) applying quality criteria.

4.3.1 Approach to reading the manuscript

Approaches to reading scientific manuscripts were explored in 52 studies, each of which offered key recommendations. The first and most fundamental step in reviewing a manuscript is its reading. While this may seem straightforward, various methods exist for the initial reading, and no single method is universally the best. Reviewers are encouraged to develop their own style that suits them [44].

Two notable approaches are commonly discussed: reading the manuscript three times [5, 45,46,47,48,49,50,51,52], and reading it twice [53,54,55,56,57,58,59]. These recommendations were analyzed and categorized into distinct phases, with specific tasks assigned to each phase. Data synthesis presents a basic approach, detailing a set of tasks (TO-DOs) to be carried out at different stages of the article review process, as illustrated in Fig. 3. This approach assumes that reviewers have already agreed to evaluate the manuscript considering their availability, relevant expertise, and potential conflicts of interest.

4.3.1.1 Pre-reading preparation

Before reading the manuscript, the reviewer should aim to understand the scope, audience, and specific requirements of the journal. This can be achieved by reviewing the “Information for Authors’ pages or relevant editorials [46]. It is also crucial to familiarize oneself with the journal’s expectations for the review, so reviewers are advised to spend a few minutes learning about the journal’s policies and preferred review format [60].

4.3.1.2 Initial quick reading

The first reading, often referred to as a "quick read," is intended to provide a general understanding of the manuscript and assess whether it fits within the journal’s scope and contributes meaningfully to the field. During this phase, the reviewer should identify the type of manuscript (e.g., review article, technical note, original research) and study design (e.g., prospective cross-sectional study, retrospective case study) [44, 61]. The reviewer should also determine the main objective of the study and ensure that the research methods are appropriate. Some guiding questions for this phase include: "What are the objectives or purpose of the manuscript? Is this purpose consistent throughout?" [62], "Is this topic timely and relevant for the journal's audience? Has the research question been addressed before, and if so, does this manuscript offer new insights?" [63]. During this first reading, reviewers can also identify any major flaws or reasons for summary rejection. If the manuscript contains numerous writing errors that hinder understanding, the reviewer may suggest that it undergo copyediting by a proficient English editor before a detailed evaluation of its scientific merits [47].

4.3.1.3 Critical reading

Second, a more thorough reading focuses on evaluating the research in detail. Reviewers should consider two key questions: How relevant or important is this paper, and could it be improved? The primary goal of this detailed reading was to assess the scientific merit and relevance of the manuscript. In addition, the reviewer should suggest possible improvements to enhance the clarity of the presentation [63]. At this stage, using a checklist for each section of the manuscript can be helpful in ensuring that all necessary information is included [46].

4.3.1.4 Final review

The final reading was intended to capture any remaining issues that may have been missed in previous readings and to verify that the abstract and title accurately reflect the content of the study [64, 65]. Before submitting their comments, reviewers should ensure that their feedback aligns with their overall recommendations for acceptance, revision, or rejection [66]. Finally, reviewers might consider the following questions to assess the quality of their review: "How would I feel if I received this review on a Monday morning?" and "Are the suggestions constructive and the recommendations clear?" [3]. These recommendations outline the key steps involved in reading an article, with the goal of preparing a review report. During the reading process, reviewers were expected to assess various aspects of the article and take note of their evaluations and suggestions.

4.3.2 Reviewing the manuscript—criteria for peer-review

The review identified 52 criteria used by reviewers to evaluate manuscripts, based on an analysis of 105 studies. These criteria were organized into six categories according to their purpose (see Table 2). A detailed catalog with descriptions and examples of all 52 criteria can be found in Appendix 1. The categories were grouped as follows:

Structural components: This category encompasses criteria pertaining to different sections and components of scientific writing. Scientific manuscripts typically follow the IMRaD structure (Introduction, Methods, Results, and Discussion), as described by Sollaci and Pereira (2004) [67]. Original research articles followed this standardized format, with each section serving a specific function in conveying the research findings. A practical strategy for offering targeted feedback is to assess the manuscript section by section, as has been suggested by various authors [63, 68, 69]. Reviewers are encouraged to perform a comprehensive evaluation of the manuscript, from the title to the references [70], and it is further recommended that reviews be organized by the manuscript’s sections [71].

Style: Criteria related to the writing and presentation of a manuscript are crucial for ensuring that readers can understand the content clearly and efficiently. Clear writing prevents ambiguities and misunderstandings, while a well-structured presentation makes it easier for readers to navigate the text and grasp information [54]. Additionally, coherent writing and organized presentation enhance the credibility of the work, allowing readers to focus on the substance of the research without being distracted by grammatical errors or formatting issues [54]. Writing style improves reading experience and contributes to the effective communication of scientific knowledge. Although grammar issues are not usually the primary focus of criticism, they should be noted if they hinder the logical flow of the manuscript. In cases where grammatical or typographical errors are frequent, it is appropriate to highlight this as a general area that requires improvement [65]. Journals often instruct peer reviewers not to focus on spelling or grammar [63, 72, 73]. However, if a manuscript is so poorly written that it becomes unreadable, reviewers should not feel obligated to decipher the authors' intended meaning [60].

Research approach: This category groups suggestions related to verifying the appropriateness of the approach for the purpose of the study. Scientific research has been conducted for various purposes. Depending on the study, the aim may be to achieve goals, solve problems, answer questions, or test the hypotheses. However, all research has a purpose and approach congruent with this purpose. A thorough manuscript review should focus on evaluating how well the study contributes to the scientific process, beginning with a clear rationale for the study's objectives and the significance of the hypothesis being tested [74]. The manuscript's purpose or aim should be clearly articulated early in the text, with the problem and key issues clearly stated [72]. The research hypothesis must align with the journal's mission and guide the methodology used [64]. The Methods section must provide enough detail for replication, covering the study design, sampling, data collection, and statistical analyses. Reviewers should ensure that these methods are adequately described and suitable for addressing the goals of the study [75]. Reviewers should assess whether the experimental design is rigorous and appropriate for testing the hypothesis, considering whether better approaches can be employed [76].

Scientific value: This category groups the criteria that determine the scientific value of the study and how it advances the field. Scientific value can be measured by the significance of the contribution, importance of the results, and ability to impact practice, among other aspects that determine how interesting the study is. When reviewing a manuscript, the primary focus should be to assess the potential of the study to make a significant contribution to the field [77]. This includes evaluating its contribution (new insights or innovations), significance (potential impact), and novelty (How new is the information?) [73]. Reviewers should consider whether the work is original, offer a new perspective, and align the mission and scope of the journal [78]. Additionally, assessing the relevance of the study is crucial; the most impactful articles are those that have the potential to change practice patterns and open new areas of research [79].

Ethical conduct: When reviewing manuscripts, reviewers should be vigilant in detecting potential misconduct, such as data falsification, fabrication, or plagiarism, and report any suspicions immediately to the editor to prevent compromised literature quality [80]. Ethical scrutiny is particularly important in studies involving humans and animals. Reviewers must ensure that these studies adhere to a robust ethical framework with proper approval and compliance with ethical and legal standards [81]. For animal studies, proper care and use must be verified, and for human studies, it is essential to confirm that the research was conducted with appropriate institutional and subject approval. Human protocols should conform to all the prevailing ethical and legal standards [82]. Reviewers should be mindful of potential conflicts of interest, especially financial or scientific influences that may affect the study's presentation or interpretation. If a study is funded by an industry or organization, reviewers must assess whether this support has biased the results or discussion [76]. Editors are tasked with ensuring that the authors adhere to ethical standards. The Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE) offers guidelines for addressing research and publication misconduct such as plagiarism and fraud. While the editorial board primarily detects plagiarism, reviewers play a key role in identifying and reporting ethical breaches [79]. Reviewers should also monitor for research bias, evaluating whether bias in the data or analysis skews the results. If present, reviewers should assess how this affects the conclusions and suggest ways to mitigate it [58]. In summary, ethical considerations in manuscript reviews are vital for preserving the integrity of scientific research. Reviewers must be diligent in identifying potential misconduct, ensuring that ethical standards are met, and reporting any concerns to the appropriate parties.

Overall criteria: Global considerations included criteria related to suitability for publication. When reviewing a manuscript, it is crucial to assess whether it aligns with the journal's mission, engages its readership, and adheres to the journal's formatting guidelines [58]. It is also important to evaluate whether a manuscript's content, theme, or topic is relevant and interesting to the journal's audience [62]. Additionally, checking how thoroughly the specific topic has been explored in recent scientific literature and whether the manuscript's argument fits within the journal's scope are recommended practices to ensure the suitability of the manuscript for publication [53].

4.3.3 Approach to writing the review

The review identified 70 recommendations for writing review reports, derived from an analysis of 84 studies. These recommendations were grouped into four categories based on their purpose. The guidelines offer practical advice on drafting comments for authors and editors, structuring the review, and maintaining a suitable tone throughout the report.

4.3.3.1 Comments to author

In the peer review process, the feedback provided to authors is of utmost importance [83]. These comments form the core of a review, delivering essential critique to justify rejection or enhance the manuscript for publication [69]. The primary objective of author feedback is to offer recommendations for manuscript improvement. An effective review should emphasize the paper's strengths, particularly its readiness for publication, while also pointing out any shortcomings that can be addressed during revision [64]. Regardless of whether the recommendation is to revise or reject, the review should be structured to clarify the reviewer's decision to the authors. Even when suggesting rejection, reviewers should strive to be supportive of the authors [56]. Nevertheless, the comments directed to the authors should align with those given to the editor and should not include the publication recommendation [84] (see Table 3).

4.3.3.2 Comments to the editor

Comments to the editor are placed in a separate section from those intended for the author, as the former are strictly confidential. Comments to the editor are useful when the reviewer provides a summary assessment of the manuscript, with justifications for the recommendation of acceptance, rejection, or revision. These comments may be brief but should not simply repeat what was conveyed to the authors; rather, they should offer an objective assessment of the manuscript, supported by clear reasoning [69]. Additionally, they should include extra information to help the editor make a final decision on the manuscript [9]. Typically, comments to the editor present a concise rationale for the recommendation, based on the quality and relevance of the research [64]. In summary, since editors carefully read the comments intended for the authors, there is no need to reiterate them here. This section allows the reviewer to offer further insights that can assist the editor in deciding whether to accept or reject the manuscript [85]. 4.3.3.3 Tone of review

Reviewers can choose the tone of their language and the way they convey their ideas, so it's critical to show authors consideration [109]. It always aims to be fair, balanced, polite, and civil. Even if it is necessary to provide strong criticism, it is recommended to use professional language that is non-accusatory and does not belittle or insult authors [88]. The goal should be to write reviews in a collegial and constructive manner [68]. Reviewers should always strive to be courteous and respectful and remember that constructive criticism can be given kindly. There is always a way to communicate that a study may not be suitable or needs significant improvement without being harsh or offensive [91]. Being tough but nice helps foster a positive and productive review process. It is interesting to note that while most studies recommend adopting a positive tone, they also recommend downplay praise. Hoppin (2002) noted that if the article is accepted, the authors will be sufficiently pleased, regardless of any praise; however, praising an article that is ultimately rejected may create discomfort between the author and editor. Additionally, Scrimgeour and Pruss (2016) emphasized that maintaining a positive and constructive tone should not compromise a referee's ability to identify and address significant flaws in a manuscript (see Table 5).

4.3.3.4 Structure and organization

Some reviews consist of a rushed list of problems presented in no particular order, making difficult for both editors and authors to interpret and follow [85]. To improve clarity and structure, we encourage our reviewers to present their comments in a clear, concise, and organized manner [70]. Although review formats vary by journal, certain common practices have proven effective [104]. To help reviewers enhance the organization of their reviews, here are several suggestions: First, to orient the author and managing editor to the key points of the critique, it is useful to provide a brief overview of the manuscript's main strengths and weaknesses. Second, we recommend that reviewers structure their critique according to the sections of the manuscript (e.g., abstract, introduction, methods, results, discussion) [71].

Prioritizing and organizing concerns is essential: doing so helps the author and editor understand which major issues need to be addressed and which minor ones are less critical for the paper’s publication or revision [121]. A well-organized review will clarify for authors the specific areas that require attention and indicate whether any deficiencies could prevent publication [91].

Additionally, organizing the review in a logical order benefits both the authors and editors, especially when points are numbered, as this allows for a clearer response and rebuttal process. Adding specific, numbered comments—divided into major and minor criticisms, if appropriate—facilitates the editor’s evaluation of the manuscript and aids the author in responding systematically to each point raised [77, 92]. 4.3.4 What constitutes a quality review?

A total of 41 studies (37%) presented the concept of a quality review. The analysis considered all related concepts, including synonyms and alternative terms used to describe a quality review, such as "effective," "great," "excellent," "good," and "optimal," among others. Each concept was examined to identify key terms that represent quality features. The method employed a multi-step systematic approach by Gentles (2016), which uses a constant comparative analysis method derived from the Grounded Theory framework [145]. This method has been used as a strategy for defining concepts, accounting for inconsistent terminology, and generating credible and verifiable analytical interpretations [21]. At the beginning, one researcher (AS) compiled all the quality characteristics of the quality concepts found in the literature review, which represent a quality characteristic of the peer review reports. Subsequently, another researcher (AL) organized the essential characteristics into a domain-specific matrix. The quality domains of the peer review reports composed the matrix, which was then followed by typical characteristics of each domain and citations of the literature sources from which the items were taken. While some concepts were broad and did not clearly identify specific characteristics, others explicitly included identifiable features that were collected and included in this result. 5 Conclusions and discussion

This article examines how to write a high-quality peer-review report by analyzing 111 primary studies on the topic, identified through a scoping review with no restrictions on time or discipline. This study sought to identify key approaches to writing a quality review report, driven by the need to establish common patterns including review criteria and writing guidelines for comments to authors and editors. This need arises due to the lack of a clear definition of “quality” in peer-review reports [21, 147]. In the absence of a unified standard, studies on quality have had to create and apply their own definitions, leading to limited findings often confined to specific research contexts [25, 148]. The review findings indicate that writing a high-quality review report involves more than a thorough reading of the manuscript and adherence to standard review criteria (e.g., structural components, research approach, style, ethical conduct, scientific value). Reviewers must also meet specific requirements when writing comments for both authors and editors, presenting corrections and suggestions that encompass eight key quality features: constructive, specific, fair, thorough, readable, professional tone, objective, and consistent. Table 8 summarizes the findings of this literature review, listing actions that can assist reviewers, authors, and editors in preparing reviews.

In a previous scoping review focused on biomedical context, the review guidance criteria with the two highest frequencies were found to be the contents criterion and the structure criterion. The authors deemed these findings important as they reflect peer review quality both internally (content assessment) and externally (report structure). This focus supports the main functions of peer review: evaluating content and clearly communicating results to editors and authors [147]. However, they found 17 review criteria only and didn’t extract the recommendations for writing the review report. Our results identified and categorized 52 review criteria into six distinct categories. This categorization projects the totality of nuances necessary to review the manuscript in its entirety and provides knowledge to better define future solutions for peer review. Not only restricted to solutions that evaluate sections (introduction, methods, results) but also broader aspects such as scientific value, ethical standards, quality of language and relevance to the journal's readers, among others.

Our study identified 70 specific requirements for writing a review report, including 34 for comments directed at the author, 19 for comments to the editor, 14 focused on the organization of the report, and 5 on tone. In a previous study, Yuan et al. (2022) highlighted five of the most frequently mentioned desiderata for review evaluation—determinacy, comprehensiveness, justification, accuracy, and kindness—as initial criteria for evaluating scientific article reviews [149]. Based on our findings, we offer a path toward more robust and objective solutions. The detailed list of requirements we present also avoids one of the biggest criticisms of review reports, their subjectivity. Subjectivity often stems from reviewers’ personal preferences, leading to potential biases and unfairness in peer review [150]. By adhering to these objective requirements, reviewers can maintain a clear focus on the purpose of the review, reduce personal opinions or preferences, and promote a more balanced and fair review process.

Another key point of this research is the identification of eight quality characteristics for review texts: constructive/helpful, specific, fair, thorough/comprehensive, readability/organization/clarity, tone, objectivity, and consistency. Chong’s (2024) systematic review identified four essential traits of quality feedback in peer reviews: constructiveness, tone, specificity, and structure. The study also highlighted the trends in feedback quality, such as reviewers’ "hijacking" and an author’s work, by suggesting irrelevant references they authored or pushed for unnecessary rewrites. Another issue is overly brief feedback, which offers general comments without actionable suggestions for improvement. Furthermore, feedback quality suffers when it includes demeaning or negative language, which hinders constructive dialogue [12].

A review of these multiple studies reveals inconsistencies in peer review guidelines across some themes: a) Recommendations in comments to authors: most studies advise reviewers to keep their publication recommendations confidential, sharing them only with the editor since final decisions are based on multiple reviews [62, 122]. However, other sources argue that reviewers should communicate their recommendations to both the editor and the author, emphasizing the importance of transparency and providing clear justifications for their stance [94, 133]. b) Grammar and language review: while many studies suggest that reviewers should address grammar, style, spelling, and punctuation issues [61, 75, 87] others argue that language corrections are the responsibility of copy editors, urging reviewers to focus on content and scientific merit instead [72, 92, 101, 142]. c) Reasons for rejection: some studies recommend rejecting manuscripts that are poorly written to the extent that a meaningful review cannot be conducted [60]. Conversely, other studies assert that rejection should be based solely on poor scientific quality, encouraging reviewers to evaluate the research's scientific rigor regardless of the manuscript’s language [45]. d) Feedback in rejections: when manuscripts are rejected, reviewers are encouraged to provide constructive feedback, guiding authors to improve their work for submission elsewhere or for future studies [51]. In cases where rejection is due to fundamental research design flaws, reviewers should prioritize significant issues over minor details like grammar or formatting [56]. Unless context-specific (e.g., for a particular journal), such inconsistencies can confuse novice reviewers who rely on literature for guidance. This aligns with findings from Seeber (2020), who noted substantial variation in the guidance provided across 46 journal reviewer guidelines, potentially contributing to low inter-reviewer agreement due to differing criteria and approaches [4].

The scientific review report is the main evidence that the manuscript has undergone an expert review process. Given its importance in influencing not only the editorial decisions on the submitted manuscript but also the future of the authors, the journal and the progress of research in the field as a whole [1], it is surprising to find that so little research has addressed its development or defined the expected quality. Ensuring quality peer review is a significant challenge, particularly because reviewers are not financially compensated for their work [151]. Unlike other professional evaluations that require specialized expertise and are typically rewarded, peer review relies primarily on scientists’ intrinsic motivation and their sense of duty to uphold the integrity of the scholarly publishing system [152]. The lack of financial incentives can lead to inconsistencies in review quality and increase the burden on reviewers. Establishing standardized review guidelines could help mitigate this issue by providing clearer expectations, reducing cognitive effort, and streamlining the review process to make it more structured and efficient.

In this way, the main contribution of this work lies in demonstrating the approaches and breadth of the review task, which involves various review criteria and multiple report-writing requirements. While respecting the specificities of each journal or field of study, all findings appear applicable to most research journals. Standardizing these patterns would aid in quality analysis research. Additionally, the definition of quality criteria shows the academic community that there is a concern to offer a good experience when preparing the review report.

5.1 Limitation of study

A limitation of this review was the predominant inclusion of publications on 'how to review,' which were primarily editorial materials, such as editorials, tutorials, and special commentaries. Additionally, although the number of eligible records identified was substantial, nearly half were editorials for specific journals. This may have introduced some level of bias, as editorial materials reflect only the perspective of their authors, raising concerns about the suggested guidelines. However, these limitations do not compromise the results because the goal was to compile all research on the topic available to the scientific community. Other limitations were bias related to search strategies, which did not incorporate structured searching techniques, such as the use of Subject Headings (e.g., MeSH or Emtree). This approach may have led to the exclusion of some relevant studies. This decision was influenced by a deliberate focus on addressing the specific research question. We applied broad search terms across multiple databases to mitigate this limitation and performed manual reference list screening to identify additional studies. Finally, for this review, we included only peer-reviewed publications and editorials to ensure credibility and acceptance within the academic community. However, excluding non-journal sources, such as publisher guidelines or institutional policies, may limit the scope of our findings. A previous scoping review analyzed 18 review guidelines from major publishers, including Elsevier, Springer, Nature, Wiley, SAGE, PLOS, and Taylor and Francis [147]. We ensured that all relevant criteria for evaluating manuscript content and providing guidance on structuring reviewer comments from that review were covered in our analysis.

6 Research agenda proposition

The progress in AI technology marks a substantial step forward, poised to usher in advanced capabilities and complexities. AI tools optimize quality control and peer review processes, particularly for routine tasks requiring less intellectual input, such as format checks, readability assessments, reviewer matching, and review report improvements [153]. However, it remains unclear how close we are to developing tools replicating the full peer-review process, which involves intricate intellectual contributions [154]. Furthermore, some scholars argue that overreliance on Generative AI could diminish or weaken the human aspect of peer review, decreasing subtle comprehension and critical analysis [155]. After analyzing AI-assisted peer review automation and this literature review, we grouped the identified gaps to propose directions for future research directions in three categories:


Reviewing papers: ML and NLP techniques have been applied to detect plagiarism and bias, as well as to generate statistical and methodological reviews of scientific manuscripts [156]. Research on article review automation primarily focuses on summarization. ASReview, for instance, has proven effective in screening titles and abstracts for systematic literature reviews, significantly reducing review time [157, 158]. Additionally, models like GPT-4 can identify relevant references on specific topics but are susceptible to hallucinations and generating false information [159]. This review identified 76 criteria for evaluating scientific manuscripts. It is necessary to explore the NLP techniques that are suited for each. For example, sentiment analysis aids in assessing tone [30], while transformer models help classify bias [160]. A challenge is the lack of comprehensive, annotated datasets. Existing datasets are limited to computer science and general manuscript features, restricting cross-disciplinary research. Models trained on single-domain data also struggle with broader applications [161]. Future research should focus on developing an extensive, annotated dataset that encompasses all review criteria. Additionally, studies should examine the impact of AI tools on interactions between authors, editors, and reviewers, particularly their effects on review quality, processing time, and decision-making.

Generation of review text: AI is rapidly transforming writing assistance, driving research into its use for peer-review writing. For example, Reviewrite provides AI-generated guidelines for peer reviews in German and led researchers to develop seven design principles for AI-generated peer-review instructions [34]. Hamta suggests textual inputs to reviewers, incorporating user requirements gathered through interviews [28]. Guo (2024) analyzed how peer feedback affects EFLstudent’ review quality based on five key features from literature [162]. However, these tools are limited to specific user groups lacking generalizability. Future research should develop a rule-based model aligned with author and editor feedback requirements. Alternatively generative AI solution could automate review report drafting, with editorial evaluation guiding model improvement. Additionally, empirical studies should assess authors, editors, and reviewers perceive AI-generated reviews comparing different strategies.

Analysis of peer review report quality: Existing studies define review quality inconsistently, making generalization difficult. For instance, Ghosal et al. (2022) defined a good peer review as one that "comments on important sections, addresses critical aspects of the paper, performs definitive functions, and clearly conveys the reviewer’s stance on the work" [25]. However, this definition omits factors like impartiality, readability, and consistency. Generative AI, tools such as ChatGPT can evaluate reviews if properly guided. A study found that over half of users rated GPT-4’s feedback as useful, with 82.4% preferring it over some human reviewers [163]. Yet, experimental tests show that while LLMs provide over 60% accuracy, fully correct responses are rare [37]. Prompt engineering Prompt engineering enhances outputs by providing attribute descriptions and example reviews. Human oversight, such as editorial feedback on AI-generated assessments, could further refine models. LLMs not only improve peer-review quality but also introduce innovative solutions to persistent issues [164].
