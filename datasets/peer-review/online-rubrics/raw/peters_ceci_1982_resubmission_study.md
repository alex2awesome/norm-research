# Peters & Ceci 1982: Reviewer Grounds for Rejection (the Coded Rubric)

Peters and Ceci resubmitted 12 already-published articles to the same prestigious psychology journals (with author names and affiliations swapped to fictitious low-prestige institutions). Of the 9 not detected as resubmissions, 8 were rejected. They coded the *reasons* reviewers gave for rejection — that coding constitutes a de-facto inventory of evaluation criteria psychology reviewers actually deploy.

## Coded Reviewer Grounds for Rejection (the operational rubric)

Peters and Ceci classified the substantive criticisms reviewers raised. The dominant categories were:

1. **Serious methodological flaws** — design, sampling, controls inadequate to draw the reported conclusions.
2. **Statistical problems** — wrong tests, inadequate power, multiple comparisons, ignored assumptions.
3. **Inadequate / inappropriate sample** — composition or size insufficient.
4. **Failure to consider alternative explanations** — confounds, third variables, plausible rivals not addressed.
5. **Weak or implausible theoretical rationale** — the question is uninteresting or the framing is muddled.
6. **Insufficient practical or theoretical contribution** — even if competent, the manuscript does not advance the field enough.
7. **Failure to control for relevant variables**.
8. **Inadequate literature review** — critical prior work overlooked or misrepresented.
9. **Poor quality of writing / presentation** — organisation, clarity, exposition.
10. **Inferences not warranted by the data**.

## Reviewer-Quality Norms Inferred from the Study

Peters and Ceci (and the dozens of commentaries that followed in BBS) made these prescriptive points that have been absorbed into modern guidelines:

- Reviewers should make explicit **what evidence would change their evaluation**; otherwise their judgements are unfalsifiable.
- Reviewers should **distinguish judgements about importance/significance from judgements about methodological soundness** — the two grounds for rejection were repeatedly conflated.
- Reviewers should be alert to **status / institutional bias** — the same paper rated favourably from a Harvard author is criticised when ascribed to a fictitious low-prestige institution.
- Editors should consider **double-blind review** to reduce institutional halo.
- Reviewers should **calibrate against the journal's published acceptance criteria** (the same article met them at first submission).
- Reviewers should explicitly state **disciplinary norms being invoked** rather than appealing to vague "standards."

## Significance for Rubric Design

The Peters & Ceci coding scheme is one of the earliest empirical inventories of "what reviewers actually attack." It is widely cited as the reason later journals introduced structured review forms, double-blind review, and editor training to separate substance from prestige cues.
