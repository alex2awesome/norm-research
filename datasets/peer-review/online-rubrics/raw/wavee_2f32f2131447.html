Abstract

In the last 20 years, there has been a sharp increase in the incidence of retractions of articles published in scientific journals, the majority of which are due to research misconduct. In some cases, researchers have revised and republished articles that were retracted due to misconduct, which raises some novel questions concerning authorship. Suppose that an article is retracted because one of the authors fabricated or falsified some data, but the researchers decide to salvage the useable data, make appropriate revisions, and resubmit the article for publication. If the person who committed misconduct has made a significant contribution to the research reported in the revised paper, should they be named as an author to recognize this contribution or should they be denied authorship because they committed misconduct? This is a challenging issue because it involves the confluence of two research ethics domains that are usually dealt with separately, i.e., resolution of authorship disputes and adjudication of misconduct findings, as well as potential conflicts among norms that underlie authorship practices and misconduct adjudication. In this paper, we (1) describe some actual cases involving articles that were retracted for misconduct and republished; (2) review policies from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, Committee on Publication Ethics, and top fifteen biomedical journals to determine whether they provide adequate guidance for cases like these; and (3) analyze the ethical and policy issues that may arise in these situations.

Keywords: Retraction, Misconduct, Replacement, Republication, Authorship, Adjudication, Research integrity, Ethical norms

Introduction

In the last 20 years, there has been a sharp increase in the incidence of retracted articles published in scientific journals that has outpaced the steady rise in the total number of publications (Casedevall & Fang, Elango, 2021; Fang et al., 2012; Lievore et al., 2021; Mansourzadeh et al., 2021; Nogueira et al., 2017; Wager & Williams, 2011; Wang et al., 2017). From 2000 to 2004, there were 315 retractions per year, on average, and from 2016 to 2020 that number had risen to 1833, or more than a 500% increase (Retraction Watch, 2021a). Most retractions are due to some form of misconduct, including data fabrication or falsification or plagiarism, duplicate publication, fraudulent peer review, or egregious violations of human research regulations (Casedevall & Fang, 2013; Fang et al., 2012; Moylan & Kowalczuk, 2016; Nair et al., 2020; Wang et al., 2019; Stavale et al., 2019).

Many people who are aware of this trend are concerned that it reflects a decline in the quality and integrity of scientific research (Zimmer, 2012). In 2010, Adam Marcus and Ivan Oransky launched the Retraction Watch (2021b) website to provide scientists and the public with a retraction database and a forum for discussing research integrity issues. Others hypothesize that the increase in retractions is not necessarily an indicator of waning research quality and integrity and is mostly due to enhanced vigilance by journals, institutions, funding agencies, and scientists to enforce ethics rules and correct the research record (Fanelli, 2013; Marcus & Oransky, 2014). For example, many journals now use software to screen manuscripts for potential plagiarism and require authors to submit original digital images so that editors and reviewers can determine whether images have been manipulated inappropriately (Butler, 2010; Cromey, 2010; Marcus & Oransky, 2014). Journals have also developed or revised policies concerning research misconduct and retractions (Resnik et al., 2010, 2015b).

The increase in retractions has forced journals to confront questions concerning resubmission and republication of retracted articles. If a retracted article still contains reliable data that has value to science or society, the researchers may decide to revise and resubmit it with a link to the original retracted article, which will remain in the research record.1 This option, known as “retract and replace” or “retract and republish” is being increasingly used by journals to handle articles that are retracted due to an honest error or even research misconduct (Bauchner & Golub, 2019; Cagney et al., 2016; Fontanarosa & DeAngelis, 2005; Heckers et al., 2015; Enserink, 2017; Marasović et al., 2018; Oransky, 2020). The Retraction Watch (2021c) database had 808 entries for “retract & replace” as of December 15, 2021.

Revising and resubmitting articles that were retracted due to research misconduct raises some novel questions concerning authorship.2 Suppose that an article is retracted because one of the authors fabricated or falsified some data, but the researchers decide to salvage the useable data, make appropriate revisions, and resubmit the article. How should authorship issues be addressed in this situation? There is little dispute that a person who has fabricated or falsified data should not be named as an author on the revised paper if that person has not made a significant contribution to it because, for example, the data they collected are no longer being used. Credit should be given only where it is due. But suppose that the researcher has made a significant contribution to the research reported in the revised paper because, for example, they collected or analyzed data or developed methods or reagents. Should they be named as an author to recognize these contributions or denied authorship because they have committed misconduct?

This is a thorny issue because it involves the convergence of two research ethics domains that are normally handled separately, i.e. resolution of authorship disputes and adjudication of research misconduct findings, and because it can give rise to conflicts among norms that underlie authorship practices and misconduct adjudication. While the responsible conduct of research (RCR) literature includes numerous publications on authorship disputes (e.g.,Ni et al., 2021; Smith & Master, 2017; Smith & Williams-Jones, 2012; Smith et al. 2020a; 2020b) and adjudication of misconduct findings (e.g., Abdi et al., 2021; Bülow & Helgesson, 2019; Dal-Ré et al., 2020; Keränen, 2006; Mello & Brennan, 2003; Redman, 2013; Redman & Merz, 2008), it does not include any substantive discussions, to our knowledge, of the issues examined herein. In this paper, we will (1) describe some actual cases involving articles that have been retracted for misconduct and republished; (2) review policies from the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors (ICMJE), Committee on Publication Ethics (COPE), and top fifteen biomedical journals to determine whether they provide adequate guidance for cases like these; and (3) analyze the ethical and policy issues that can arise in these situations.

Defining Research Misconduct

At the outset of this paper, it is important to state the definition of research misconduct used in identifying cases, describing policies, and analyzing issues, since there are various definitions of this term (O’Grady, 2021; Resnik et al., 2015a). The definition used in this paper is the one stated in the US federal policy, which has been adopted by US federal agencies that conduct, support or regulate research:

Research misconduct means fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism in proposing, performing, or reviewing research, or in reporting research results. Research misconduct does not include honest error or differences of opinion (Public Health Service, 2005, at: 45 CFR 93.103; see also Office of Science & Technology Policy, 2000)

To prove research misconduct under this policy, a research institution or funding agency must find that there was a significant departure from the accepted practices of the relevant research community and that the misconduct was committed intentionally, knowingly or recklessly. The standard of proof is preponderance of evidence, i.e. the evidence shows that it is more likely than not that the allegation is true (Public Health Service, 2005, at: 45 CFR 93.104).

The definition of research misconduct used by federal agencies is quite narrow, because it includes only three misbehaviors: fabrication, falsification, or plagiarism (FFP). Definitions of research misconduct (sometimes referred to as scientific misconduct) adopted by other countries often go beyond FFP and may include additional ethical transgressions, such as improper authorship, failure to disclose a significant conflict of interest, violating the confidentiality of peer review, and interfering with a research misconduct investigation (All European Academies, 2017; O’Grady, 2021; Resnik et al., 2015b). Definitions adopted by US universities also often go beyond FFP and may include serious deviations from widely accepted practices and serious violations of research regulations (Resnik et al., 2015a).

To help clarify these definitional issues, we find it useful to distinguish between research misconduct, defined here as FFP, and scientific misconduct, which includes research misconduct as well as other types of misconduct in science. Most studies of misconduct related to retractions fail to observe this distinction (see, for example, Fang et al., 2012), but we think it is important to keep in mind, because there are ethical and epistemological differences among these different types of misbehaviors (National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine 2017).

The federal definition of research misconduct does not define three terms—intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly—that describe the mental state of the person accused of misconduct (also known as the respondent). These terms are important to define because they have implications for the culpability of the respondent and are often disputed in misconduct cases (Harvard Magazine, 2012; Keränen, 2006; Redman, 2013; Resnik et al., 2017b). In this paper, we will follow Black’s Law Dictionary (2022) and define intentionally as doing something “with meaning or purpose,” knowingly as doing something with “full awareness,” and recklessly as doing something in a way that is “carless or indifferent to the welfare of other people.” Recklessness is more than mere carelessness or negligence. In a research misconduct case, recklessness involves careless disregard for the truth (Resnik et al., 2017b). As we shall show later, the accused person’s mental state is an important consideration in adjudicating misconduct, since misconduct that is committed intentionally or knowingly can be viewed as more egregious than misconduct that is committed only recklessly (Keränen, 2006; Abdie et al., 2021).

A case met our criteria if: (1) the article was retracted; (2) the stated reason for the retraction was research misconduct; and (3) the article was republished.

Our initial list identified ten cases, three of which were eliminated because the stated reason for retraction was mistake or error and did not mention research misconduct, and two of which were eliminated because a correction was published and the original article was not retracted.

The first case involved misconduct committed by Emily Horvath, who was a graduate student in Jeffrey Elmendorf’s endocrinology laboratory at Indiana University School of Medicine. In April 2010, Indiana University and the U.S. Public Health Service (PHS)’s Office of Research Integrity found that Horvath committed misconduct in research on insulin resistance supported by two National Institutes of Health (NIH) grants and an NIH predoctoral fellowship award. During the investigation, Horvath admitted to falsifying data for numerous figures used in two grant applications, two papers, and her PhD thesis. The falsifications were performed to increase the statistical significance of the data. As part of her settlement agreement with PHS, Horvath agreed to write to the editors of the journals of the published papers asking for a retraction or correction and explaining the reason for the retraction or correction (Department of Health & Human Services, 2010). One of these papers (Horvath et al., 2008) was retracted but not republished. The other paper (Bhonagiri et al., 2009), which was published in Endocrinology, was retracted, corrected, and republished (Bhonagiri et al. 2011) in the same journal. In this paper, Horvath manipulated data to enhance statistical power, specifically in Fig. 2c. This data manipulation did not change the overall conclusion of the paper and a revised version of the manipulated data was published as Fig. 2B in the republished article. Horvath was not listed as an author on the republished paper and was not even mentioned in the acknowledgements. A new author, Lixuan Tackett, a technician in Elmendorf’s lab, was added to the republished paper, presumably to help generate data for the revised paper. The republished paper included a note that referred to the retracted paper and stated that invalid data had been corrected (Bhonagiri et al. 2011).

In the second case, Martina Caduco and nine other researchers in the Department of Molecular Medicine at the University of Padova published an article on proteins involved in herpes simplex virus-1 (HSV-1) budding and envelopment (Caduco et al., 2012). The research was supported by grants from the University of Padova. The authors retracted the article the following year after discovering erroneous and manipulated images pertaining to six of the figures in the paper (Caduco et al., 2013). The retraction notice stated the editor-in-chief of the journal determined, after reviewing the raw data, that the data manipulation did not affect the overall conclusion of the paper, which still stands. All the authors took responsibility for these mistakes and misbehaviors and did not identify a person (or persons) to hold responsible. However, when the research was corrected and republished in 2013, the first author, Martina Caduco, had been removed and another author, Elisa Franchin, had been added (Calistri et al., 2015). The corrected article did not acknowledge Caduco or discuss data integrity problems related to the retracted article (Calistri et al., 2015). We were not able to find additional, publicly available details concerning the misconduct investigation related to this case from the university, funding organizations, journal, or news media.

In the third case, Ching-Wei Chen, a student in Laurent Zimmerli’s laboratory at the Department of Life Science and Institute of Plant Biology at National Taiwan University, and ten coauthors published a paper on plant immunity in Plant Cell in 2014 (Chen et al., 2014; Retraction Watch, 2016). The research was supported by National Science Council of Taiwan, National Taiwan University, The European Research Council, and The Gatsby Charitable Foundation. The authors later retracted the paper after discovering data manipulation in figures produced by Chen, the first author on the paper. The same image blot appeared in two differently labelled figures. The researchers tried, unsuccessfully, to replicate the experimental work for these figures (No authors listed, 2016). However, the retraction notice states that the authors believe the major conclusion of the article was not affected by the manipulations. Later, the researchers re-executed all of Chen’s experimental work and published a corrected version of the paper in the Plant Cell in 2016. The republished paper did not include Chen on the authorship list, nor did it mention Chen in the acknowledgments (Yeh et al., 2016). It did, however, discuss problems with the retracted paper and changes that were made. We were not able to find additional, publicly available details concerning the misconduct investigation related to this case from the university, funding organizations, journal, or news media.

The fourth case involved misconduct that impacted numerous papers. In March 2018, an investigation committee at the University of Liverpool concluded that liver cancer researcher Daniel Antoine had falsified data related to mass-spectrometry, Western blots, and magnetic resonance spectroscopy throughout his scientific career. The committee identified 30 published papers in which the integrity or reliability of the data was questionable (University of Liverpool, 2020), which led to 13 retractions and 6 corrections. One of the retracted papers was retracted and replaced (Retraction Watch, 2022). The retracted paper was a study of the effects of oxidative stress on acquired epilepsy in rat models published in Brain in 2017 (Pauletti et al., 2017). The paper had 19 authors from eight institutions and multiple funders, including NIH, the European Union’s Seventh Framework Programme, and Citizen United for Research in Epilepsy. Antoine was in the 16th position on the authorship list. In 2019, the researchers published a retraction notice in which they said that mass spectroscopy data that supported several figures could not be traced back to the original experiments (No authors listed, 2019). The republished paper did not list Antoine as an author, nor did it mention him in the acknowledgments. It also did not discuss the retracted paper or changes that were made to it (Pauletti et al., 2019).

We were unable to find any cases where the authors were the same in the retracted and republished paper. We did, however, find one case in which misconduct occurred and the authors were the same when the paper was corrected but not retracted. This case also involved Antoine. Antoine was listed as the fourth author on a paper on the molecular mechanisms that link liver injury, cell death, and liver cancer in the Journal of Clinical Investigation (Hernandez et al., 2018). The authors then published an expression of concern after they learned about the results of the University of Liverpool’s investigation of Antoine. In the expression of concern, they said that Antoine had fabricated mass spectroscopy data used in two figures in the paper but that the main findings of the paper were not affected (Hernandez et al., 2019a). Instead of retracting the paper, they published an erratum (Hernandez et al., 2019b). The erratum removed the questionable mass spectrometry data (Fig. 5H and 6D) and all conclusions inferred from the data, but did not remove Antoine as an author. It is worth noting, however, that Antoine was removed from a different paper when it was corrected and republished with the mass spectrometry data contributed by Antoine removed (see Huebener et al., 2019). Notably, in this republished version an additional author, Rosalind Jenkins was removed because of lack of a significant contribution to the revised manuscript.

There are probably many cases of retracted and republished papers involving misconduct that we were unable to find because journals often do not clearly indicate that a paper is being retracted for misconduct. Very often, the retraction notice for a paper impacted by misconduct may describe the reason as something like “data unreliable,” without any further explanation, if any reason is given at all (Resnik & Dinse, 2013). Also, even journals that indicate that misconduct was the reason for the retraction may not name a responsible party. The main reason why journals often print cryptic retraction notices when misconduct occurs may be to avoid being sued for libel.3 While this is an important ethical/legal issue that impacts the integrity of the research record, there is insufficient space to discuss it in-depth here (see Bilbrey et al., 2014; Casedevall & Fang, 2013; Fang et al., 2012; Resnik & Dinse, 2013; Wager & Williams, 2011;). Since more than half of all papers are retracted due to misconduct, it is likely that a large percentage of the 808 retracted/republished papers in the Retraction Watch database were retracted due to misconduct.

Existing Policies and Guidelines

Since cases do exist in which papers are retracted due to misconduct and then republished, a natural question to ask is whether journal policies address the authorship issues in these situations. To answer this question, two of us, TB and DBR, independently reviewed authorship, retraction, and research misconduct policies from ICMJE, COPE, and fifteen top biomedical journals.

Over 6000 journals have stated that they follow ICMJE guidelines (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, 2022). ICMJE has policies on authorship, retractions, corrections, and research misconduct (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, 2021a, 2021b, 2021c). None of these policies specifically address the authorship issues we are concerned with in our paper but the authorship guidelines provide general guidance:

The ICMJE recommends that authorship be based on the following four criteria:

  • Substantial contributions to the conception or design of the work; or the acquisition, analysis, or interpretation of data for the work; AND

  • Drafting the work or revising it critically for important intellectual content; AND

  • Final approval of the version to be published; AND

  • Agreement to be accountable for all aspects of the work in ensuring that questions related to the accuracy or integrity of any part of the work are appropriately investigated and resolved.

In addition to being accountable for the parts of the work he or she has done, an author should be able to identify which co-authors are responsible for specific other parts of the work. In addition, authors should have confidence in the integrity of the contributions of their co-authors.

All those designated as authors should meet all four criteria for authorship, and all who meet the four criteria should be identified as authors. Those who do not meet all four criteria should be acknowledged (International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, 2021a).

While these guidelines provide some important factors to consider when assigning authorship, they do not settle the question of whether a researcher who has committed misconduct should be named as an author on a resubmitted paper because they do not specify what it means for a contribution to be “substantial” or for someone to be “accountable.” Accountability is an important issue when misconduct occurs, because coauthors may no longer trust the guilty party to be accountable. Also, researchers who do not want to include someone as an author on a paper could refuse to allow her/her/them to read and approve the final version, which would disqualify that person from authorship. Although ICMJE guidelines prohibit this practice, it still occurs (Abimbola, 2013; Moffatt, 2011).

Over 13,000 journals and publishers are members of COPE (Committee on Publication Ethics, 2022b). COPE guidelines also address authorship, retractions, corrections, and misconduct (Albert & Wager, 2003; Committee on Publication Ethics, 2019a, 2019b, 2022a). None of these documents specifically address authorship issues when misconduct leads to a retraction followed by resubmission and republication. Unlike the ICMJE guidelines, COPE authorship guidelines do not establish clear authorship criteria and defer to scholarly disciplines to define authorship. According to the guidelines:

Editorial, professional, or academic organisations within different specialties have set out criteria and guidelines that members could adopt in their consideration of authorship. The minimum requirements for authorship, common to all definitions, are (1) substantial contribution to the work and (2) accountability for the work that was done and its presentation in a publication. (Committee on Publication Ethics, 2019a)

As one can see, these guidelines also do not settle the question of whether a researcher who has committed misconduct should be named as an author on a resubmitted paper because, like the ICMJE guidelines, they do not specify what it means for a contribution to be “substantial” or for someone to be “accountable.”

Do journal policies provide any guidance for this issue beyond what one finds in ICMJE and COPE? To address this question, we reviewed documents available on websites from fifteen top biomedical journals, ranked by impact factor, which publish original research. We used the website provided by the National Institute of Environmental Health Sciences/NIH (https://tools.niehs.nih.gov/srp/publications/highimpactjournals.cfm) to identify these journals. Previous research has shown that journal policy development is positively associated with impact factor (Resnik et al., 2010, 2016, 2017a). It is likely, therefore, that if none of the top-tier biomedical journals have policies that address the issue we are concerned with in this paper, then very few middle and lower-tier journals have such policies. With regard to policy development, we distinguished between a journal having its own policy and following COPE or ICMJE. We reviewed four types of policies to determine whether they address the misconduct/retract/resubmit issue: authorship policies, retraction policies, correction policies, and misconduct policies. We categorized (or coded) a policy as addressing the issue only if it specifically described or referred to the issue; we did not categorize a policy as addressing the issue if it applied to the issue in a very general way. For example, if a journal had an authorship policy, we did not categorize the policy as addressing to the issue if it provided general criteria for authorship and did not mention authorship in situations when papers are retracted due to misconduct and resubmitted. We independently reviewed and coded the policies and had 100% agreement on our coding. The results of our journal policy review are reported in Table 2.

Discussion of Ethical Issues

As noted previously, ethical issues are not likely to arise when the person who has committed misconduct has not made a significant contribution to the revised and resubmitted paper, because journal policies and authorship norms state that a person should not receive authorship if they have not made a significant contribution (Moffatt, 2011).4

Hypothetical Case

Conflicts are more likely to arise, however, if the person who committed research misconduct has made a significant contribution to revised paper. Consider the following hypothetical case5:

Dr. Y is a well-funded, senior investigator at University U who has published hundreds of papers on how cancer cells evade the immune system. Following a lengthy misconduct investigation, Dr. Y was found to have intentionally fabricated and falsified data in one NIH grant and three published papers. Although Dr. Y did not collect any data, the evidence shows that Dr. Y changed research records to strengthen statistical support for hypotheses that were being tested. As part of its adjudication of this case, University U required the researchers to retract or correct the papers, whichever is appropriate, and terminates Dr. Y’s employment. ORI imposed an indefinite funding debarment on Dr. Y and also required the researchers to retract or correct the papers. The same research team authored all three papers. The team includes Dr. Y, the last and senior author; Dr. X, the first and corresponding author; Dr. Z, Ms. Q, Ms. R, and Mr. T. Dr. X decides to retract two papers because they are so corrupted that nothing can be salvaged from them. However, Dr. X would like to retract, revise, and resubmit a third paper, which includes valid data and novel methods. Dr. X wants to resubmit the paper because its findings advance the understanding of cancer immunology and may have applications for the treatment of cancer. Because Dr. Y committed misconduct, Dr. Y will not be listed as an author on this paper but will be mentioned in the acknowledgments. All of the members of the research team agree with this plan, except Dr. Y, who insists on being included as an author for making significant contributions to the research by designing experiments and developing the methods that were used to test hypotheses. Dr. Y points out that if they had not committed misconduct, there would be no question about authorship. Dr. X agrees with Dr. Y on this point but insists that Dr. Y be removed as an author. Dr. X contacts University U’s research integrity officer (RIO) and asks for some guidance and assistance in resolving this dispute.

This is a difficult case because there are compelling arguments for including Dr. Y as an author and for not including Dr. Y as an author. Also, there are two ways of framing the case: as an authorship dispute or as a situation involving adjudication of misconduct findings. Let’s consider this case as an authorship dispute first.

Analysis of the Case as an Authorship Dispute

Before analyzing this case as an authorship dispute, we need to say a bit more about authorship norms. Authorship practices and policies have traditionally been viewed as supported by four, mutually reinforcing norms: fairness, accountability, honesty, and transparency (1997; Moffatt, 2011, 2013; Rennie et al., 1997; Smith & Williams-Jones, 2012; Smith, 2017; Shamoo & Resnik, 2022).

Fairness (or justice) is a complex concept in moral and political theory that applies to various social issues, including distribution of socioeconomic goods (distributive justice), criminal punishment (retributive justice), reparation of wrongs (rectifactory justice), and procedures for making fair decisions (procedural justice) (Barry, 1989; Rawls, 2001). In scientific research, questions of fairness frequently arise when assigning credit for research contributions (Smith, 2017). Most researchers agree that fairness requires that (1) credit should be given where credit is due and only where it is due; and (2) authorship credit should be awarded for significant contributions, and (3) those who are not named as authors but who have contributed to the research should be acknowledged (Rennie et al., 1997; Smith, 2017). These rules imply that honorary authorship and ghost authorship are unethical because they give credit where credit is not due, in the former case; or fail to give credit where it is due, in the latter (Moffatt, 2011, 2013; Smith, 2017). Ghost authorship can be equated with plagiarism when a person who has made a significant contribution to a research project does not receive any form of credit or acknowledgment.6

Accountability requires that contributors to the research be named as authors or mentioned in the acknowledgments so that other researchers know who did the work if questions arise about the quality, reproducibility, or integrity of the data or methods, or if other scientists want to build upon the research (Rennie et al., 1997). Authors should take responsibility for their research and be able to explain what they did and why (Resnik, 1997). Ideally, an article will include a complete accounting of who contributed to the research and what they did. Those who have substantially contributed to the research may be named as authors while others may be listed in the acknowledgements (Rennie et al., 1997).

Honesty requires that authorship lists and contribution statements (including the “contributed equally” designation) reflect the truth about what happened in a research project (Resnik et al., 2020; Shamoo & Resnik, 2022). Honorary authorship is dishonest because it exaggerates or fabricates the truth and ghost authorship is dishonest because it hides the truth (Matheson, 2011). Viewed in this light, authorship is largely a factual matter because it provides a historical record of how discoveries and innovations are produced. It is important for the research community and future generations of scientists to know who developed the germ theory of disease, discovered the structure of DNA, or invented the polymerase chain reaction (PCR), for example.

Transparency requires that scientific and technical contributions, and financial and other forms of support, be fully disclosed and described in the paper. Transparency is important for promoting accountability and honesty and for mitigating the effects of bias and competing interests (Elliott & Resnik, 2014; Smith, 2017). Readers need to know, for example, whether an author who played a key role in analyzing and interpreting the data had a significant financial stake in the outcome of the research, because this information could be useful in evaluating the credibility of the research (Resnik & Elliott, 2013). Ghost authorship can significantly impair the integrity and trustworthiness of research if someone who made an important contribution to the research is omitted from the authorship list in order to hide their financial interests (Matheson, 2011).

While all of these norms imply that authorship should be granted for significant contributions, none of them define what is meant by ‘significant,’ which is often contested in authorship disputes (Smith et al. 2020a, 2020b). Journal policies list some roles (such as data collection and research design) that qualify as significant, but they also do not provide detailed specifications of what is meant by significant, because deciding whether a contribution is significant is a contextual and discipline-specific determination (Committee on Publication Ethics, 2019a; International Committee of Medical Journal Editors, 2021a; Moffatt, 2018).

For more than a decade, journals have tried to promote responsible authorship by requiring authors to describe their contributions in the paper and sign a statement affirming that they have met the authorship criteria (Resnik et al., 2016; see, for example Nature, 2009). Recently, some journals and institutions have taken this idea a step further by requiring authors to use the Contributor Roles Taxonomy (CRediT) system (McNutt et al., 2018). The CRediT system distinguishes between 14 different categories of activities (or roles) a person could perform on a research project. It includes activities that have been traditionally associated with authorship, such as data collection and research design, and activities that have not been traditionally associated with authorship, such as data curation and project administration. The CRediT system helps to clarify contributions to research but does not define authorship or replace it with contributor roles (Brand et al., 2015).

In applying the norms to this case, we shall assume that Dr. Y has made a significant contribution and that Dr. Y would be named as an author if not for their misconduct. One could argue that Dr. Y should be included as an author for the following reasons. First, fairness requires that Dr. Y receive credit for their contribution; listing Dr. Y in the acknowledgments would not be sufficient. Second, accountability requires that Dr. Y be named as an author so that Dr. Y can help explain what was done if questions arise. Indeed, Dr. Y could be uncooperative if questions arise and they had not been listed as an author. It is important to note, however, that Dr. Y’s coauthors may doubt whether they can be accountable, given that they have already intentionally fabricated and falsified data. Third, honesty requires including Dr. Y as an author because it is dishonest to say that Dr. Y contributed in a manner that only merits an acknowledgment. And fourth, transparency requires that Dr. Y be named as an author because not naming Dr. Y as an author obscures Dr. Y’s actual involvement in the research.

One could argue that Dr. Y should not be included as an author for the following reasons. First, as noted earlier, fairness is a broad concept that involves more than receiving proper credit for one’s contributions. Fairness implies that credit should be received only if it is deserved and one could argue that Dr. Y does not deserve authorship because Dr. Y seriously violated ethical norms. It would be unfair to allow Dr. Y to benefit from this misbehavior, especially given the seriousness of Dr. Y’s transgressions, i.e. Dr. Y is a senior investigator who should have known better and Dr. Y acted intentionally. Second, one could argue that it would be unfair to Dr. Y’s coauthors to include Dr. Y as an author because having Dr. Y’s name on the authorship list could morally taint the research, undermine its credibility, and damage their reputations. Third, one could argue that the other norms—accountability, honesty, and transparency—could be satisfied by acknowledging Dr. Y’s contributions and accurately describing Dr. Y’s role. The authors could use the CRediT system (discussed earlier) to clarify the different contributions that individuals made to the research.

As one can see, this is a difficult case from an authorship perspective. There are good arguments, based on fairness, for including Dr. Y as an author and for not including Dr. Y as an author. Fairness can be interpreted differently (Barry, 1989; Smith, 2017), and well-informed, reasonable scientists might reach different judgments about what counts as fair in this case (Moffatt, 2018; Patience et al., 2019; Smith et al. 2020b). Aggravating and mitigating factors (discussed in Sect. 5.3, below) could strengthen or weaken the case for denying authorship to Dr. Y. If Dr. Y were a trainee or junior investigator who only committed research misconduct recklessly, one would be more inclined to grant authorship to Dr. Y, since it would seem to be unnecessarily punitive and unfair to take away authorship in that situation.

Since fairness can be interpreted as implying contradictory courses of action, what this case may boil down to then is whether the other criteria—accountability, honesty, and transparency—can be satisfied by only mentioning Dr. Y in the acknowledgments. Using the CRediT system might help satisfy these criteria in a situation like this one, but this is unknown, especially since the CRediT system is a new development that has not been widely adopted by journals and institutions or tested. If the authors use the CRediT system to acknowledge Dr. Y’s contribution, they should clearly explain Dr. Y’s role in the research, why the article was originally retracted, and how it has been revised.

Analysis of the Case as Adjudication of a Research Misconduct Finding

We will now analyze this case from the perspective of research misconduct adjudication. Adjudication occurs after there has been an official finding of research misconduct (Mello & Brennan, 2003). Adjudication involves taking administrative actions to address the misconduct and may include imposing a sanction (or sanctions) on the person who is found to have committed misconduct and correcting the research record. Sanctions imposed by academic institutions (ranging from lenient to severe) typically include: required correction of the research record, letter of reprimand, mandated education and training, supervision of research, demotion, and termination of employment. Research sponsors (if there are any) may also take administrative actions, such as: requiring the respondent’s research to be supervised, suspending or terminating the respondent’s grant, prohibiting the respondent from receiving future funding (funding debarment), excluding the respondent from service on committees, and requiring the respondent to correct the research record. Correcting the research record may involve retracting or correcting publications (Redman, 2013).

Several norms apply to misconduct adjudication, including: fairness, deterrence, transparency, accountability, and integrity.

Fairness imposes two requirements on adjudication. First, similar cases should be treated similarly. This requirement, also known as the formal principle of justice, ensures that decisions will be consistent (Rawls, 2001) and is closely related to the norm of integrity (discussed below) because integrity requires that actions and rules or policies be consistent (Shamoo & Resnik, 2022).

Second, sanctions should be proportional to the seriousness of the transgressions (Branham, 2017; Department of Health & Human Services, 1995; Keränen, 2006; Redman & Merz, 2008; Walen, 2020).7 In criminal law, judges can take aggravating or mitigating factors related to the circumstances of the crime into account when sentencing criminals. Aggravating factors, such as the person’s lengthy criminal history, may be used to increase severity of the punishment, while mitigating factors, such as lack of a criminal history may be used to decrease the severity of the punishment (Branham, 2017). Some commonly used aggravating and mitigating factors in misconduct adjudication include (Keränen, 2006; Abdie et al., 2021):

  • The person’s intent (i.e. was the misconduct intentional, knowing, or reckless?)

  • The person’s career stage (i.e. is the person a trainee, junior researcher, or senior researcher?)

  • The person’s history (i.e. has the person committed research misconduct or violated other ethical/legal rules previously?)

  • Evidence of a pattern of misbehavior (i.e. was the misconduct a single incident or did it happen many times?)

  • The impact of the misconduct (i.e. were persons or organizations substantially harmed by the misconduct?)

  • The institution’s responsibility (i.e. was the person provided with adequate education, training, and guidance?)

To illustrate how these factors apply to cases, one could say that intentional data falsification committed by a senior investigator that harms research participants would be more serious (and deserving of more severe sanctions) than reckless falsification committed by a trainee that substantially arms no one (Abdie et al., 2021).

Deterrence requires that sanctions should be likely to deter others from similar crimes (Walen, 2020). For deterrence to be effective, misconduct findings and sanctions should be publicized, so that others can learn about them and be motivated not to commit misconduct. Thus, deterrence is related to the norm of transparency, discussed below. To deter misconduct and promote transparency, ORI publishes its official case summaries in the Federal Register and on its website. The case summaries identify individuals and institutions (Office of Research Integrity, 2022).

Sometimes deterrence may conflict with fairness if legal authorities decide to impose a harsh punishment on a person to send a message to other researchers. For example, in 2006 Elizabeth Goodwin admitted to fabricating and falsifying data on three NIH grant applications. After ORI barred her from receiving PHS funding for three years, she was convicted of defrauding the federal government. She was sentenced to two years of probation and ordered to pay the Department of Health and Human Services $100,000 in restitution (Couzin-Frankel, 2010). The punishments in this case probably had a strong deterrent effect. Although ORI’s sanctions were consistent with those it had imposed in similar cases, one could argue that her conviction for criminal fraud was unfair because researchers who have admitted to far worse types of research misconduct have not been subsequently tried or convicted for fraud (Shamoo & Resnik, 2022).

Transparency requires that misconduct policies, procedures, and findings be disclosed to researchers and to the public. Most US academic institutions make their misconduct policies and procedures available on their websites and publicly acknowledge findings of misconduct once they have completed their investigations and initiated administrative actions (Resnik et al., 2015a). Government funding agencies also publish their misconduct policies and procedures on their websites, and, as noted above, ORI publishes case summaries. The National Science Foundation (NSF) publishes case closeout memoranda; however, these documents do not identify individuals or institutions (National Science Foundation, 2022). To avoid violating confidentiality and damaging reputations unecessarily, misconduct proceedings conducted by institutions or funding agencies should not be made public until they are complete.

Accountability requires that individuals and institutions be held accountable for their contributions to misconduct and take actions to redress harms that have occurred. Although there is a tendency to hold individual respondents solely responsible for misconduct, it is often the case that collaborators, supervisors, and research institutions bear some responsibility as well (Redman, 2013). As part of its accountability, an institution might respond to a misconduct case by revising its research ethics policies, increasing its RCR education and training efforts, providing better support for whistleblowers, or periodically auditing research records (Shamoo & Resnik, 2022; Titus et al., 2008). Correcting the research record, mentioned above, is a key part of redressing harms to the scientific community and larger public. Other ways of responding to the effects of research misconduct, depending on the circumstances, might include: compensating human research subjects for injuries or repaying a sponsor for mismanaged funds.

Finally, integrity requires that institutions develop and follow policies and procedures for dealing with ethical issues and problems in research, and that they administer policies and procedures consistently, impartially, and transparently. Though we mention integrity last, it may be the most important of these institutional norms, because violations of institutional integrity can undermine all other research norms (National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine 2017).

In thinking about this case from the perspective of misconduct adjudication, we should note that adjudication is the responsibility of Dr. Y’s institution and the funding agency, not Dr. Y’s coauthors. Although Dr. Y’s coauthors may view denying authorship to Dr. Y as an appropriate form of punishment, it is not their role—legally or morally—to administer sanctions. Adjudication of misconduct findings should be handled by independent parties who do not have a conflict of interest (Office of Science & Technology Policy, 2000; National Academies of Science, Engineering, and Medicine, 2017). The authors could, however, approach the RIO and ask that Dr. Y not be included as an author as an administrative action. Would this be a good idea?

There are two arguments that may favor of this idea. First, one could argue, based on fairness, that the seriousness of Dr. Y’s misconduct justifies not including Dr. Y as an author on the paper. Though denial of authorship credit would be an unusual administrative action, one could argue that the seriousness of the misconduct supports such a sanction in this case. Second, one could argue, based on deterrence, that Dr. Y should be denied authorship to send a message to other researchers about the seriousness and consequences of research misconduct. Given the importance of authorship in scientific research, there is little doubt that this type of administrative action would have a powerful deterrent effect.

While fairness and deterrence support denying authorship to Dr. Y as an administrative action, there are two compelling arguments against this position. First, denying authorship improperly could encourage the weaponization of misconduct allegations and seriously damage the institution’s integrity. Other researchers who are involved in authorship disputes and who learn that the institution has taken away authorship as a result of a misconduct finding might decide to accuse their adversaries of misconduct. The motivation for weaponizing misconduct allegations could be even stronger if significant financial interests are at stake, such as intellectual property rights and royalties. Indeed, there is evidence that weaponization already occurs. A common situation is when an investigator accuses a collaborator of plagiarism because the collaborator used products of their collaboration without giving proper credit to the investigator (Resnik, 2013). ORI has stated that it regards these cases as authorship disputes, not plagiarism, and that it will refer them to institutions for resolution (Office of Research Integrity, 1994).

Second, authorship disputes are best resolved by the corresponding author with concurrence of members of the research group, not by administrative actions (Albert & Wager, 2003; Smith & Willimas-Jones, 2012). While institutional officials may need to become involved in cases that cannot be resolved at a lower level, this should be an option of last resort. Many institutions have ombudspersons and research ethics faculty and staff who can mediate authorship disputes but who do not have the authority to settle them (Cho et al., 2008).

Conclusion

Situations in which papers are retracted due to misconduct and then resubmitted raise some novel and interesting issues because they involve the convergence of two research ethics domains that are usually dealt with separately—resolution of authorship disputes and adjudication of misconduct findings—and can give rise to conflicts among ethical norms, such as fairness, accountability, deterrence, and integrity, as well as conflicts based on different interpretations of fairness. In this paper, we have shown that these situations do occur and that journal policies do not provide insufficient guidance concerning authorship issues that can occur in these situations.

While there is little dispute that a researcher who has committed misconduct should not be named as an author if that person has not made a significant contribution to the research reported in the revised paper, difficult ethical questions can arise if a researcher has made a significant contribution to research. While some scientists may feel strongly that individuals who commit misconduct should be denied authorship even if they have made a significant contribution to the research, others may feel that authorship should be based on one’s contributions, irrespective of one’s moral transgressions. In cases such as these, aggravating and mitigating factors, such as whether the misconduct was committed intentionally, knowingly, or recklessly, whether the respondent was a senior investigator or trainee, and whether anyone was harmed by the misconduct, may impact moral assessment.

We tend to support the position that a person who makes a significant contribution to a research project should be named as an author on a paper describing the project, even if the paper is a revised version of a paper that was retracted due to that person’s misconduct. The person should be granted authorship not because they are morally worthy of receiving it, but because the scientific community needs to know who made a substantial contribution to the research. Accurate assignment of authorship is important for promoting accountability in research and preserving the integrity and transparency of the scientific record. We recognize, however, that this a complex issue, and that some may disagree with our viewpoint.

A counter argument to our position is that it may be possible to document a researcher’s contributions accurately by mentioning them in the acknowledgements section and describing what they did. The practicality of this option depends, in part, on how flexible journals are willing to be when it comes to publishing acknowledgments sections. If there are space restrictions, additional information related to acknowledgements could be available in the supplementary material. The CRediT system for describing research contributions could be helpful in this regard.

Some commentators have argued that researchers should move away from the authorship model toward a contributor model in which major contributors are authors but all other contributors are named with their contributions carefully described (Rennie et al., 1997; Resnik, 1997; Vasilevsky et al., 2021). If this model were widely adopted, it could help to resolve some of the ethical dilemmas discussed in this paper because it would place less of a premium on authorship and provide meaningful recognition to contributors.

The cases examined in this paper also raise issues related to the adjudication of research misconduct findings. Although some might argue that institutions should deny authorship to authors who have committed misconduct as an administrative action, we think this is an unwise suggestion because it has the potential to weaponize misconduct allegations and seriously damage the institution’s integrity. Authorship disputes are best resolved by corresponding authors with concurrence of members of the research group, not by administrative fiat. Institutions should provide researchers with resources to help them address authorship disputes, but refrain from resolving these disputes administratively, unless absolutely necessary.

We have only scratched the surface of this rich topic. Questions that merit further research and analysis include:

  1. Does the nature of the respondents’ contribution make a difference in the decision to include them as an author on the revised paper? Is there a difference, for example, between collecting data, developing methods or reagents, or writing text or computer code?

  2. What if the research involves a patentable invention? Would authorship and inventorship issues be resolved in the same way?

  3. What is the role of the corresponding author in addressing these issues? What should be done if the corresponding author is the person who committed misconduct?

  4. How should institutions and funders deal with these issues? Should institutions or funders require that authorship be taken away from a respondent as an administrative action?

  5. How should journals deal with these issues? If an article is retracted because of misconduct and resubmitted and the authorship changes, should journals require the authors to include an explanatory note explaining the changes and why they were made?

  6. How do international differences in the definition of research misconduct affect these issues? When there is an international research collaboration, what should be done if an author commits misconduct according to one country’s definition but not another’s?

  7. What do researchers think about these issues? Are they aware of them through personal experience or by hearing about them? Do they think that researchers who commit misconduct should be denied authorship?

We encourage the scientific community to reflect on these issues and for journals and institutions to address them in their policy and guidance.
