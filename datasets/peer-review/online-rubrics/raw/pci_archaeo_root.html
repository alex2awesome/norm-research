PCI Archaeology is a peer review platform to submit preprints in the field of Archaeology.

Preprints positively evaluated are publicly recommended and can be published directly in Peer Community Journal, an open-access, indexed, and generalist journal, free for authors and readers, or can be submitted to PCI-friendly journals or any other journals.

Towards an Easy-to-Use Machine Learning Framework for Cultural Heritage Scientists

Christos Chatzisavvas, Thomas Pappas, Panagiotis Rigas, Nikolaos Mitianoudis, George Pavlidis, Chairi Kiourt, Anestis Koutsoudis, Vassilis Katsouros, and George Ioannakis https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19856328

Manage you Heritage. A ready-to use machine learning framework for cultural heritage practices

Recommended by Mathias Bellat and Anastasia Eleftheriadou based on reviews by 4 anonymous reviewers

In the context of the growing importance of new methods, especially machine learning, for cultural heritage applications (Cacciari and Pocobelli, 2022), one may be lost in the flow of processes, algorithms, and data.

This manuscript proposes a newly developed framework in Python, enabling fully reproducible and accessible data labelling and model training. Data can be directly handled from a page with CVAT integration for segmentation annotations. Currently, 4 deep learning models are available for model training. Custom parameters can also be implemented. While still limited to one-class segmentation, the proposed framework has proven efficient on a textile-domain training dataset.

This paper will enable newcomers and beginners in machine learning segmentation applications to test their models and datasets in a ready-to-use environment. Besides utility for researchers' implementation, it will also benefit the whole community by establishing a standardised protocol for some communities, enabling de facto improvements in reproducibility.

Siligîtes: a QGIS extension to investigate mobility networks in archaeology

Thomas Andre, Christophe Tufféry, Vincent Delvigne https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19632833

Advancing the study of Palaeolithic raw material networks through open-source GIS tools

Recommended by Richard Hewitt and Manuel Alcaraz-Castaño based on reviews by Bruno Gómez de Soler, María Soto and Alejandro Prieto

This article presents the Siligîtes tool, a programmed extension to the QGIS desktop GIS software to assist in the exploration of lithic resource procurement and utilisation networks in the Palaeolithic (Andre et al 2026). It is likely to be especially useful for the study of complex networks between multiple procurement and use/discard locations within individual cultural traditions or technocomplexes which often may cover hundreds of kms. The main contribution of the paper is to reduce computational intensity and processing workflow steps in lithic network analyses by directly integrating the identification of relevant geological formations with the creation of least cost corridors and least distance accessibility zones. Originally developed in French, the tool is now also available in English, which will greatly enhance its reach and potential user base. The tool has been tested by referees, who have suggested improvements which have been addressed. Among the points the referees made in their recommendations, the following stand out: (1) the work provides a significant contribution to GIS-based analysis applied to the study of territorial mobility among hunter-gatherers in relation to the procurement of abiotic resources; (2) it identifies a genuine need in archaeological spatial analysis, and has strong potential to benefit a broad community of researchers and to encourage more transparent and systematic use of spatial modelling tools in archaeology; and (3) it assists in the production of fast, systematic and reproducible spatial analyses based on raw material research. This paper also synthetizes a high quantity of information collected by the Collective Research Project (CRP) ‘Réseaux de lithothèques’ in France. Despite its French context and origin, the work is clearly very relevant to other territories, especially adjacent areas such as Iberia, where lithic procurement networks display similar characteristics and, according to recent studies, were even interconnected with those of France across large areas during the Middle and Upper Palaeolithic (Sánchez de la Torre et al 2026).

From a broader scientific perspective, the experimental nature of this study is also extremely welcome. From our own point of view, too much effort has been expended in the past in presenting single, untested hypotheses around the ancient human use of the territory as absolute truth, and too much computational research is naïve in that it seeks a single best fit model or "right answer". Methodological approaches like this one benefit the collective scientific endeavour by allowing multiple hypotheses to be rapidly tested, explored and compared. In this sense, this research makes an important contribution to the emerging lines of enquiry around uncertainty in Least Cost Analysis, which seeks to draw conclusions about movement in the past from probabilistic approaches derived from multiple possible pathways, see e.g. Lewis (2021), Gravel Miguel and Wren (2018), and our own contribution (Hewitt et al 2025).

In terms of limitations, it should be recognised that this is primarily a methodological contribution developed within the framework of a broader ongoing research programme, and as such, detailed theoretical discussion of Palaeolithic raw material networks and their interpretation is not addressed in depth. Referees in particular suggest that there is room for greater engagement with the archaeological implications of the work. In this sense, the authors note that important context is provided by Delvigne et al, an article currently in press. Future work from these authors addressing aspects like site functions, raw material circulation, or mobility patterns would help strengthen the connection between the technical contribution of this research and its empirical grounding in Palaeolithic mobility studies.

Supporting a Celtic coin die study with image matching and hierarchical clustering for creating a social networks analysis

Sebastian Gampe, Karsten Tolle, Caroline von Nicolai, Markus Möller https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19665595

A Computer-Aided Pipeline for Die Studies: An Example on Celtic Coinage

Recommended by Sofia Kouki based on reviews by Eythan Levy and Simon Bickler

The study by Gampe et al. (2026) sits at the intersection of computational applications in archaeology and numismatics for Celtic coin die studies and builds directly on recent work in computer-assisted die analysis. By integrating Deepcluster for pre-sorting (Caron et al., 2018) and comparing the ORB and DISK (Rublee et al., 2011; Tyszkiewicz et al., 2020) object matching, the authors provide a transparent, domain-specific pipeline that moves the field beyond the "black box" approach. Providing an interactive workflow, the authors put the “numismatist-in-the-loop” that facilitates the expert adjudication. 

In the broader background of computational approaches in coin die studies, previous pipelines integrated image matching algorithms with clustering to analyze these materials recently. In Greek coins, Taylor (2020) demonstrated that die studies are highly amenable to computer vision using CADS (Computer Aided Die Studies) that combine ORB-based detection with hierarchical clustering. However, this was only tested on less challenging numismatic series. More recent work has extended this framework through large-scale hoard analysis using Deepcluster-based pre-sorting and ORB matching (Deligio et al., 2023) and through SSIM (Structural Similarity Index Measure)-based scoring systems that achieve great clustering performance on Roman hoard studies (Labedan et al., 2025).

To my knowledge, only Gampe et al. (2026) focus on Celtic coins, making their study innovative, and they position their proposed pipeline as an extension of previous studies. Methodologically, their contribution lies in (i) integrating DeepCluster as a pre-sorting step for heavily worn coins, (ii) comparing ORB and DISK for keypoint-based matching within a common preprocessing framework, and (iii) embedding the resulting similarity matrices into an interactive hierarchical clustering workflow. 

In doing so, they bridge between earlier ORB-centered systems (Deligio et al., 2023; Taylor, 2020) and SSIM-based scoring systems (Labedan et al., 2025), discussing where deep learning matchers outperform the other and where they remain weaker (e.g., for strong rotations) and how these trade-offs play out on difficult Celtic coins. 

The paper’s strength lies in its methodological transparency, and the authors provide clear justification for their design choices while remaining candid about limitations like rotation sensitivity. Their approach aligns with the current trends in computational numismatics that prioritize robust, benchmarkable workflows over exhaustive interpretation. 

By positioning the underrepresented Celtic series as a demanding test-bed for AI-driven developments, the authors outline a convincing trajectory for future work for die links to social network analysis, and it would be a great opportunity to see how their future work fits this together. 

The revision process has further improved the clarity and contextualization of this paper. Regarding the title, the authors have explained that they wish to retain the CAA 2025 conference title because they aim to publish in the CAA proceedings; in this context, I consider it acceptable to keep the current title for PCI Archaeology as well. Overall, this paper has a significant contribution to coin studies and computational numismatics for the reasons outlined above, and I would like to thank both reviewers and authors for their efforts and time in strengthening this paper.

Replication report for Marwick (2025) “Is archaeology a science?”, including new data from OpenAlex

Alain Queffelec https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19371104

Reproducibility-ception: A reproduction of a bibliometric study with an open dataset

Recommended by Mathias Bellat based on reviews by Zachary Batist, Alan Farahani and 1 anonymous reviewer

The reproducibility of an experiment is one of the bases of the scientific practice (Popper, 2002). In this regard, the paper presented here by Queffelec (2026, member of the board of PCI archaeology), is a perfect example of a reproducibility test. The starting point is to assess the reproducibility of Marwick's (2025) bibliometric study of the scientific “value” of archaeology as a science. In his study, Marwick used the WoS database to extract information and identify general trends in archaeological research practices and the potential for reproducibility. Ongoing efforts have been in this direction, notably with new editorial positions of “Associate Editors for Reproducibility” in some archaeological journals.

The results of the study presented here highlighted two elements. The first, Marwick's article, is reproducible with minor modifications, enhancing the potential to produce truly reproducible articles. Second, it offers slightly different results from those of Marwick (2025), while using the open-access library OpenAlex (Priem et al., 2022). The number of databases increased by thousands, but also became more heterogeneous, producing a more diverse and nuanced view of the results than the WoS database used by Marwick.

The general output suggests, as in Marwick (2025), a scientific tendency for Archaeological publications to be closer to the humanities than to physics or social science. Furthermore, the different journals also present a variety in the number of authors, the length of the title or article, and the reference style, unique to each. This led to the researchers' strategic practices when submitting their work. The OpenAlex database showed some limitations in data curation and in the variety of sources. Overall, this study highlighted the future potential for bibliometric meta-analysis and its reproducibility.

Vulnerability of Monumental Structures to Climate Change The Cases of “Building K” in Epidaurus and the Roman Balneum in Rafina

Emmanouil Tzannidakis, Dimitris Egglezos, Dorina Moullou https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18957254

Assessing two cases of monumental structures and their damage risk due to climate change

Recommended by Anno Hein based on reviews by 2 anonymous reviewers

Within the framework of the present case study the structural integrity of monuments on two archaeological sites in Greece was investigated for assessing their damage and degradation risk related to climate change and potential seismic events (Tzannidakis et al. 2026). The two selected cases concern a Hellenistic to Roman building complex on the archaeological site of Epidaurus in the Peloponnese and a Roman balneum (bathhouse) in Rafina at the coast of Eastern Attica. The monumental structures were investigated integrating civil engineering with climate modeling and seismic risk assessment. The civil engineering approach included the generation of digital models of the built structures and the simulation of their performance for example during earthquakes applying the Finite Element Method (FEM). For climate modeling and the assessment of the climatic risk the Heritage Hazard Index (HHI) proposed by Kapsomenakis et al. (2023) was used. The related climatic degradation expected through thermal stress, moisture variation and material deterioration appears to significantly influence the simulated seismic performance.

The present paper definitely was an important contribution to the Thematic Session on ‘Computational models concerning climate change and its effect on cultural heritage assets’ within the framework of the CAA 2025 in Athens. The main outcome of the present case study is the comprehensive damage risk assessment of monuments on the two selected sites taking into account the environmental conditions expected due to the ongoing climate change and potential seismic events. For this, an integrated approach was selected combining methods from civil engineering and the monitoring of climatological data as well as their modeling in future scenarios.

Nekyia: Descent into Hades – A case study on playing with the Past through Serious Games.

Nikos Tsironis, Angeliki Chrysanthi https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18929480

Between Homer and the Necromanteion: A Serious Game as Interactive Mythological Museum

Recommended by Sebastian Hageneuer based on reviews by Jannis Werner and Kevin Körner

The use of digital interactive media for communicating archaeological knowledge to wider audiences has become an increasingly active area of research within digital archaeology and cultural heritage studies. Serious games — defined as applications designed primarily for purposes other than entertainment — occupy a particularly productive space within this discourse, as they combine narrative engagement with structured learning objectives (Mariotti 2021; Politopoulos & Mol 2021). Despite a growing body of theoretical literature on the potential of archaeogaming and serious game design, empirically grounded case studies that document the full development cycle, user testing, and iterative refinement of a concrete application remain comparatively rare. Nekyia: Descent into Hades addresses this gap directly.

The scientific question at the heart of this case study is both practically and theoretically relevant: can a serious game effectively balance informative archaeological and literary content with sufficient player engagement to constitute a meaningful educational experience? This tension between "edutainment" and scholarly rigour is well known in the field (cf. Vandewalle 2023), and the authors approach it with commendable honesty by subjecting their application to structured user evaluation rather than relying on design intent alone.

As a recommender, I was particularly drawn to this submission for two reasons. First, the integration of photogrammetric 3D models of the Acheron Necromanteion — a real, archaeological site in Preveza, Greece — into a playable narrative environment represents a methodologically significant step. Photogrammetry has become a standard documentation tool in field archaeology. Still, its integration into interactive applications as primary environmental content rather than as supplementary illustration remains relatively uncommon in serious game contexts. Second, the choice of the Homeric Nekyia as narrative framework, supplemented by Virgil's Aeneid, reflects a genuine engagement with primary literary sources that goes beyond surface-level thematic borrowing.

The authors' methodology is appropriately mixed: participant observation during playthroughs, a structured questionnaire, and follow-up interviews with 19 participants. While the sample size is modest, the triangulation of methods provides a more nuanced picture of user experience than questionnaire data alone would permit. The results indicate that the application successfully sustains player engagement, that participants responded positively to the supplemental informational content, and that a notable proportion expressed intentions to seek further knowledge about the Odyssey and to visit the Necromanteion. These are meaningful outcomes for a heritage communication tool.

From a design perspective, Nekyia's most notable feature is its commitment to participatory engagement. Rather than presenting the necromantic ritual as a cutscene or passive narrative, the application requires players to perform the ritual steps themselves. This "learning by doing" approach is well grounded in educational theory (Friedlander 2010) and is here applied with evident care for the archaeological and textual sources that describe the ritual practice.

The peer review process for this submission was notably productive. The two reviewers raised a substantial number of technical and design concerns in their initial evaluations, covering issues ranging from motion sickness induced by first-person camera movement and excessive speed to collider geometry problems, inconsistent cursor behaviour, HUD misalignment, and insufficient navigational guidance. The developer engaged with these concerns systematically and transparently, implementing fixes for the majority of issues and providing detailed justifications for the cases where design decisions were intentionally maintained — most notably the use of darkness and spatial disorientation as historically grounded atmospheric choices reflecting ancient descriptions of the Necromanteion's ritual function. The revised application submitted for final evaluation demonstrates a clear and measurable improvement across most of the flagged dimensions. This iterative responsiveness to peer feedback is itself a model worth noting for the broader serious games development community.

Among the points most positively evaluated by the reviewers were the integration of photogrammetric models derived from real archaeological contexts, the use of ancient literary texts as the basis for in-game dialogue, and the effectiveness of the participatory ritual sequence in creating a sense of player agency within the narrative. I share these assessments and would add that the transparency of the development and evaluation process — evidenced by the full archiving of the developer response document alongside the application files on Zenodo — makes this submission a useful reference point for future case studies in the field.

Nekyia: Descent into Hades is recommended as a well-executed and archaeologically informed contribution to the study and practice of serious games for cultural heritage. It demonstrates that rigorous engagement with primary sources — both literary and material — is compatible with engaging interactive design, and that structured user evaluation, however small in scale, can meaningfully inform iterative development. The application and its accompanying documentation are openly available (Tsironis & Chrysanthi 2025), which further supports its value as a replicable model for the field.

Press Play to Enter: Serious Gaming and the Body at the Asklepieion of Kos

Asja Müller, Martin Kim https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19032680

Walking the Sanctuary: Game Engines as Tools for Praxeological Analysis of Ancient Built Space

Recommended by Sebastian Hageneuer based on reviews by Dajana Ehlers and Despoina Vasiliki Sampatakou

The use of game engines and virtual reality technologies as research tools for analysing ancient built space sits at a productive intersection of digital archaeology, architectural history, and spatial theory. While the potential of immersive digital environments for heritage communication has been widely discussed (Politopoulos & Mol 2021; Morgan 2022), their application as instruments for generating and testing hypotheses about past spatial practice — rather than merely visualising established interpretations — remains comparatively underexplored. This paper addresses that gap directly, with methodological transparency and a clearly defined archaeological object of study.

The paper examines the second-century BC Asklepieion of Kos as a case study for praxeological spatial analysis conducted through a game engine environment. Building on earlier visualisations and functional hypotheses about the sanctuary, the authors investigate how integrating animated human and animal bodies into a digital reconstruction can facilitate analysis of festival logistics, including processional routes, sacrificial areas, observation points, gathering spaces, and the constraints imposed by the site's topography. The theoretical grounding in phenomenological concepts of embodied movement and perception gives the paper a firmer conceptual footing than is common in methodologically oriented contributions to digital archaeology.

As a recommender, I was particularly drawn to this submission for two reasons. First, the explicit framing of the game engine environment as a research tool rather than a communication or outreach product is a valuable and still relatively rare position in the field. The author is clear that the aim is hypothesis generation — examining festival logistics from an embodied, situated perspective that bird's-eye ground plans and static visualisations cannot provide — rather than demonstrating verifiable results in a positivist sense. This epistemic honesty is a strength. Second, the choice of the Asklepieion of Kos as a case study is well motivated: the site's terraced topography, its combination of sacred, processional, and feasting spaces, and the tension between public visibility and restricted access make it a genuinely productive object for this kind of analysis.

The peer review process was substantive and resulted in meaningful revisions. The two reviewers raised important concerns about the paper's initial framing, most notably the imprecise use of the term "gamification" and insufficient engagement with the literature on embodiment in digital heritage contexts. Both points were addressed seriously in the revised version. The authors deleted the concept of gamification from the paper — rightly, given that the project does not employ game design mechanics such as reward systems, missions, or interactive affordances — and replaced it with "serious gaming" in the specific sense of using a game engine to create a playable research environment. The theoretical discussion of embodiment was substantially expanded, with explicit reference to Merleau-Ponty and Gibson, and a brief but honest acknowledgement of the current model's limitations with respect to non-normative bodies and social diversity. The anonymous reviewer also raised a pointed and legitimate critique of the uniform, all-white figure models used in the reconstruction, as well as the interpretive and political implications of these visual choices. The authors' response — acknowledging the issue, explaining the small team's practical constraints, and signalling ongoing work toward a full-colour version — is reasonable. However, this remains an area where further development is both necessary and, as the reviewer rightly observed, not merely a technical but an interpretive matter.

Among the aspects most positively evaluated by both reviewers were the clarity and coherence of the methodological section, the well-justified selection of the Asklepieion as a case study, and the logical integration of the results within current research. I share these assessments. The paper's most notable contribution is its demonstration that game engine environments can serve as a medium for praxeological thinking about past space: by placing the analyst's body within a reconstructed festival context, the approach foregrounds questions of movement, sightlines, crowding, and logistical feasibility that remain largely invisible in plan-based analysis. The three accompanying videos — documenting processional routes, sacrificial activities, and feasting — make this contribution tangible in a way that text and static figures alone could not.

Press Play to Enter is recommended as a methodologically grounded and intellectually honest contribution to the growing literature on game technologies as research tools in archaeology. Its explicit acknowledgement of the experimental and interpretive nature of the approach, combined with its concrete engagement with a specific site and set of spatial questions, makes it a useful reference point for future work in this area.

The “fish-bone galleries” of Lyon, France: combining archaeological and geological 3D modelling to understand a mysterious ancient underground structure

Hervé Tronchère-Cottet, Catherine Pothier, Philippe Dessaint, Christian Obrecht https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18826402

Integrating Digital and Geological Approaches to Reassess the “Fish-Bone Galleries” of Lyon, France

Recommended by James Stuart Taylor based on reviews by Danilo Campanaro and 1 anonymous reviewer

The subterranean “fish-bone galleries” beneath Lyon’s Croix-Rousse hill occupy a distinctive place in Roman archaeology. They are clearly significant, clearly ancient, and yet difficult to explain through ordinary typological comparison. Tronchère-Cottet, Pothier, Dessaint and Obrecht (2026) address this long-standing problem by combining geological modelling, 3D reconstruction and thermal simulation to test a functional hypothesis for the network. The study is situated within the wider context of Roman Lugdunum, a major political, religious and economic centre associated with the annual assembly of the Three Gauls, and it speaks more broadly to current archaeological interest in how digital and geoscientific methods can be used to evaluate difficult interpretive questions when excavation evidence is limited or ambiguous.

What makes the article particularly interesting is the nature of the archaeological question. The galleries are unusual in form, extensive in scale, and apparently without a convincing equivalent elsewhere in the Roman world. The authors set out this problem clearly: conventional explanations such as water management or minting are not well supported by the physical evidence, whereas the possibility that the southern sector functioned as storage for perishable goods associated with the Three Gauls assembly can be framed as a testable hypothesis. That interpretive move is important. Rather than simply multiplying possibilities, the paper asks whether the galleries’ placement, architecture and environmental behaviour are actually consistent with sustained storage use. 

I decided to recommend the article primarily because of the way its methodology is tied directly to archaeological reasoning. This is not an instance of digital modelling being used decoratively or retrospectively. Each component of the workflow addresses a specific dimension of the problem. The authors build a 3D geological model of the hill from more than 600 borehole and pseudo-borehole data points using GemPy; reconstruct the galleries from LiDAR and other survey evidence in Rhino; and then use KoZiBu, supplemented by simplified CFD analysis, to simulate the thermal conditions inside the network. What is notable is the coherence of this design: geology, morphology and microclimate are brought together in order to assess whether the structure would have provided the stable conditions needed for long-term storage of organic goods.

The main results are both clear and archaeologically meaningful. The geological model indicates that the deeper southern galleries were excavated in dry strata, above the substratum and away from the main aquifers, suggesting that geological conditions constrained or guided Roman construction choices. The thermal simulations then show that the deepest parts of the system would have maintained a buffered regime, remaining warmer than outside air in winter and cooler in summer, stabilising at around 14 °C. Taken together, these findings support the interpretation of the southern sector as a plausible cold-storage environment. At the same time, the paper avoids overstatement: the northern sector differs in form and depth, and the authors sensibly leave open the possibility that it had an additional or alternative function. That combination of analytical ambition and interpretive restraint is one of the article’s main strengths. 

The article also benefited from peer review. In the revised version, the chronological evidence is easier to assess, notably through the clearer presentation of the radiocarbon datings; the figures are more informative; and the workflow and reconstruction are easier for readers to follow. The revisions did not alter the scientific core of the paper, but they improved its transparency and made the argument more durable as a point of reference for future work.

The reviewers appreciated many of the same qualities that led me to support the paper: its originality, the care of its interdisciplinary design, and the fact that its conclusions are presented cautiously rather than rhetorically. What I particularly appreciated, as recommender, was the way the study makes digital archaeology genuinely explanatory. The modelling does not stand apart from the interpretation; it is the means by which the authors reconstruct the relationship between Roman engineering choices, hill stratigraphy and subterranean environmental conditions. Although I accepted the role of recommender because the paper had been submitted to a session I co-organised, my recommendation rests on the scientific interest of the question and on the quality of the article’s methodological and interpretive contribution. In that respect, I see this paper as a strong example of how integrative digital methods can advance archaeological understanding of otherwise enigmatic structures.

Crossing software boundaries: From iDig and ODK to QField for archaeological field documentation

Kyriakos Sgouropoulos, Periklis Chrysafakoglou, Ilias Ladenis https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.19054070

Adapting Digital Fieldwork through Mobile GIS: Aligning Workflows with the Spatial Nature of Archaeological Data

Recommended by Quentin Drillat and Killian Regnier based on reviews by Christophe Tuffery, Hallvard Indgjerd and Guiseppe Guarino

The rapid integration of digital technologies into archaeological fieldwork has profoundly reshaped how data are recorded, structured, and mobilized between field and laboratory environments. This evolution was a central theme of Session S24, “Digital Fieldwork Documentation in Archaeology: Innovations, Challenges and Standards,” at the Computer Applications and Quantitative Methods in Archaeology (CAA) 2025 conference, where the study by Sgouropoulos and colleagues was originally presented. The paper, “Crossing software boundaries: From iDig and ODK to QField for archaeological field documentation” (Sgouropoulos et al. 2026), offers a timely and methodologically grounded contribution to ongoing discussions on digital infrastructures in archaeology.

The article is based on two well-documented case studies, focusing respectively on excavation and surface survey contexts. In both cases, the authors analyse transitions from established digital recording systems, iDig and ODK, to a mobile GIS-based workflow centered on QField. Rather than presenting a purely technical comparison, the study adopts a reflexive perspective on digital practices, highlighting how choices of software ecosystems directly impact data structure, interoperability, and long-term research potential.

A key strength of the paper lies in its detailed and transparent description of workflows. The excavation case study demonstrates how QField enables a fully GIS-native environment, integrating data collection, visualization, and post-processing within a single system. The survey case study further illustrates the analytical advantages of near-real-time visualization, allowing adaptive decision-making during fieldwork. In both contexts, the authors convincingly show that the elimination of intermediate data transformation steps and the direct integration of spatial and attribute data significantly enhance both efficiency and data quality.

Beyond these technical contributions, the article makes an important conceptual argument. The effectiveness of digital field documentation does not depend on any single software solution, but on the capacity of research teams to transition between systems while maintaining methodological coherence and data integrity. This emphasis on adaptability, openness, and interoperability aligns closely with current discussions around FAIR data principles and sustainable digital archaeology.

Crucially, this contribution also highlights, implicitly but convincingly, that archaeological data are fundamentally spatial by nature. Whether in excavation or survey contexts, archaeological recording always involves the articulation of spatial entities such as features, units, and finds with associated attributes and relationships. In this respect, the limitations observed in systems such as iDig or ODK stem not only from technical constraints, but also from a partial misalignment between their underlying data structures and the inherently spatial logic of archaeological data. By contrast, mobile GIS environments such as QField are intrinsically designed to manage this integration, allowing geometry, attributes, and spatial relationships to be recorded, visualized, and analysed within a unified framework.

The comparison between closed, specialised systems and open GIS-based environments is therefore particularly instructive. While tools such as iDig and ODK offer optimized workflows for specific tasks, they introduce limitations in terms of interoperability, scalability, and long-term data reuse. By contrast, the QField ecosystem, through its integration with QGIS, PostGIS, and open data formats, supports a more resilient and adaptable approach to archaeological documentation, even if it requires a higher level of initial technical expertise.

We recommend this article because it provides both a rigorous empirical evaluation and a clear conceptual framework for understanding current transitions in digital archaeological practice. By grounding its arguments in concrete field applications while engaging with broader methodological issues, the paper offers valuable insights for archaeologists seeking to design workflows that are not only efficient, but also fully aligned with the fundamentally spatial nature of archaeological data.

Spatiotemporal reconstruction of pot burial excavations

Melina K. Grigoriadi, Georgios Papaioannou https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.18877160

Documenting the Archaeological Excavation Process through Sequential 3D Modelling: The Case of a Pot Burial from Phaleron

Recommended by Quentin Drillat and Killian Regnier based on reviews by Rosie Campbell and Géraldine Sachau-Carcel

The integration of digital recording technologies into archaeological practice has fundamentally transformed how excavation processes are documented, analysed, and communicated. In recent years, three-dimensional recording techniques have increasingly enabled archaeologists to capture not only static archaeological remains but also the temporal dynamics of excavation itself. This topic formed a central theme of Session S24, “Digital Fieldwork Documentation in Archaeology: Innovations, Challenges and Standards,” at the Computer Applications and Quantitative Methods in Archaeology (CAA) 2025 conference, where the work of Grigoriadi and Papaioannou was originally presented. Their study exemplifies how emerging digital documentation techniques can enhance both archaeological recording and the communication of excavation processes to wider audiences.

The paper by Grigoriadi & Papaioannou (2026) presents a workflow combining Structure-from-Motion (SfM) photogrammetry with interactive 3D visualization to document the micro-excavation of a pot burial from the Phaleron Delta Cemetery in Attica, Greece. Through sequential image acquisition and photogrammetric reconstruction, the authors generated a series of three-dimensional models capturing successive micro-excavation​ stages. These models were subsequently integrated into an interactive application developed in the Unity game engine, allowing users to explore both the spatial configuration and temporal progression of the excavation through a dynamic interface. This approach highlights the potential of digital documentation not only as a recording method but also as a tool for reconstructing and communicating the inherently destructive process of archaeological excavation.

​We decided to recommend this article because it convincingly demonstrates how relatively accessible technologies can be combined into a coherent workflow that documents excavation processes at a high level of spatial and temporal detail. The study shows that sequential photogrammetric recording can effectively capture the evolving stratigraphic context of a micro-excavation, while the development of an interactive visualization environment enables both specialists and non-specialists to explore the excavation sequence in an intuitive way. The authors further evaluate the usability of their application through a small user study, showing that interactive 3D representations can facilitate engagement and understanding among diverse audiences​.

​Beyond the specific case study of a pot burial excavation, the methodology proposed by Grigoriadi and Papaioannou offers a transferable framework for integrating photogrammetric documentation with interactive visualization tools. As digital recording increasingly becomes standard practice in archaeological fieldwork, such approaches highlight the potential of combining 3D documentation, temporal reconstruction, and interactive visualization to create richer records of excavation processes and to broaden the accessibility of archaeological knowledge. In this respect, the study contributes to ongoing discussions within digital archaeology concerning the role of immersive visualization and interactive media in both research and public dissemination​.​​
