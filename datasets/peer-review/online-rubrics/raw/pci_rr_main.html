How Interviewees Determine What Interviewers Want to Know

David A. Neequaye, Alexandra Lorson, Holly K. Barnett https://osf.io/qay3v/files/7e8sk

Decoding Interviewer’s Intent: Question Specificity and the Inference of Interviewer’s Informational Goals

Recommended by Yikang Zhang based on reviews by Bruno Verschuere and 1 anonymous reviewer
Investigative interviews are structured social interactions in which interviewers seek information from interviewees to achieve specific objectives (e.g. Neequaye, 2023). Across contexts such as eyewitness recall and intelligence gathering (e.g. Geiselman et al., 1986; Granhag & Hartwig, 2015), interviewees must first infer the interviewer’s goals before deciding whether to cooperate with or resist their requests.
 
In a previous study, Neequaye and Lorson (2023) reported an unexpected finding: interviewees tended to assume that interviewers were interested in all the information they possessed about a topic, regardless of how specific the questions were. The current study by Neequaye et al. (2026) aimed to replicate this finding while addressing two potential confounds in the original research.
 
Replication 1 employed a within-subjects design manipulating question specificity, whereas Replication 2 used a between-subjects design. In both studies (Replication 1: N = 318; Replication 2:  N = 292), participants first read case-relevant information and were then presented with interview questions. They subsequently indicated, via free-text responses, what they believed the interviewer wanted to know.
 
Results showed that more specific questions led interviewees to focus more narrowly on details that would provide pragmatically appropriate responses. Consistent with predictions, interviewees’ disposition – cooperative, semi-cooperative, or resistant – did not affect these information selections. Contrary to expectations, however, interviewees remained equally confident in their judgments regardless of question specificity.  
 
These findings offer practical insight into how interviewers should formulate questions. At the same time, the inclusive effects for confidence suggest that subjective confidence ratings may have limited value in this context. Because the task was not incentivized, future research could strengthen participant engagement by introducing motivational elements.

Real-time fMRI-triggered experience-sampling: a proof-of-concept study

Tiara Bounyarith, David Braun, Aaron Kucyi https://osf.io/as2q9/files/d3kmc

Capturing spontaneous thoughts using real-time fMRI-triggered experience sampling

Recommended by Gemma Learmonth based on reviews by Chris Chambers and Michal Ramot
In the past, the neural correlates of mind wandering have been investigated by probing participants with experience sampling ratings at random times during neuroimaging scans. Analytic approaches of such studies typically set the nature of the thought as a predictor and activity in brain areas of interest as the dependent variable. While this procedure has been informative, it yields variability in thought onset relative to probe onset as well as variability in the onset of neural events of interest relative to probe onset. Ingeniously, Bounyarith et al. (2026) argue that a more efficient procedure would be to use brain activity (in previously identified regions of interest) to trigger a thought probe, so that the normal method is reversed: brain activity becomes the predictor, and the nature of a thought the dependent variable. They call their method "real-time fMRI-triggered experience-sampling" (rt‑fMRI‑ES).
 
The authors chose two regions that are hypothesized to predict different subjective states. In particular, they tested the claims that that dorsal anterior insular cortex (daIC) triggered probes would elicit higher arousal ratings than non‑daIC‑triggered probes, and that PMC‑triggered probes would elicit lower external‑attention ratings than non‑PMC‑triggered probes. The authors successfully implemented rt‑fMRI‑ES in 60 participants. The results provide a mixed pattern: the daIC hypothesis was not supported, but the PMC hypothesis was confirmed. Probes triggered by PMC activation were associated with significantly lower external‑attention ratings than other trials. These findings demonstrate the feasibility of the rt‑fMRI‑ES method, and offer initial validation that real‑time neural events can be used to efficiently capture spontaneous thought states.

Effects of partial sleep deprivation on sustained attention and inhibitory control in a dual-task paradigm

Marta Milanesi, Nicola Canessa, Claudia Gianelli https://osf.io/tehy2/files/qx2ez

Multitasking is hard but sleep loss doesn’t necessarily make it harder

Recommended by Chris Chambers based on reviews by Sarah Schoch
Acute partial sleep deprivation, corresponding to a single night of reduced sleep, is a familiar experience even for people without long-term sleep problems. Lack of sleep has negative consequences for cognitive functioning, but little is known about how acute partial sleep deprivation affects our ability to do two tasks at once, as daily life often demands. In the present study, Milanesi et al. (2026) tackled this question by measuring the performance cost (relative to single-task conditions) when a concurrent motor task (Finger Tapping, FT) is added to a test of inhibitory control (Go/No-go task, GNG) or of sustained attention (Psychomotor Vigilance Task, PVT). Sleep deprivation was manipulated in healthy young adults with an habitual sleep duration of 7-9 hours. Each participant was tested after a night of normal sleep (9 hour sleep opportunity, from 12am - 9am) and after a night of partial sleep deprivation (5 hour sleep opportunity, from 1am - 6am), with the critical test sessions separated by one week and the order of conditions counterbalanced across participants. In these two sessions, participants performed three single tasks (FT; GNG; PVT) and two dual tasks (GNG-FT; PVT-FT), allowing the dual task effects to be quantified for the GNG and PVT tasks.
 
Bayesian t-tests were used to confirm the presence of a general dual-task effect for the GNG and PVT tasks separately. For each task for which this condition was met, a Bayesian paired t-test was used to test whether the dual-task effect was stronger (reflecting greater interference) after partial sleep deprivation than after normal sleep. Data collection proceeded according to a preregistered sequential sampling plan, but the evidential stopping threshold (BF≥6 or ≤1/6) was not reached for all hypotheses. Recruitment therefore continued to the maximum planned sample size, after which preregistered exclusion criteria were applied, yielding a final analysed sample of N=46.
 
The study successfully induced robust dual-task interference effects across both inhibitory control and sustained attention, confirming the effectiveness of the experimental paradigm. However, contrary to the hypotheses, no reliable increase in dual-task costs was observed following acute partial sleep deprivation. Bayesian analyses provided only anecdotal evidence for increased interference for inhibitory control, and no evidence for such an effect in sustained attention. Exploratory analyses suggested a more nuanced pattern, with possible domain-specific or task-dependent effects and indications of increased response variability under sleep deprivation, but these findings remain tentative.
 
Overall, these findings challenge a simple resource-depletion account in which sleep loss uniformly exacerbates interference, instead suggesting that the effects of acute partial sleep deprivation on dual-tasking may be subtle, selective, and potentially shaped by compensatory strategies or task demands. By demonstrating strong and reliable dual-task effects alongside limited evidence for modulation by sleep loss, the study makes an important contribution to the literature on sleep and attention, with implications for understanding cognitive performance in everyday multitasking contexts.

Leonardo Bergmann, Christina Bauer, Andrei Cimpian, Nina Vaupotic, Lucia Volpi, Anna Fekete, Fran Miocic-Stosic, Aljoscha Dreisörner https://osf.io/au6pz/files/gzqye

How do brilliance beliefs and institutional support impact PhD students’ mental health?

Recommended by Daniel Dunleavy based on reviews by Chris Chambers and Sreejata Dutta
Although there has been increased emphasis on the well-being and mental health of graduate students, the determinants of poor mental health remain understudied. For example, while researchers have explored the impact of cultural beliefs around ability (i.e., "brilliance beliefs") among first-generation students (Bauer et al., 2023), and have linked these beliefs to anxiety (e.g., Bauer et al., 2025), how these factors impact the mental health of PhD students is poorly understood. In this Stage 1 programmatic registered report, Bergmann et al. (2026) describe a longitudinal study investigating the impact of student-level factors (e.g., brilliance beliefs) and institutional supports on the mental health of PhD students at one university, across a four-month period. 
 
The proposed study involves administering surveys to PhD students in the natural and social sciences at two points in time. The first registered report utilizes a quasi-experimental design to examine how awareness of and delivery of institutional supports (e.g., self-help groups, psychoeducational seminars on mental health and wellness) relate to changes in mental health over time. The second registered report will focus on whether and how brilliance beliefs predict PhD student mental health outcomes, such as anxiety and depression, both cross-sectionally and longitudinally.
 
Two expert reviewers with methodological and field expertise assessed the Stage 1 manuscript over three rounds of in-depth review. Based on detailed and considered responses to the reviewers' comments, the recommender judged that the manuscript met the Stage 1 criteria and therefore awarded in-principle acceptance (IPA).

Using Shakespeare to Answer Psychological Questions: Complexity and Mental Representability of Character Networks

Christian M. Thurn, Simone Sebben, Zoran Kovacevic https://osf.io/pvzks/files/vht3g

Complexity of Shakespeare’s Social Networks

Recommended by Veli-Matti Karhulahti based on reviews by Tomáš Lintner, Matus Adamkovic and 1 anonymous reviewer
The rapid methodological development in digital humanities keeps opening new possibilities to better understand our cultural artifacts and, in the process, also ourselves. Some of the historically most influential works of literary human culture are the plays of Shakespeare, which continue to be read and treasured around the world. Although the social networks of Shakespeare’s plays have attracted scientific attention for already more than two decades (Stiller et al. 2003), the understanding of their complexity in terms of character networks remains limited and not fully contextualized in the larger landscape of European drama.
 
In the present registered report, Thurn and colleagues (2026) apply Kolmogorov complexity analysis to investigate the social networks in 37 existing plays of Shakespeare. The authors replicate some of the original work by Stiller et al. (2003) and situate the findings in a larger regional context by further analyzing over 3,000 plays available in the European Drama Corpus. Ultimately, the authors explore the relationships between Kolmogorov complexity and the size of character networks as well as the robustness of their results in relation to possible researcher decisions in the analytic process.
 
Across four studies, the authors found the Kolmogorov complexity of character networks to be highly correlated with the number of speaking characters, whereas Shakespeare’s plays lie at the upper end of the complexity distribution. On the other hand, the researcher degrees of freedom clearly affected absolute complexity estimates. The results enable interesting reflections on the cognitive limits of human social network processing, but such reflections ential caution and require follow-up work to be investigated in detail.

Do line bisection judgements and simultaneous bilateral attention share neural activation patterns in posterior parietal cortex?

Nicola E. Burns, Catriona L. Scrivener, Edward H. Silson, Robert D. McIntosh https://osf.io/stbc8/files/fygkw

Is line bisection an endpoint-attention task?

Recommended by Marietta Papadatou-Pastou based on reviews by 2 anonymous reviewers
This study by Burns et al. (2026) investigates whether line bisection judgements and simultaneous bilateral attention rely on shared neural activation patterns within posterior parietal cortex. The authors propose that the core operation underlying bisection is the concurrent representation of two spatial endpoints, and test this by comparing fMRI activation during line bisection and a dot reproduction task requiring attention to two lateralised locations. Using a within-subjects design, they plan to quantify between-task similarity through both spatial overlap (Dice coefficient difference, ΔD) and pattern similarity (reliability-corrected correlation, rc), with clearly specified thresholds for confirming their hypotheses​. The study is theoretically well grounded, methodologically rigorous, and transparently preregistered, with detailed analysis plans and outcome-neutral criteria. The proposed tests provide a strong and complementary assessment of the central claim.​

Conversational Remembering about Personal Lived Experiences: Shared Reality and Autobiographical Reflection

Kristi A. Costabile, Abby S. Boytos, Grace Wasinger, and Emma Bognar https://doi.org/10.31234/osf.io/arx5v_v3

Communicative processes in autobiographical remembering

Recommended by Louis J Moses based on reviews by Elaine Reese and Karen Salmon
In our everyday lives we frequently discuss past events from our lives with others. Such conversational remembering of autobiographical events is thought to be important for forming, developing, and consolidating the self concept, for helping create and mantain interpersonal closeness and connection with others, and for guiding future decisions. In these ways, autobiographical memory can help give purpose and meaning to our lives.
 
The broad goal of this study by Costabile et al. (2026) was to examine in closer detail how communicative processes in conversational remembering influence self, social, and future outcomes. Building on prior work, the research did this in two quite different, complementary ways. Study 1  employed an experimental design which manipulated the extent to which rememberers experience “shared reality” with their audience. 1200 online participants (1095 after exclusions) were recruited from Prolific and randomly assigned to either a high shared reality condition (told their audience believes autobiographical reflection is important for identity) or a low shared reality condition (told their audience does not believe it is important). They were then asked to write an essay for their audience in which they reflected on either a high self-esteem or a low self-esteem event from their lives. Finally they completed measures assessing the self, social, and future outcomes mentioned earlier.
 
Study 2 took a very different approach, using a more ecologically valid, daily-diary method in which communicating about autobiographical events was examined in real-life, everyday contexts. For 10 consecutive days, 504 participants (343 after exclusions) from a US midwest university community were asked to recount a conversation involving an autobiographical event that took place earlier in the day. After controlling for relevant individual difference characteristics, the researchers assessed whether shared reality experienced during these conversations relates to self, social, and future outcomes.
 
The two methods employed in these studies have complementary strengths and weaknesses (experimental rigor vs. ecological validity). In both studies shared reality was found to be an important component of communicative processes enhancing a range of memory outcomes. By taking both approaches, the research sheds light on how reflecting upon and discussing past autobiographical events influences important facets of identity, social conection, and future behavior.
