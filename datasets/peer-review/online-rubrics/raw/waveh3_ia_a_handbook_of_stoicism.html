Furthermore, for the Stoics, the Logos is Providence (in Greek, pronoia), because it gives to each being what it needs to exist, like a general providing each soldier with the equipment (in Greek, paraskeue) they need for war, and it is also fate (in Greek, heimarmene), because it determines, through efficient causality, the chain of events that occur in the Cosmos. COSMOPOLITANISM In Classical Antiquity and the Hellenistic and Roman periods, the Stoics acted discreetly in moral terms. We do not see them urging the crowd to convert to Stoicism. One of the foundations of this posture is that the Stoics, heirs of Socratic thought, consider that progress through philosophy must be sought by free choice. They did indeed propagate their teachings (through dissemination texts and public lectures), but they explained them only to those who, by their own will, sought them. Philosophical exercise must be carried out uncoerced.* The practitioner of Stoicism should only do something and only refrain from doing something when they know the reason for doing or not doing it. The process of moral development presupposes a free individual who is willing to undertake it voluntarily. Training, which does not take enlightenment into account, is outside of this process, since it cannot be a proper action of a free human being to do something not knowing why it is right, but only because someone determines it coercively. Vices, Virtues and Passions in Stoicism To effectively perform the self-examination exercise mentioned above, the philosophy student must have at hand certain fundamental concepts of Stoicism. Among them, those of virtue (in Greek, arete) and passion (in Greek, pathos) stand out. We can say that passions are our first and unquestionable reality, because it is possible to doubt the existence of things perceived by the senses, but not of emotions. Think for a moment: how could we doubt, for example, that we feel anger when we actually feel anger? The Stoics understand passions as movements in the human soul, considering some good and others bad. In the physical sense, the good emotions make an orderly and symmetrical movement in the soul, and the bad ones produce a disorderly and chaotic movement. In the epistemic sense, emotions are the counterpart of correct or mistaken opinions or beliefs, being good in the former case and bad in the latter. The basic idea behind this conception is the fact that the natural state of the human soul is tranquility, the absence of agitation (in Greek, ataraxia). Thus, a bad emotion consists of an asymmetric and irrational movement in the soul that destabilizes it and agi- tates it violently. The bad emotion, by its association with a false belief about reality, translates into a lack of knowledge or, in Stoic 18 Seneca, Letter 41—Divinity in Man. 98 A HANDBOOK OF STOICISM terminology, constitutes a vice (in Greek, kake). The good emotion, on the other hand, is associated with true beliefs. The Stoics have, therefore, an epistemic conception of passions, which, according to Chrysippus, can be understood as the result of assent (in Greek, synkatathesis)"» to judgments. Therefore, fear would result from assenting to the proposition “there is an imminent evil and it is suitable for me to avoid it” and the thing is not actually an evil; suffering, “there is a present evil and it is suitable for me to reject it” and the thing is not actually an evil; appetite, “there is an imminent good and it is suitable for me to seek it” and the thing is not actually a good; and pleasure, “there is a present good and it is suitable for me to enjoy it” and the thing is not actually a good. The Stoics subdivided those four basic emotions in subspecies. How foolish it is to ask the Immortal Gods for what you can obtain for yourself. Hands should not be raised to the heavens, nor should one implore the temple guardian to allow us to reach the ear of the statue, as if we could be better heard; the Divinity is near you, it is with you, it is within you. Thus, I say, Lucilius, a sacred spirit dwells within us, observing our evils and goods, and being our guardian. As the Divinity is treated by us, so it treats us. No one is truly a good man without the Divinity. Could anyone rise above fortune unless helped by It? It gives noble and righteous counsel: In each good man dwells a god, but what god it is, is uncertain. If a dense forest presents itself to you, obstructing the view of the sky with intertwined branches, with ancient trees that rise to an uncommon height, this elevation of the forest and the solitude of the place and, in the clearing, the awe of the dense and continuous shadow will produce in you faith in the Divinity.
