Can peer review accolade awards motivate reviewers? A large-scale quasi-natural experiment

Abstract

The utilization of accolade awards to motivate reviewers is widespread, but their effectiveness remains uncertain. We aim to explore how receiving an accolade award affects reviewers’ subsequent number of reviews. In contrast to small-scale, unrepresentative experiments, we perform a large-scale, global, and all-disciplinary analysis based on quasi-natural experiments. By integrating the Publons, ORCID, MAG, SciSciNet, and OpenAlex databases, a large dataset that tracks the reviewers’ annual number of reviews and their bibliometric indicators is compiled, encompassing 179,794 individuals. Among them, 6605 individuals who receive the “Publons Global Peer Review Award” in 2018 are constituted as the experimental group. Those non-winners are then matched to form a control group. Using propensity score matching (PSM), 711 and 762 reviewers are matched as the experimental and control groups respectively. An analysis employing the difference-in-differences (DiD) method is conducted to examine the impact of an accolade award on reviewers’ subsequent number of reviews. It is found that, following the receipt of an accolade award, reviewers, on average, reviews about four fewer manuscripts, with the reduction exhibiting a V-shaped pattern. Additional analyses are conducted to examine how individual differences and socio-economic factors influence the awarding effects. Besides, we analyze the mechanisms underlying the awarding effect and propose strategies to motivate reviewers. In brief, the sharp decrease in marginal utility of accolades, the voluntary nature of peer review, and the unexpected properties of accolade awards are the potential mechanisms that generate the negative effect. The academic community should reassess the existing incentive strategies for reviewers.

Introduction

Motivating reviewers is key to supporting the basic operation of the peer review system. While previous research predominantly emphasizes reviewers’ motivation to improve review quality, it is also vital to consider reviewers’ motivation to complete a sufficient number of reviews. In 2018, a survey published in Nature News (Vesper, 2018) reveals that journal editors consider identifying reviewers the most challenging task, and the shortage of reviewers has been a persistent and ongoing challenge (DeLisi, 2022). Nowadays, the primary approach to incentivizing reviewers is by establishing accolade awards (Zaharie and Seeber, 2018). We can see that numerous academic journals and publishers have instituted annual accolade awards, such as “2022 Outstanding Reviewers”, to acknowledge reviewers’ contributions throughout the year. Examples of such editorials are shown here (Brooks, 2023; Wu, 2023; Yom, 2022).

Despite the widespread use of accolade awards, there is a lack of empirical research to comprehensively investigate their impact on the reviewers’ subsequent number of reviews. Intuitively, incentives may boost the motivation of recipients, but many contradictory studies have demonstrated that awards can elicit both positive and negative motivational effects (Hidi, 2016). Therefore, it is imperative to examine the outcomes of peer review accolades.

Publons is the largest global peer review platform. This platform grants the annual “Global Peer Review Award” to outstanding reviewers. The accolade is open to all reviewers worldwide, regardless of their field of expertise. It provides an opportunity to explore the influence of the peer review awards on reviewers’ motivation in a representative way. In this study, with the propensity score matching (PSM) method, 6605 winning reviewers are matched as the experimental group, while the non-winners are designated as the control group. The difference-in-differences (DiD) method is employed to investigate the impact of the Publons Global Peer Review Award on reviewers’ subsequent motivation.

This study aims to address the following purposes:

  1. (1)

    Investigate the impact of peer review accolade awards on the subsequent motivation of reviewers (e.g., the number of reviews), using a representative sample and quasi-natural experiment method.

  2. (2)

    Explore how individual and socio-economic factors influence the awarding effect.

  3. (3)

    Based on the empirical results, discuss the mechanisms underlying the awarding effect, and propose strategies for motivating reviewers.

Theories and backgrounds

The peer review motivations

Peer review serves as a main filter for modern academic communication (Tennant and Ross-Hellauer, 2020). Existing research on peer review primarily focuses on improving quality and increasing quantity. Therefore, in terms of motivating reviewers, increasing their review quantity and improving the quality of their reviews are two crucial aspects. In our study, we only focus on the quantity of reviews. The term “motivation” in the following text also underscores the number of reviews.

We still briefly introduce the motivation behind review quality here, although it is not our primary focus. In terms of review quality, prior studies primarily focus on investigating the credibility of peer review process in upholding academic quality (Hug, 2022; Siler et al. 2015). Evaluating the reviewers’ performance is one of research hotspots, which includes the quality of review comments (Perlis et al. 2023), the subjectivity and bias (Tennant and Ross-Hellauer, 2020), specialized training for reviewers (Willis et al. 2023), standardizing the peer review process (Chauvin et al. 2019), attracting public participation in peer review to promote preprints (Rodríguez, 2019), and support systems for peer review system based on intelligent models (Liang et al. 2024).

Regarding our focus, the quantity of reviews, there is an urgent requirement to expand the pool of peer reviewers. Certain studies have emphasized the importance of considering the involvement of reviewers (Jordan, 2020), and there is a growing concern regarding the scarcity crisis of reviewers (DeLisi, 2022). The scarcity of reviewers brings challenges not only to the function and the sustainability of the peer review system, but can also lead to an influx of unprofessional review comments, a decline in the quality and efficiency of the peer review process, increased biases, and other issues (Bianchi and Squazzoni, 2015; Deslandes and Silva, 2013).

Measures to motivate peer reviewers

Reviewing manuscripts is a voluntary activity. It is optional, time-consuming, and has no direct bearing on reviewers’ career advancement (Bianchi et al. 2018). In response to the diminishing participation in peer review processes, academia has consistently explored external incentives as the predominant approach to attract reviewers (Barroga, 2020; Ellwanger and Chies, 2020).

Incentives in the form of material rewards can be used to motivate reviewers (Copiello, 2018; García et al. 2022), but this approach relies heavily on a sustainable economic foundation, especially as the number of submitted manuscripts grows exponentially (Fortunato et al. 2018). Moreover, monetary incentives have not been successful and may even lead to contrary outcomes (Garg et al. 2015; Squazzoni et al. 2013), such as reducing intrinsic motivation and voluntary participation (Fishbach and Woolley, 2022; Gneezy et al. 2011). This dilemma is commonly known as “motivation crowding” (Frey and Jegen, 2001).

Accolade awards are increasingly used to motivate reviewers due to cost-effectiveness (Zaharie and Seeber, 2018). Platforms and journals often set annual accolade awards for reviewers as the primary way to incentivize them. Theoretically, this incentive has the potential to align with reviewers’ prosocial and self-achievement motivation, thereby overcoming the limitations of monetary rewards (Chloros et al. 2022; Gallus and Frey, 2016; Gasparyan et al. 2015). Currently, there is a lack of empirical research on the mechanisms and effectiveness of non-material awards (García et al. 2015; Zaharie and Seeber, 2018). A study designs review invitations to observe the responses, and it is found that the acceptance rate for those invitations with the commitment of public recognition usually increases, and it decreases when recognitions depend on their performance (Zaharie and Seeber, 2018). However, there is currently a scarcity of empirical research investigating the impact of accolade awards for reviewers on their subsequent number of reviews.

Additionally, there are some other methods apart from the incentives. Utilizing intelligent algorithms (Bianchi et al. 2019; Zhao et al. 2018; Zhao and Zhang, 2022) to match the most appropriate reviewers is a potential solution, but it is restricted to matching reviewers only, and it still has problems with cold start issues and limited dataset within merely several journals/conferences or fields (Pradhan et al. 2021). Another approach is optimizing the review invitation strategy, including the design of invitation letters, as well as considerations regarding timing, frequency, selection, and form of the invitation (Breuning et al. 2015; Chetty et al. 2014; Franceschet et al. 2022). However, this method has its limitations and has not proven to enhance the pool of potential reviewers.

Theories on awarding effects

Numerous studies have demonstrated that rewards can boost participants’ motivation (Malek et al. 2020). Nevertheless, a large number of studies also identify adverse effects associated with rewards (Hidi, 2016), with the Self-Determination Theory (SDT) being one of the most renowned theories in this regard. The SDT (Deci and Ryan, 2012) suggests that motivation can be categorized into intrinsic and extrinsic motivation. Extrinsic motivation is driven by external awards, but it may weaken people’s internal motivation and subsequent motivation, known as the “Desi effect”. These conclusions may be related to the representativeness of samples, including individual differences and varying socio-economic factors (Okike et al. 2016; Ortega, 2017; Robinson et al. 2021; Ryan and Deci, 2020). While SDT is partially introduced in this study, its applicability is still too general and limited. A theoretical framework specific to peer review is needed.

Some studies have proposed a motivation framework specifically for peer review. Motivations for participating in peer review can generally be categorized into two categories (Zaharie and Osoian, 2016). One is prosocial, and the other is self-achievement (Tenbrunsel and Northcraft, 2009). The former refers to the fact that reviewers engage in peer review activities as a result of their recognition and responsibility within the academic community or their personal academic obligations (Tajfel, 1981). The latter means that scholars have the opportunity to enhance themselves through review activities, including staying updated with the latest knowledge, gaining social capital, and increasing their academic influence (Boerckel et al. 2021; Knobloch-Westerwick et al. 2013; Pytynia, 2017; Tennant et al. 2017). When analyzing the mechanism of the awarding effects and related aspects, it is necessary to learn from this motivation framework.

Individual differences and socio-economic factors are two aspects that can influence the awarding effect. For instance, employees’ intrinsic motivation and performance are influenced by individual and collective cultures (Yousaf et al. 2022). Confucian culture and official language can influence reviewers’ performance (Zhang et al. 2022). Individual differences in personality traits and prior experiences with awards can influence how extrinsic awards impact intrinsic motivation. For another example, students’ age has been identified as a factor in educational rewards (Visaria et al. 2016). Our study will use a highly representative sample to explore how these certain factors influence the awarding effects in peer review activities.

Data and methodology

Previous empirical evidence on motivation research is primarily derived from small-scale artificial experiments. In artificial experiments, improving sample representativeness is hindered by the high experimental costs. Besides, the artificially constructed experimental environment differs from the natural living environment, making it easy for participants to react with biased behavior. Quasi-experiments, unlike artificial experiments, allow for large-scale inferences and occur naturally, resembling real-world scenarios. To achieve representative causal inferences, the study aims to utilize quasi-experimental methods based on a large sample across all disciplines and the whole world. Therefore, the review data in Publons, is employed. We initially use the 2018 Publons Global Peer Review Award winners as the experimental group. Then, a control group is formed through extensive data collection by PSM. Finally, DiD analysis is conducted based on the panel data.

Data

Data processing

Publons is recognized as the largest global and all-disciplinary peer review platform. When reviewers assess manuscripts for journals partnered with Publons, their review records will be stored on Publons. As early as 2017, the platform claims that it has 3,000,000 users and partnerships with more than 25,000 journals. After its integration into Clarivate in 2017, the platform experienced a greater increase in both user base and partner journals. Nature has also acknowledged and reported the contribution of Publons (Van Noorden, 2014). This study conducts empirical research based on the Publons data.

Publons annually identifies the top 1% of reviewers in their respective fields and awards these individuals with the “Publons Global Peer Review Award”. Due to various reasons, the award has ceased since 2019. We have collected all the winners in 2017, 2018 and 2019. To avoid biases in heterogeneous multi-periods DiD analysis (De Chaisemartin and d’Haultfoeuille, 2020), we mainly focus on peer review winners in 2018, and ensure that the control group excludes winners from 2017 and 2019. Of course, for robustness, we also perform the analysis of the 2019 and 2017 winners, as seen in 4.4 Robustness.

The bibliometric data is acquired from MAG, OpenAlex (Priem et al. 2022), and SciSciNet databases (Lin et al. 2023). These databases include scholars’ identifiers, number of publications, academic age, discipline, number of citations, and gender, which serve as our control variables. Different databases use different ID systems: Publons uses ResearcherIDs; MAG and SciSciNet use MAGIDs; OpenAlex uses OpenAlexIDs. Overall, it is necessary to establish connections between these identifiers, involving ResearcherIDs, MAGIDs, ORCIDs, and OpenAlexIDs.

We begin the merging process with the ORCIDs in OpenAlex database. OpenAlex database itself establishes links with ORCIDs. To filter out those inactive reviewers, the ORCIDs of authors with more than 10 publications in the full OpenAlex database are selected, totaling 2,678,929 authors. In the subsequent steps, there is no sampling process. Then we access the data records in the ORCID database for all the above ORCIDs by crawling scholars' homepage. In the ORCID database, 357,948 authors have linked their ORCIDs with ResearcherIDs; in this way, we have successfully linked OpenAlex (OpenAlexIDs), ORCIDs, and Publons (ResearcherIDs). Lastly, the (annual) review data of scholars is acquired via ResearcherIDs, which are the identifiers of Publons.

The subsequent step is to establish a connection with MAGIDs. We link the MAG with ORCIDs through certain name disambiguation databases. Further elaboration on this matter will be provided in the following section (Details of name disambiguation). To summarize, in this phase, we successfully link MAGIDs with ORCIDs, ResearcherIDs, and OpenAlexIDs. Consequently, our designated database has been effectively interconnected. As a result, a dataset containing 179,737 reviewers is acquired.

In summary, a panel dataset of reviewers from 2012 to 2023 has been established. The dataset includes both review data and bibliometric data. Data collection is conducted from August 10 to August 27, 2023, using the API provided by Publons. The 2018 Global Peer Review Award is the beginning of the treatment. Therefore, the pre-treatment period of the experimental group ranges from 2012 to 2017 (a total of six years), and the period of after-treatment spans from 2019 to 2023 (a total of five years).

Details of name disambiguation

Ambiguity in names has the potential to introduce bias in the inference. For instance, a common occurrence is when two authors sharing the same name are mistakenly consolidated under a single ID within a database (Zhang et al. 2023).

In the phase of name disambiguation, we address two primary issues. Following the data acquisition process, we have incorporated MAGIDs (for acquiring bibliometric data, control variables, etc.) and ResearcherIDs (for obtaining review data in Publons). Nevertheless, both ResearcherIDs and MAGIDs encounter the issues of name ambiguity.

Firstly, the name ambiguity of ResearcherIDs, is taken into account during the initial stages of data collection. Our first step in data collection is to extract ResearcherIDs from the ORCID database, which are self-registered by the authors, thus reducing the likelihood of ambiguity to some extent. Any ResearcherID not explicitly registered in the ORCID database is excluded from our analysis.

Secondly, the name ambiguity of MAGIDs is also a problem, as there is no MAGID record in the ORCID database. To tackle the name ambiguity of MAGID, we leverage three golden datasets: OpenAIRE (Manghi et al. 2019), EMAKG (Pollacci, 2022), and LAGOS‐AND (Zhang et al. 2023).

LAGOS-AND is the largest name disambiguation database and serves as the primary dataset for our research in the name disambiguation process. It conducts extensive name disambiguation using DOIs and ORCIDs. This process entails cross-referencing the records of DOIs in the ORCID database with those in the MAG database, leading to data blocks that align accurately with ORCIDs and MAGIDs. Since DOIs can ensure uniqueness and precision, the data matched via DOIs are deemed reliable. This disambiguation methodology allows for large-scale implementation, effectively meeting our research requirements. We exclusively use these disambiguated data blocks for data integration and aggregation, thereby fulfilling the objective of name disambiguation.

OpenAIRE and EMAKG are supplementary databases in our study. After disambiguating the data in the LAGOS-AND database, any ORCID that remains ambiguous undergoes further disambiguation using these two databases. OpenAIRE employs a methodology akin to LAGOS-AND for name disambiguation. During the validation of their name disambiguation algorithm, EMAKG complies with a mapping of ORCIDs and MAGIDs after name disambiguation, and we also utilize this mapping to establish connections between ORCIDs and MAGIDs.

Inferring method

The difference-in-differences (DiD) method, widely acknowledged as a useful causal inference strategy for panel data, is adopted in our study, using the fect package in the R language (Liu et al. 2022).

The DiD is based on two fundamental assumptions. Firstly, the “Linear Assumption”, that the treatment effect changes at a constant rate throughout the study period. Secondly, the “Parallel Trend Assumption”, that both the control group and the experimental group exhibit similar trends before treatment. The linear assumption is generally met, but the assumption of parallel trends presents a main challenge for the DiD method (Liu et al. 2021).

Propensity score matching (PSM) is used to create a control group closely resembling the experimental group with a similar pre-treatment change pattern. In this study, the single-period matching method is adopted, which typically focuses on the first period (2012) as the matching period. The matching process is conducted with scholars’ academic age, gender, discipline, cumulative number of publications, and cumulative citations as matched variables, employing the MatchIt package. In summary, before conducting the DiD analysis, the PSM method is used to identify a suitable control group, utilizing the MatchIt package in the R language (Stuart et al. 2011).

Variables

The dependent variable in this study is the annual number of reviews conducted by reviewers. The independent variable pertains to the \({DiD}\), whether the individual is an accolade award winner and the year is over 2018 (after the 2018 Publons Global Peer Review Award). Prior to 2018 (specifically, from 2012 to 2017), the winners are considered as before treatment. The accolade award winners from 2018 onwards (2018–2023) will be regarded as having been treated. In the control group that does not receive the award, the time spanning from 2012 to 2023 is all considered as the untreated period.

Although the counterfactual estimation framework has made great efforts to address the endogeneity caused by temporal confounding variables, it remains necessary to control for as many of these variables as possible to derive more precise and reliable conclusions.

Some literature suggests that editors show a preference for reviewers who have recent publications or significant academic influence in their respective fields (Glonti et al. 2019). The number of publications in the previous year and cumulative citations will be controlled in this study. Scholars with more academic power may prioritize personal endeavors, thereby influencing their review activities (Handgraaf et al. 2008; Zaharie and Osoian, 2016). This will also be controlled by considering the intersection of a scholar’s academic age and cumulative citations, as well as the intersection of academic age and the number of publications. To some degree, this also helps to control for potential confounding factors, such as reviewers’ job titles and administrative responsibilities.

The academic age of scholars may also influence their motivation to review manuscripts. Therefore, the academic age of scholars will be controlled. The academic age of a scholar is determined according to the year of their first publication.

Furthermore, the interdisciplinary nature of scholars may influence their engagement in review activities, which can be quantified by the number of second-level disciplines of scholarly outputs. The quantity of disciplines is determined by the OpenAlex database using proven machine learning algorithms (Priem et al. 2022).

The scholar’s social capital also needs to be taken into consideration and controlled. In practice, a scholar’s social capital is often proxied by the number of their collaborators, which is determined based on the number of authors in publications.

Results

PSM result

In this study, the earliest period (2012) is selected for matching. Reviewers’ career age (\({Ag}{e}_{t}\)), first-level discipline, their seniority (\({Ag}{e}_{t}\times C{P}_{t}\) and \({Ag}{e}_{t}\times C{C}_{t}\)), gender (\({Gender}\)), and basic bibliometrics indicators (\({P}_{t}\) and \(C{C}_{t}\)) in 2012 are taken as matching variables. Following the suggestion (Ho et al. 2007), the non-parametric nearest distance method is employed in this study using the MatchIt.

After the matching, a balance test is necessary to verify the matching effect. The standardized mean difference (SMD) represents the difference in means of each covariate between groups. We assess the matching effect by comparing the SMD values before and after matching. As shown in Fig. 1, after matching, all SMD values are below 0.1, with only a few variables having SMD values exceeding 0.05. The matching effect is satisfactory.

After matching, the dynamic trajectories of the number of reviews for the experimental and control groups are displayed in Fig. 2. It is obvious that the changing trends of the two groups before treatment are essentially similar. However, after treatment, a clear difference in the changing pattern is observed between the two groups. The experimental group’s rate of change is slower than that of the control group after the treatment.

PSM-DiD results

Estimations

Based on the fect package, the DiD inference (the interactive fixed effects in fect) is conducted, and the brief results are presented in Table 2. When control variables and fixed effects (the interactive fixed effects in fect include the two-way fixed effects) are included, the coefficient for \({DiD}\) is −4.06, indicating a decline in reviewer enthusiasm after receiving the 2018 Publons Global Peer Review Award.

Furthermore, we plot the average treatment effects on treated (ATTs), as shown in Fig. 3. It is evident that the average ATTs are around −4 after treatment, and the trend demonstrates a V-shaped pattern. After undergoing a consecutive decline for four years, this negative trend will slow down to some extent.

Parallel trend and placebo tests

The F test is commonly used for pre-trend testing. A higher p-value from the F-test suggests a better fit of the parallel trend. In the fect package, equivalence tests known as TOST tests are also included. The equivalence tests offer an additional advantage compared to the F test. In cases with a large sample size, even a minor confounding factor or a few outliers can significantly affect the accuracy of F test, potentially leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis. Equivalence tests serve as a solution to circumvent this issue. A general guideline is that if the minimum range is within the equivalence range, the test is considered passed. The TOST checks whether the confidence intervals for the estimated ATTs in pretreatment periods exceed the equivalence range. A smaller p-value for the TOST indicates a better fit to the pre-trend. The results are displayed in Fig. 4, showing statistical significance in passing both the F test and the TOST test at a significance level of 5% (F > 0.05, T < 0.05).

We fictionalize a treatment time before the 2018, one and two years before the actual award year. If DiD results of the estimator remain significant (the estimated ATTs are significantly different from zero) with fictitious treatment time, the original estimation result may be biased. The placebo test also involves the p-value of the F-test and TOST. Similar to pre-trend testing, the larger the p-value of the F test, and the smaller the p-value of the TOST, the better. The test results are displayed in Fig. 5. The fictitious treatment and its next year are highlighted in red, and they do not generate award effects. The p-value of the F-test in our analysis is significantly greater than 0.05, and the TOST is 0, passing the placebo test.

Additional analysis

Individual differences and awarding effects

Individual differences can influence awarding effects, as noted in Theories and Backgrounds. Understanding these factors can improve our knowledge of how awards impact reviewers comprehensively and provide strategic recommendations for incentivizing reviewers. By analyzing the existing literature on peer review motivation, this study examines six variables: academic age, seniority, funding, social capital, interdisciplinarity, and gender of reviewers, to understand their influence on the awarding effect. Moderating effects are mainly employed. For the analysis of gender effects, subgroup modeling is employed, and Wald test is employed to assess the presence of a statistically significant variance in the DiD coefficients.

The findings on how individual differences affect the awarding effect are presented in Table 3. It shows that academic age, seniority, social capital, and gender significantly influence the awarding effect. The remaining variables, such as funding and interdisciplinarity, are not statistically significant. For reviewers with higher seniority and social capital, winning the accolade awards makes a more negative impact. There is a decrease in motivation among male reviewers after receiving accolades, while for women, this almost has no impact.

Socio-economic factors and awarding effects

As mentioned in Theories and Backgrounds, Relevant indicators, including official language (English usage), Confucian culture, pro-active tendencies (measured by the sum of the reviews of a country), income level, and scientific research performance (measured by the H-index), are collected. Scientific research performance is measured by the average H-index of countries per year spanning from 2012 to 2023.

Subgroup analyses are conducted to explore the impact of these factors on the awarding effects based on their coefficients of the \({DiD}\) estimator. In Table 4, the Wald test is employed to assess the presence of a statistically significant difference in the \({DiD}\) coefficients across the two models. As shown in Table 4, English-speaking countries, high levels of income, and advanced national scientific research can mitigate the negative effects of accolades. All other factors are not significant.

Robustness

Winners in 2017 or 2019 as research samples

To demonstrate the robustness of our findings, robustness tests are carried out using data that select the winners in 2017 and 2019 as the samples. Figure 6 shows that, the findings of the PSM-DiD analysis follow a similar pattern across the years 2017, 2018, and 2019.

Discussions on missing data beyond Publons

There is indeed a problem where certain journal reviews are not linked to Publons, and as of now, there is no technology capable of capturing all review records. However, we posit that this issue is unlikely to significantly affect the empirical findings. According to an article in Nature Computational Science (Liu et al. 2021), if the Y2 - Y1 of experimental group is affected by confounder and the control group is not, the inference result is biased (Y1 and Y2 are the average Y before and after treatment; in our study, Y refers to the number of reviews). The main focus of this discussion is whether the data coverage by Publons (C) (confounder) only affects Y2 - Y1 in the experimental group without influencing the control group.

Figure 7 illustrates two hypotheses concerning the coverage of Publons data over time in both the experimental and control groups. In the context of Fig. 7A, C2 - C1 is equal between the experimental and control groups. In this case, the missing review data will not influence the empirical outcomes. Conversely, if a situation akin to Fig. 7B, it has a biased impact on the empirical data. We argue that Fig. 7A aligns with the real world, and our findings are unbiased. In the experimental group, the data coverage of Publons dose not exhibit a sudden change in slope post-treatment, as partner journals of Publons will not be affected by this award obviously. We also argue that the slopes of the experimental group and the control group exhibit a high degree of similarity, as shown in Fig. 7A. This similarity can be attributed to the control of scholars’ disciplines to some extent, along with other relevant control variables. After the PSM, the experimental and control groups are drawn from similar disciplinary backgrounds, thereby regulating the range of academic journals they engage with, and resulting in an analogous journal coverage of Publons.

Simply stated, the review data beyond Publons do not affect our empirical results. Intuitively speaking, this is because the missing data affects both the experimental and control groups in a similar manner, and our conclusions are drawn by comparing the differences between these groups.

General Discussions

Mechanisms of awarding effects

Marginal utility of awards and voluntary nature of peer review

After receiving a peer review accolade award, reviewers’ subsequent motivation decreases. This seems counterintuitive. It is necessary to study the inner mechanisms. Reviewers’ motivations can be classified into self-achievement and group focus within the academic community (Zaharie and Osoian, 2016). The former emphasizes the academic influence that peer review activities bring to reviewers, while the latter highlights the prosocial qualities of reviewers, such as recognition and responsibility in academia.

In the context of self-achievement motivation and prosocial framework, the diminishing marginal utility of academic influence and mental satisfaction from peer review accolades is particularly pronounced. For reviewers, the second peer review accolade award is unlikely to provide as many benefits (academic influence and psychological satisfaction) as the first award, so reviewers may not seek a second award and will not continue to work as hard as before. Hence, reviewers who are motivated by self-achievement or prosocial factors all may experience a natural decline in motivation for reviewing after receiving an accolade award.

Furthermore, in the context of self-achievement framework, the decrease in marginal effect, to some extent, is exacerbated by the voluntary nature of peer review. Review activities are voluntary in nature and do not offer any tangible reward for reviewers (Besançon et al. 2020; Northcraft and Tenbrunsel, 2011). As a result, for those reviewers motivated by self-achievement, the second honor reward is a very low cost/benefit option and probably it is more beneficial to turn to other academic activities, so the awards’ appeal will be further diminished.

Unexpected nature of the peer review awards

In recent years, awards have been categorized into expected and unexpected. Most previous studies focused on the effects of expected incentives, yet unexpected awards may have distinct effects (Hoogveld and Zubanov, 2017; Neckermann and Yang, 2017).

Peer review accolade awards are typically unexpected, as the participants are not informed about the award, and winners do not anticipate winning. Most reviewers are typically scholars, and while publishing is a privilege for scholars, peer review is also seen as an obligation (Nash, 2023). The winners are unaware that they have surpassed social norms before receiving the award (Neckermann and Yang, 2017), which will worsen the negative impact (Robinson et al. 2021). After receiving a peer review accolade award, individuals may believe that their review performance exceeds academic norms (Gallus and Frey, 2017; Rogers et al. 2018), and it may lead to peer social sanctions (Bradler et al. 2016), which can result in a decline in the number of reviews they conduct (Mullen and Monin, 2016).

Mechanisms and additional analysis

These mechanisms are supported by various findings in the Additional Analysis. First of all, male reviewers are often seen as more driven by self-achievement motivations, especially in terms of academic influence which benefits career advancement (Meece et al. 2009). Our results may suggest that peer review accolade awards have a stronger effect on reducing the motivation of male reviewers. Secondly, reviewers with higher seniority, more academic ages, and more social capital experience a higher drop in motivation after accolades. This phenomenon may be due to the fact that reviewers with more seniority and social capital have likely already achieved a higher level of academic influence, resulting in a heavier diminishing marginal effect. Finally, in countries with less developed economies, non-English speaking countries, and nations with weaker research strength, the negative effects are more significant. This phenomenon can be explained at two different levels. (1) Conducting reviews is more expensive. In these countries, scholars may face more economic and language limitations, leading them to prioritize scientific or teaching activities with higher direct benefits, potentially neglecting the review activities. (2) The accolade award in this study is an international honor. Individuals are more likely to be surprised and perceive themselves as surpassing international standards after receiving accolades, leading to a higher reduction in their motivation.

Policy implications

Overall, based on our findings, the academic community needs to rethink the peer review accolade awards and their designs. While Publons does not provide a specific reason for discontinuing the award after 2019, our empirical findings partially align with their decision. If accolade awards are canceled, attracting reviewers may have to rely on alternative avenues.

Enhancing the marginal utility of peer review is crucial. We can stimulate the motivation for reviews by de-volunteering the review activities and increasing their marginal revenue. Enhancing the interdependence between peer review and scientific research represents a viable approach. In this particular scenario, the incentives that drive scientific research can be extended to peer review activities, thereby enhancing the range of incentives available to reviewers. For instance, it is possible to incorporate pertinent review indicators into the scientific evaluation. In fact, such efforts are already under exploration in some studies (Ortega, 2017). Alternatively, we emphasize the importance of enabling peer review activities to enhance the academic careers of reviewers. Strengthening the feedback to reviewers for publishing is an effective strategy. Besides providing them with the most up-to-date knowledge advancements and facilitating their access to scientific data, journals and publishers can offer conveniences for those outstanding reviewers in manuscript submissions. For instance, they can expedite the submission process for these reviewers. For exceptional reviewers from developing countries, one potential strategy is to reduce article processing charges for their subsequent scientific publications.

Though our study reveals a negative impact, a well-designed award may be able to motivate reviewers. Based on our discussion of the underlying mechanism, the primary focus is on reducing the unexpected nature and minimizing the diminishing marginal utility of the awards. To increase the expected nature of the accolade awards, it is recommended to boost publicity efforts for greater visibility of the accolade, and the transparent election rules, guidelines, and processes of the awards are all essential. In order to improve the marginal utility of awards, it is crucial to create various peer review accolades to compensate reviewers for their diverse contributions in the peer review process. In addition to awards for reviewers who actively participate in peer review, the following awards can be established: the Interdisciplinary Review Award for those who provide reviews for papers across disciplines, the Innovation Award for those who bring fresh ideas and innovative perspectives to manuscripts, and the Emerging Reviewer Award for new and promising reviewers. In light of our findings, it may be more effective to focus awards on young reviewers, male reviewers, as well as reviewers from developing countries, non-English speaking countries, and countries with weaker research strength. Lastly, implementing a long-term incentive program to motivate reviewers to sustain their engagement and contributions is also a viable strategy for enhancing the awards’ marginal utility.

Additionally, it is necessary to prioritize cultural construction in academia, to get rid of incentives as much as possible. The recent announcement of the REF 2028 (UK Research Excellence Framework) (Callard, 2023) reform signifies a transition from “incentives for scientific research performance” to “cultivating a scientific research culture”. Promoting the significance of participating in review activities, strengthening the protection of reviewers’ rights and interests, and other areas are all pivotal in the future.

Contributions

Our main contributions are as follows:

(1) Upon a representative investigation, we find that peer review accolade awards can reduce the subsequent number of reviews by reviewers. We analyze the relevant factors, explain the mechanism, and propose strategies. Previously, despite the widespread use of peer review accolade awards, there is limited prior empirical research on how they impact reviewers’ subsequent motivation, let alone the mechanism.

(2) In contrast to previous incentive research on a small scale, our study uses a well-represented sample and a quasi-experimental approach. Small-scale experiments usually ignore individual differences, economic factors, and social contexts. Our research has examined various variables related to individual differences and socio-economic factors.

(3) There is a lack of research on incentives and motivation in the field of science of science, specifically in the field of peer review studies. We offer a representative study in this field, providing an instance and insights into the improvement of peer review systems and the science of science studies.

Limitations and future work

As highlighted, while the Publons awards and our study focus on the number of reviews, review quality is overlooked. Exploring the subsequent influence of awards on review quality is an intriguing question. We have reviewed various methods for assessing the quality of reviews (Sizo et al. 2019). (1) The most classical approach involves manually assessing the review report, which is costly and impractical on a large scale. (2) Many studies are working on evaluating the review text automatically. They utilize text length, specific textual vocabulary, entities, and artificial intelligence systems as metrics to gauge review quality (Ausloos et al. 2016; Falkenberg and Soranno, 2018; Zhang et al. 2022). However, there are limitations, such as the weak causal relationships, potential biases, and prediction accuracy (Manzoor and Shah, 2021; Sizo et al. 2019; Zhang et al. 2022). Moreover, few reviewers and academic journals disclose review data. While there are open peer review platforms like OpenReview, it is not feasible to establish a connection between the text and the respective reviewers, as most reviewers are anonymous, even if we have access to these review texts. Moving forward, it is aspiring to overcome the above difficulties in quantifying the quality of reviews and conduct more comprehensive studies on the awarding impact on review quality.

Besides, the DiD method is used to analyze the main effect and effectively capture the impact of awards on the subsequent number of reviews. However, some additional analysis solely employs the comparative method of subgroup modeling, which is limited to discussions at the correlational level rather than the causal level.

Conclusions

The research questions outlined in the Introduction are addressed. The answers to these questions and the main research findings can be summarized as follows:

  1. (1)

    Peer review accolades can lead to a negative awarding effect. After receiving the 2018 Publons Global Peer Review Award, there is typically an average reduction of about four reviews in Publons.

  2. (2)

    The awarding effect is influenced by individual and socio-economic factors. Scholars with higher academic seniority, more social capital, higher academic age, male scholars, and scholars from lower-income countries, non-English-speaking countries, or less advanced scientific research countries are likely to reduce a greater number of reviews after receiving the award.

  3. (3)

    Certain mechanisms are proposed to explain the negative awarding effect. The negative outcomes may stem from the diminishing marginal utility of academic influence, the voluntary nature of peer review, and the unexpected properties of accolade awards. The academic community should reassess the existing incentive strategies on reviewers, and according to these proposed mechanisms, we put forward some potential strategies for advancement.
