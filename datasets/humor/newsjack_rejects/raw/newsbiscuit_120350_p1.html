    Hi Crayon, I was googling Newsjack this evening and came across this post. I appreciate your pain (I have one sketch I submitted this week that I have very high hopes for) and the wait is definitely painful.

    Hope you don't mind but I have a few bits of feedback based on my experience (not to show off but 11 credits, 5 of which are sketches and have managed to get 3 sketches in the script for one episode but that was when I earned a place as one of their weekly commissioned writers), so I feel I have a half-decent sense for what they'll go for (even if I can't consistantly meet the stand each week).

    1. Angela intro - there's no joke in the intro. There's nice idiomatic play on words on the whole driving theme but no actual setup and punchline. You could have gone with the use of the word steam-rolled as a launch point - joke about Labour being like a road runner cartoon for example.

    2. The premise of the sketch seems a bit generic. It feels like you've gone for a premise around the term Momentum (car based framing device), which as I said feels a bit generic but more importantly isn't germane to the story - which is about infighting. Again first theme that jumps to mind is the cartoonish term steam-rolling and the current internal struggle in the Labour party, those two things go together. But there are plenty of directions you can take that story, premise-wise.

    3. The dialogue feels very right-wing attacking left-wing. Now though BBC comedy has a historically left wing bent (to say the least at times), the BBC is crying out for right-wing informed comedy, and they will use it (look at the Mash report). So if you can do right-wing informed sketches you have a much higher chance of getting on than left-wing informed sketches (because they get far more of those). Attcking an ideology isn't funny, so just going on the attack against the Conservatives isn't funny. What is (IMHO) is ironically exposing the inherent hypocrisy or ridiculousness of something they've done. To summarise my point, don't attack who they are, attack something they've done, and be specific, comedy is in the specifics.

    4. More punchlines, a gag per line of dialogue if possible.

    5. An outro joke, doesn't have to be a big laugh, just a twist of the narrative or a call-back to an earlier joke, but there needs to be an outro joke.

    6. Show don't tell - don't just rely on exposition, sound effects don't just set the scene, they can progress the narrative.

    Anyway that's just my opinion, and I've found relying on these rules really helpful in getting credits.

    Hope that helps.

    I don't know much about the Haringey story but if it helps I've noticed Newsjack don't seem to do 'roving reporter' or interview-based sketches like other shows. It might be a good thing as it forces you to come up with more original scenarios and characters and prepare you for other radio writing. You would also then usually have to set up the situation and characters at the start of the sketch. Usually there should be away of adapting the idea into a mini-drama, such as, in the case of the above, using a newly promoted officer or some such instead of an interviewer.

    I don't know if a funny presenter's introduction is absolutely required as the presenters write their own material at the start of the show so may well adapt the introductions to their own styles anyway; if in doubt just make sure it introduces the subject of the sketch clear.

    Having been semi-successful once (they picked a sketch though it didn't make it into the edit) I managed to get my hands on the notes from a writers' meeting, you can view it here.
