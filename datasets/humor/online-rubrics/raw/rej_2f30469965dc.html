When South Park premiered its landmark 200th and 201st episodes on April 14, 2010, and April 21, 2010, Matt Stone and Trey Parker found themselves backed into a corner with conversations about free speech and censorship. It began when Comedy Central aired episodes 200 and 210, the two-parter called Super Best Friends, to celebrate the 200th installment of the show. However, problems began when the Prophet Muhammad was depicted in these episodes in a way that was deemed inflammatory and blasphemous.

Several Muslim advocacy groups objected to this, and death threats followed. Due to this, Comedy Central pulled both of the South Park episodes from streaming services, home video box sets, and reruns later. Essentially, it was banished from the official channels. This decision sent shockwaves and ignited debates over religious respect, free speech, and what were the limits of satire.

Fans and advocates of civil liberties allegedly deemed the network as cowards; however, supporters argued that it was a necessary step to ensure the protection of the creators. Today, the two episodes only survive in private archives and underground torrents.

Diving Deep Into South Park’s Episodes 200 and 201 Controversy

Episodes 200 and 201 of South Park season 14 were monumental to the show’s legacy, as they featured a meta-plot showing Tom Cruise and other celebs mocked by the people of South Park coming together to file a class action lawsuit. However, Cruise promises to take back the lawsuit if the residents of South Park can get the Prophet Muhammad to meet him.

What upset people was the imagery of the Prophet Muhammad in its entirety, since it is forbidden in several Islamic traditions. CBS News reported in 2010 that the group, RevolutionMuslim.com, had warned Trey Parker and Matt Stone about the possible violent repercussions by posting a picture of a Dutch filmmaker, Theo van Gogh, who was stabbed and shot to death by a fanatic in 2004.
