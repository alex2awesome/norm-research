All Apologies

Dan Harmon's Co-Written Apology with/to Community Writer Megan Ganz

Excellent public apologies are rare, but they do exist. In this week's submission, Harmon's willingness to improve and the hard work of an unexpected editor makes for an A+ apology.

Nicole
Nov 03, 2023

I first encountered Dan Harmon’s now-famous apology through Nancy Updike’s reporting on “Get a Spine!” — a This American Life episode that aired in 2019. The snippets of apology featured in that episode definitely left a mark. I was impressed. I sought out the apology on Dan Harmon’s podcast so I could listen to it in its entirety. I shared it with my partner so I could listen for a second time and hear someone else’s thoughts about it. I discussed it with friends. Way before I ever thought about blogging about the art of public apology, Harmon’s public statement got me thinking about what a heartfelt apology sounded (and felt) like.

As I did more research, I discovered that part of the reason Harmon’s apology is so good is because he had a zealous editor — Megan Ganz, the person his apology was meant for. And though he often gets much of the (undoubtedly deserved) credit for his statements, there has been little coverage on the various iterations of apology Harmon attempted before getting it right. In today’s blog post, we examine Harmon’s apology as a multi-stage, collaborative composition.

The Elements of the Apology’s Circumstance

Who: Dan Harmon (TV writer and producer, best known as the creator of Community and co-creator of Rick and Morty)

What: In simple terms, Harmon’s apology accounts for years of sexual harassment and bullying targeted at Megan Ganz, a junior writer on Community and Harmon’s employee. The full apology provides more context about their relationship and his actions. But in short, Harmon was attracted to Ganz and acted on that attraction by being (in his words) “flirty, creepy, everything other than overt enough to constitute betraying your live-in girlfriend to whom you're going home every night.” When Ganz did not reciprocate his attraction, he retaliated by (his words again) treating her “cruelly, pointedly [and doing] things that I would never, ever, ever have done if she had been male and if I had never had those feelings for her.”

When: The harassment and bullying happened around the same time Harmon worked as a showrunner for Community, so sometime between 2009 and 2012. The public apology was submitted in first draft form on October 16, 2017, and the second draft appeared on January 10, 2018.

Where: Harmon’s drafted apologies appeared on Twitter and his podcast, Harmontown. The final draft of his apology appeared on the “Don’t Let Him Wipe or Flush” episode. The ten-ish-minute monologue begins at 18:16. I highly recommend listening to it in its entirety. You can also read the transcript here.

Why: I think it’s fair to say Harmon held a lot of guilt about what he did to Ganz. Based on his confession on the “Porcelain Birds” episode of his podcast and his New Year’s Tweet (which I’ll discuss below), the #MeToo movement likely stirred up memories of and shame about his role in that relationship. Like any feeling human, he probably had a strong desire to be forgiven for his misdeeds. But it is also evident that his apology wasn’t a means to forgiveness ends. He also endeavored to validate Ganz’s experience, regardless of how poorly it reflected upon him. Fundamentally, I believe Harmon apologized because he knew it was the right thing to do.

Like Harmon’s first and second draft apologies, this post is public. Unlike Harmon’s podcast, my platform is teensy tiny. Feel free to broaden it by sharing this post far and wide!

Rubric Review

For those who are new here (or who understandably might have forgotten the rubric), here are the necessary components of an authentic apology. The apologizer should:

  • Demonstrate that the accusation or criticism has been taken seriously.

  • Include a statement of accountability (something akin to “I’m sorry.”)

  • Make a commitment to personal growth and/or repairing the harm caused.

An excellent apology (A+ to A-) will show evidence of all three components. Less stellar apologies (B+ to C-) may include one or two. An apology fails (D+ to D-) if it does not include any of these components or undermines any of these components at any point in the apology. A student who does not complete the assignment (i.e., doesn’t offer an apology when one is warranted) receives an F.

The First Draft: Using Cluelessness as Cover

Like Donald Trump’s Access Hollywood apologia, Dan Harmon went through some rough drafts of his apology. The first came shortly after Harmon got fired from Community (not because of sexually harassing Ganz, but because of his “erratic behavior”). The apology was sent as a private text message to Ganz. And while it doesn’t count as a public apology, Ganz did discuss the text message briefly with Updike for her “Get a Spine!” reporting. Ganz’s assessment of the apology was that it was inadequate. Updike reported, “Dan texted Megan saying basically, sorry for being a bad boss — very general, short. Megan felt nothing.”

Podcast Apology or Public Confession?

It wasn’t until the #MeToo Movement began in 2017 that Harmon decided to redraft the apology. Or, I suppose it’s not quite accurate to call Harmon’s statements an apology. As I’ll explain, they function more as a confession and, simultaneously, a self-defense.

On October 16, 2017, on the “Porcelain Birds” episode of Harmontown, Harmon and two guests discuss the snowballing accusations of sexual harassment and assault against Harvey Weinstein and others in the entertainment industry. The conversation is full of digressions, anecdotes, and armchair philosophizing about formal and informal sex education in the U.S. Harmon, for example, laments his belief that there is “no sexual culture in modern society” and asserts that U.S. culture is “in a mire of non-education about how to interact with each other” (he means sexually). The prevailing idea of the first 30 minutes of the episode seems to be that men aren’t properly educated, coached, or outright told how to express romantic and sexual feelings toward women.1

In the middle of Harmon’s critique of the state of sex education in the U.S., he somewhat surprisingly admits, “I have abused my power dynamic at work,” adding, “I didn't know I was doing it when I did it.” He doesn’t go into specifics about how he abused his power dynamic, but he does acknowledge that he had a crush on one of his employees, and he recognizes that the context of the workplace imposes different standards of behavior when dealing with romantic feelings. He admits to realizing (after the fact), “If you have a crush on someone that works for you, it’s not the same as having a crush on someone at a library or a bar that doesn’t work for you.”

Deferred Realizations and Diminished Accountability

Harmon talks about how this realization came much later in his life when he reflected on his position and relationship with the as-yet-unnamed employee. He concludes his brief confession by admitting, “I got that into my head [the realization that work crushes are different than non-work crushes] by doing it wrong. But I did it wrong, and I’m really ashamed of it when I look back on it.” In other words, he only realized the wrongness of what he was doing well after he did the wrong thing. He is, essentially, crouching behind a cover of ignorance. Though he admits feeling shame about his behavior, he doesn’t own it. It almost seems like he’s saying he had to commit the sin to understand it’s sinfulness.

Harmon clearly feels guilty about his as-yet-unspecified work behavior. And while the “Porcelain Birds” episode gestures toward an apology, it does not fully commit to it. Instead, this draft of an apology appears to be more like a confession cocooned in multiple layers of ignorance as self-defense.

Evaluation: The Effort Is There, but the Assignment Is Incomplete

Framed in terms of the apology rubric, Harmon gets points for presenting a near-apology without being prompted by an accusation. Ganz did not confront Harmon until months later, but Harmon’s confession in the “Porcelain Birds” episode demonstrates his willingness to reexamine and account for past behavior. It is also possible to glean statements of accountability within his confession, but they are severely diminished by circumscribing assertions of ignorance. Finally, Harmon demonstrates some personal growth by acknowledging his behavior was wrong, but the implication that he will do better in the future or repair any harm done to the unnamed employee is, if anything, only implicit.

Grade: C

The Second Draft: Once More with Editing

Let me just get this out there: while it’s far from stellar, I find Harmon’s first draft apology to be extremely rational. For a lot of people, receiving criticism is akin to being under attack. In my own experience, my heart rate quickens, my jaw and neck muscles clench, and my thoughts race as the powerful lump of grey matter in my skull tries desperately to figure out how to save my precious, fragile ego. It seems to me that’s basic physiology; the doctor tapped my knee, and I kicked him.

What’s impressive about Harmon’s apology is that he let down his defenses and took his vulnerability to the next level. He hatched a rare and beautiful apology by breaking his cocoon of cluelessness. And he did it with some assistance from the last person expected to provide it — his victim, Megan Ganz.

When Calling Out Becomes Collaboration

At the very end of 2017, two and half months after the “Porcelain Birds” episode aired and the #metoo hashtag spread like wildfire across the digital and cultural landscape, Harmon tweeted: “This was truly the year of the asshole, myself included. We don't have to make 2018 the year of the mensch, but I hope it can be the year of the not as much of an asshole. #realisticgoals.” In response, Megan Ganz — the unnamed woman he admitted to having a work crush on — asked him to be more specific about his own “asshole” behavior. “Redemption follows allocution,” she replied.

For several days into the new year, Harmon and Ganz engaged in a very public back-and-forth over Twitter about Harmon’s behavior and its effects on Ganz’s self-confidence and career. One of the most significant parts of the conversation occurred when Harmon tried to apologize, again. He tweeted at Ganz, “I'm filled with regret and a lot of foggy memories about abusing my position, treating you like garbage. I would feel a lot of relief if you told me there was a way to fix it. I'll let you call the shots. Till then, at least I know I was an awful boss and a selfish baby.”

Ganz didn’t tell Harmon exactly how to fix it, but she did provide some insight into how his behavior impacted her well after they had both left Community. She responded, “I wish my memories were foggier. I wish there was a way to fix it. It took me years to believe in my talents again, to trust a boss when he complimented me and not cringe when he asked for my number. I was afraid to be enthusiastic, knowing it might be turned against me later. You want relief? So do I. Figure out how to give me that relief and I'll return the favor.”

Ganz also told The New York Times in an interview that she had listened to Harmon’s “Porcelain Birds” episode and given him feedback about it. She pointed out that Harmon seemed to misunderstand what she meant when she said he harassed her. “I sent him an email to clarify that the harassment was everything that happened after I said no,” she said, adding, “Then I detailed those events, and explained how they made me feel powerless and traumatized. And I told him that if he was serious about wanting to do better, he needed to face the reality of what he had done.” Ganz went beyond providing Harmon tweet-sized feedback on his vague admission of being an “asshole” on Twitter; she provided him lengthy notes on the first draft of his apology.

All the Accountability

One of the most profound aspects of Harmon’s second draft apology is the extent to which he owns up to his behavior. The second draft includes epic levels of ownership that likely would have been impossible without Ganz’s input and Harmon’s willingness to take the story of her experience seriously.

In the second draft, the “I didn’t knows” of the first draft apology are replaced by several statements of accountability. For example, early on in the apology, Harmon admits:

The most clinical way I can put it in ’fessing up to my crimes is that I was attracted to a writer that I had power over because I was a showrunner, and I knew enough to know that these feelings were bad news. That was easy enough to know. I knew that they ran the risk of undercutting people’s faith in my judgment, her faith in her talent, the other writers’ respect for me, the entire production, the audience. I knew that I wasn’t doing anybody any favors by feeling these things, and so I did the cowardly, easiest, laziest thing you could do with feelings like that, and I didn’t deal with them. And in not dealing with them, I made everybody else deal with them. Especially her.

I’ve added the italicized emphasis to show how often Harmon talks about being aware of the inappropriateness of his behavior. He’s no longer trying to hide behind a cover of ignorance. Instead, he admits to being aware of his feelings but refusing to deal with them in a mature way.

Harmon doesn’t end his accountability tour there. He not only owns up to his impulsive and destructive behavior, but he also takes full ownership of the fallout caused by it: “I lied to myself the entire time about it, and I lost my job. I ruined my show. I betrayed the audience. I destroyed everything, and I damaged her internal compass.” The amount of active “I” statements in this 10-second clip is incredible. Harmon lists the cascading consequences caused by his inability to deal with his feelings, and he accepts responsibility for all of them.

The Difference between Not Knowing and Not Thinking

Toward the end of his monologue, Harmon explains how he thinks he got away with his misbehavior: he didn’t think about it. And on a certain level, this might be construed as just another version of the cluelessness defense, but it’s actually quite different. Here’s what he said:

In addition to obviously being sorry, but that’s really not the important thing, I wanna say I did it by not thinking about it, and I got away with it by not thinking about it.

Harmon’s “not thinking about it” here is not the same as the “I didn’t know I was doing it.” Knowing what you are doing and thinking about what you are doing are very different. The former is simply a matter of being a conscious brain in a living body. The latter requires a higher level of cognition: reflecting on actions as choices and giving some serious thought to whether there are better choices available to pick from. It’s the next step toward galaxy brain.

What Harmon is saying here is that he acted on his impulses. He didn’t bother to do the adult, professional, appropriate thing, i.e. consider how his feelings and related behaviors might affect those around him and choose better. This is what he means when he says, “I did the cowardly, easiest, laziest thing you could do with feelings like that, and I didn’t deal with them.”

Harmon’s Call to Action

Harmon’s apology itself helped to repair the harm he inflicted on Ganz. Sharing his podcast on Twitter, she wrote, “This was never about vengeance; it’s about vindication. That’s why it didn’t feel right to just accept his apology in private (although I did that, too). Because if any part of this process should be done in the light, it’s the forgiveness part. And so, @danharmon, I forgive you.”

But Harmon also did some repair work by using his relatively sizable platform for good. At the end of his apology monologue, he entreats his audience to “think about it,” i.e. consider what role power, sex, and gender play in their interpersonal and professional relationships:

Think about it. No matter who you are at work, no matter where you’re working, no matter what field you’re in, no matter what position you have over or under or side by side with somebody, just think about it.  You gotta, because if you don’t think about it, you’re gonna get away with not thinking about it, and you can cause a lot of damage that is technically legal and hurts everybody. […] And if we can make it a normal part of our culture that we think about it and possibly talk about it, then maybe we can get to a better place where that stuff doesn’t happen.

Evaluation: Exceeds Expectations

Harmon’s second draft apology earns high marks for his serious consideration of the accusations made against him, his comprehensive ownership of his actions and their consequences, and the use of his platform to invite more honest, radical, and vulnerable conversations about the interplay between power, gender, and sex.

Grade: A+

Closing Thoughts on the Radicalness and Rarity of the Harmon/Ganz Apology

The Harmon apology is great, most people agree on that. But it’s also fascinating because the collaboration that led up to it relied on increasingly rare and radical acts of courage, honesty, openness, and attentiveness. Harmon’s willingness to come clean about his behavior, Ganz’s willingness to engage and challenge him on his statements, Harmon’s willingness to hear Ganz’s critiques and incorporate them into his apology, Ganz’s willingness to forgive him publicly — these are by no means common or unchallenging approaches to the dance that occurs when an accusation elicits a response.

It is incredibly and understandably rare for public apologies centering around sexual misconduct to play out in this way. It is unthinkable to expect a victim of any sort of sexual misconduct to coach the person who victimized them. But Ganz went there. And thanks to her courageous editing efforts and Harmon’s commitment to improvement, we have a public apology that serves as a model of what it looks like when a public figure says they’re sorry and the public knows they mean it.
