Tips for Writing Satirical News Headlines

5 writers share their secrets (for writing satirical news)

Julie Vick
Sep 15, 2025

Today’s post is part of my “Writing Resources and Tips” section. Friendly reminder that if you are not interested in writing topics, you can adjust your subscription preferences by visiting this link and unselecting any sections that don’t interest you.

You have probably read satirical news headlines on sites such as The Onion and Reductress and may be interested in writing them yourself.

Submitting to satirical news sites is a little different than other satire sites because you often submit batches of headlines rather than a full piece (and sometimes the sites only run a headline without a full article).

My experience with satirical news writing is limited (and by limited, I mean I submitted one batch of headlines to Reductress several years ago, and none of them were picked up). So, I asked other writers to weigh in on this topic.

In this post, five writers — Jeff Bender, Kelley Greene, Heidi Lux, Katy Maiolatesi, and Ysabel Yates — share the backstory behind one of their published headlines and additional tips for this type of writing.

Since I know many people may also wonder about the process for writing for The Onion (which does not have open submissions that anyone can submit to), Jeff also shared some intel on The Onion and his experience doing a headline trial for them.

I picked up lots of good tips from these writers, and I think you will too.

Ysabel Yates

Ysabel Yates is a humor writer and co-author of Jokes to Offend Men. She writes Humor Science, a newsletter about humor writing.

What is the backstory to one of your headlines?

My first headline that was accepted for Reductress was, ‘I Couldn’t Imagine Living Outside the City,’ Says Woman Who Only Leaves Her Apartment Once a Week. It was actually the headline that got me accepted as a contributor, because you submitted a packet with multiple headlines, and that was the one from my packet that got chosen for publication.

I know exactly the moment I thought of it: my partner at the time and I were talking, and he said, "I couldn't imagine living outside the city" and I said, "...says the man who only leaves his apartment once a week." At the time, we were both workaholic homebodies living together in Brooklyn. I thought what he said was really silly, but I could relate to it as well. So I guess he gets half the writing credit on that one.

Any headline writing tips?

Write a bunch of headlines and brainstorm continuously. I wouldn't have thought of that headline if I weren't actively putting together a packet at the time and hadn't already spent a few days brainstorming.

I think that's really the key with satirical news writing — at least for me, I have to really shift into that zone where I'm refracting every thought I have through the lens of, "Could this be a headline?" Once your brain is in that zone, the ideas come naturally and you'll be able to capture them as they come.

Heidi Lux

Heidi Lux a Los Angeles based screenwriter and satirist who has written for sites including Eater, Business Insider, Reductress, and McSweeney's and is currently a managing editor of The Belladonna Comedy. Her screenwriting credits include Tubi (Crushed) and Nickelodeon (Dora the Explorer, The Casagrandes). She's on instagram at @fabulux.

What is the backstory to one of your headlines?

I wrote the Reductress headline Studies Show Women Have Happier Relationships When Their Partners Aren't Massive Douchebags, which I wish I could say came out of a personal frustration, but was born because I had to force myself to come up with a 10th idea to be able to submit to Reductress.

I came across an article about a study on women's relationships, which felt a little obvious. Like, no shit Einstein, you needed a study to figure that out? I thought of what would be dumb, obvious thing you don't need a study to know, and came up with this idea.

It ended up going crazy viral, which was surprising (and on some level, frustrating because the pieces I worked hard on or were based on something personal didn't get as much traction).

But I think it spread so fast because it's simple, relatable, and it sparked a debate. Somehow, people have a lot of strong opinions on whether or not women want someone who doesn't treat them like dirt, unfortunately.

Any headline writing tips?

The formula that I used for this headline that I find helpful is to go to the source material for the thing you're making fun of, take the first half of a real headline, and use it as the setup, then fill in the end with a funny twist, which serves as the punchline - if that makes sense?

It's helpful to mimic the "normal" thing (e.g., using similar wording, matching tone), because it sets up the context, then your funny, silly, imaginative idea breaks the context with something unexpected. You basically have to make the reader think they're walking into something normal, then hit them with a surprising twist to find the humor.

Kelley Greene

Kelley Greene is a chronically online satirist and writer, currently working at BuzzFeed. She has a weekly Substack newsletter called TMI - Too Much Internet, and you can find more of her work at kelleygreene.pizza.

What is the backstory to one of your headlines?

Here's one of my most recent headlines, published by The Hard Times: Hooters Closes Stores Amid Bankruptcy as Younger Generation Favors Bespoke, Craft Titties

I saw a news story about Hooters closing stores due to bankruptcy, and the first thing that crossed my mind (and made me laugh) was, "Oh, look, millennials are killing another industry!" so I knew it was something I thought could be funny.

The original headline on that story says, "Hooters abruptly closes dozens of restaurants amid bankruptcy restructuring," so I wanted to keep enough of that headline to give context (and make it feel like a real news headline), and then end with a funny twist. "Hooters Closes Stores Amid Bankruptcy" felt short and snappy, and got the point across.

I solidified that as the first half of the headline. Next, I spent some time brainstorming about the things that have been said in the past when something falls out of fashion with a younger generation (what all have millennials "killed" these days, and why does the media say it's dead...?). One thing that came to mind is the craft beer movement, and people's desire these days for a more unique experience versus something commercialized.

That felt like the right angle, and after a few tries writing it out, both "bespoke" and "craft" seemed the right descriptors and read the funniest to me. It was the same with the word choice of "titties" over "boobs" or something else — my rewrites included a few different options, but that ultimately sounded best to me.

Any headline writing tips?

My best tips for writing satirical headlines are: the shorter the better. That means sometimes taking out even syllables that feel unnecessary. Read your headline aloud to yourself a few times.

Do you find yourself stumbling over any of the words, or slowing down? Is the portion of the headline that's there for context so long that it's taking away from the joke? Shorten it!

And secondly — try to go beyond the first thing that comes to mind. As I mentioned in my headline, my first thought was "millennials have killed another industry," but rather than writing that directly, I spent some time figuring out how to get at that joke from another angle.

It's still ultimately the same joke, but with another layer on top that makes it feel more news-like versus more like commentary or opinion. Finally, think about what makes YOU laugh, and write the headlines you want to see. Chances are, a lot of other people are going to resonate with the same thing!

Katy Maiolatesi

Katy Maiolatesi is a comedy writer and artist with work in Reductress, McSweeney’s, Jenny Magazine, and The Belladonna, where she also does social media. Find her on Instagram @kmaiolatesi, Bluesky @kmaiolatesi.bsky.social, and at katymaiolatesi.com.

Katy shared this Headline Backstory, which she originally wrote about in this piece:

I workshopped this published Reductress headline: “People-Pleasing Woman Gives Soul to Devil Without Him Even Asking.” Initially, I went through several ideas:

  • I LIVED IT: I Sold My Soul to the Devil Immediately After He Said I Had Nice Eyes

  • Woman Immediately Sells Soul to Devil After He Compliments Her Hair

  • Wow! This Woman is Such a People-Pleaser She Handed Her Soul to the Devil Without Him Even Asking

  • ‘Here, Take It!’ Says People-Pleasing Woman to Devil Without Him Even Asking About Soul

I ultimately decided to use fewer words, like deleting “the” in front of devil, placing “people-pleasing” in front of “woman,” and eliminating an unnecessary “wow!”

Jeff Bender

Jeff Bender writes comedy for The Hard Times, McSweeney's, Electric Literature, Points in Case, and Slackjaw. He's a Senior Writer at Sports Riot. He is also teaching a 6-week course on this very subject called News-Style Satire for Hugo House (Seattle) that starts next month.

What is the backstory to one of your headlines?

I went to The Brandywine Museum of Art in Chadds Ford, PA, while my car was being worked on and saw a couple dads breezing through the galleries, like, yeah, yeah, I get it. I thought about how dads (men?) like me are so scared to confront art and their feelings, and I wrote the headline "Nation's Dads Get Through Art Museum in 6 Minutes," which was published in The Hard Times.

In the article, the 6 minutes of course includes a stop at the men's room and a visit to the cafe. For Sports Riot, I wrote "Bengals In Talks With Jaguars About Cat Sex," which did well online and which I still don't really understand.

Any headline writing tips?

Yes, from Scott Dikkers, former editor of The Onion: Write 10 jokes a day. Not all of them will be news-style satire, and definitely not all will be funny. I do it on the Notes app on my iPhone.

Also: When you want to submit a headline, WAIT a few days. The Hard Times lets you pitch whenever, and it's tempting to get trigger happy with a new idea. I recommend waiting three days to see if it's still funny.

You also mentioned doing a headline trial for The Onion. Can you talk more about that?

From what I know, there are 3 ways to write for The Onion:

  1. Apply through their "Careers" site when jobs are posted. Jobs are posted once every two or three years, but if you join the Facebook group How to Write Funny (another Scott Dikkers' creation), you should be well informed. A lot of the full-time jobs necessitate living in or moving to Chicago, though I know some people who were offered remote headline / features trials based on the strength of their applications.

  2. Get recommended for a trial by someone who already writes headlines or features for The Onion. (This is how I got my headline trial.) "Features" just means you write jokes on a premise, like "All The Demands Trump Is Making Of The Smithsonian."

  3. Attend the Diverse as Fuck workshops in person (Chicago) or online.

My experience: There's so much I hope to do differently next time, but I was very lucky to be recommended for a 4-week headline trial. In the trial, I submitted 10 headlines every Thursday. These were added to the general pool and then sifted by the editors into the Shortlist (1st Round) and then the Roundup (final round). The headlines from the roundup made the website.

It was a lot of fun writing for the editors, and I hope to do it again. For two days I was really kind of broken up when I didn't make the headlines staff, which has about 50 writers. But then my wife tried to cheer me up saying, "It's not that great a job." (She was referring to the remote-headline-contributor.)

She has a point: You barely make any money, it's highly impersonal, and many groups of 10 headlines are unceremoniously rejected. That said, it is The Onion! The power of the brand can't be understated. I still want to do it.

But I really am enjoying writing for The Hard Times and Sports Riot. Sports Riot is a small group of writers, some of whom write for The Onion and one writes for The Daily Show. We're incorporated on Instagram and have a growing mass of 85-87K followers, depending on who we offend (mostly bowling fans). We meet every Wednesday night and spend the first half hour talking about our lives. In other words, highly personal.

Back to me, Julie, here.

I appreciated all these insights, as one thing on my writing goal list this year is to submit a batch of satirical headlines somewhere (and I still have a few months in the year left to do it!)

If you are looking for places to submit satirical news, I’ve got a few places listed in this humor markets list.

One other final note, I have for many years been referring to the type of piece we discussed here as “fake news” pieces, I think because that is how I first heard them referred to several years ago, and because that’s how some satirical venues have referred to them.

But I believe that term started before fake news came to mean something different in the Trump era. Jeff mentioned this when I corresponded with him, and I can see why confusing these two might be problematic! So, I will probably try to refer to Onion-style headlines and pieces as satirical news in the future.
