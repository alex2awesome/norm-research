Born Standing Up Summary In 10 Words

Steve Martin. Childhood, Magician, College, Sixties, Seventies, Building and Quitting.

High 5 Key Ideas

  1. Do What You Love: Steve Martin is telling you to do what you love—to do what you were born to do.
    • His story, and ascent into comedy, are fluid and smooth because that was who he was.
  2. Pursue the Process: Focus on the craft, the process, and the learning and growth not the money or accolades.
    • The easiest way to reach your standard of success is not to pursue it but to let it emerge as a side-effect of your effort, learning, and skill development.
  3. You’re the Only One in Your Way: There’s nothing holding you back but yourself.
    • He wasn’t a naturally talented singer, dancer, or actor, and because of that reality, he had to be creative and find solutions to keep doing what he loved: sing, dance, and act.
    • You don’t have to be great to love what you do.
  4. It Takes Time: Overnight success takes decades of building yourself and your skills, one brick at a time.
  5. Walk Away: When you are done, truly done, it’s okay to walk away and let go.
    • Learn the difference between quitting and walking away from something for the right reasons.

Born Standing Up Summary in 4 Sentences

  1. This is a short read of Steve Martin’s slow meander down memory lane and how he became one of the most prolific comedians, almost accidentally.
  2. The book starts from his childhood and his first gig as a magician to getting started in stand-up in San Francisco during the 60’s, and culminates at his peak in the 80’s.
  3. Born Standing Up isn’t a celebrity memoir filled with shocking tales. It’s more like notes on his past life—a meditation on how art gets made when you don’t even know you’re making art.
  4. Martin let go of stand-up so completely that he didn’t think about it for 25 years.

In a sense, this book is not an autobiography but a biography, because I am writing about someone I used to know.

Born Standing Up Summary Top 3 Quotes

  1. “Enjoyment while performing was rare—enjoyment would have been an indulgent loss of focus that comedy cannot afford. After the shows, however, I experienced long hours of elation or misery depending on how the show went, because doing comedy alone onstage is the ego’s last stand.”
  2. “Through the years, I have learned there is no harm in charging oneself up with delusions between moments of valid inspiration.”
  3. “I did stand-up comedy for 18 years. Ten of those years were spent learning, four years were spent refining, and four years were spent in wild success.”

2 Ways Born Standing Up Will Change You

  1. You’ll know that you are not alone in your struggles—even those who are the “Rich and Famous” have the same struggles, too.
  2. This book will make you think about what you are truly meant to be doing with your life. And hopefully, you’ll be inspired to go after it.

1 Thing To Implement After Reading Born Standing Up

  1. Remember, always enjoy what you have right now because in the future you will miss it when it is no longer what it once was.
    • You’ll miss the beginning struggles when they are gone.

I wanted to be there again, if only for a day, indulging in high spirits and high jinks, before I turned professional, before comedy became serious.

The Rating Explained

  • I enjoyed Martin’s candid nature in telling his life story, but this isn’t the best memoir out there, nor is it the worst. Steve Martin lived such an interesting life because he inadvertently revolutionized comedy but had no idea he was doing anything big at all—he was just doing what he wanted. Despite his impressive comedy career, this book is average at best with some entertaining insights, but it lacks a bit of intensity. It feels more like a slow walk down memory lane. In a way, it reads like a journal, and while there is nothing wrong with that, it isn’t particularly gripping either, which gives Born Standing Up a 3.5-Stars: Good. On top of that, it only spans the course of his stand-up career, ending in the Eighties. I did, however, enjoy his description of the changing times and general attitude of the country in the 60’s and 70’s. Honestly, there are much better memoirs out there if you are limited on time—I would even say Greenlights by Matthew McConaughey is much better.
  • The Stickiness Factor is a 2/10: I really don’t remember much from this book. Only a few silly anecdotes here and there.

Who Should Read It?

  • If you are into comedy, or curious about a comedian’s life, this one has enough to keep you entertained.
  • Of course, if you grew up watching Steve Martin, you’ll enjoy learning more about his comedy career before life on the screen.

My Contrarian Takes

(My critical perspective on the Born Standing Up Summary key lessons.)

What If…?

  1. Success doesn’t always have to be lonely?
    • Admittedly, I’m writing this argument while sitting alone, in a room in Thailand, trying to grow my business…
    • But maybe success doesn’t have to be lonely.
    • So many successful people, Steve Martin included, say they went through the loneliest era of their life during their ascent to the top. I do believe you have to truly be obsessed with your process to make it, but that doesn’t mean you have to go at it alone.
    • In fact, surrounding yourself with others who are on the same path will probably accelerate your growth.
  2. Martin’s success was mostly luck?
    • Martin would admit this himself, I’m sure: much of his “comedy revolution” just came down to pure luck and timing.
    • Martin was original, yes, but he was also in the right place at the right moment when absurdism and anti-humor became briefly mainstream.
  3. The book was too emotionally flat?
    • One of my main arguments with the book was that it was lacking any real emotional connection to Martin and his story.
    • It felt like he was just as disconnected from that part of his life as we are.
    • He never really explores his joy, rage, or grief, other than just stating emotional situations as just facts.
    • Even the tragic scenes—his father’s coldness, the deaths, the loneliness—are told with no emotional connection.

Born Standing Up Summary, Review and Notes

My summaries are organized in a way that tells the book’s story through a thread of essential lessons and ideas—supported by life-changing quotes—not by chapter. This benefits understanding and recall. All quotes are from the author unless specified otherwise (I’ve added emphasis for effect). My transformative summaries are intended as companions to the book and do not substitute reading the original work.

Where It All Started

  • Martin’s comedy education began at Knott’s Berry Farm and Disneyland, where he worked in the magic shop, learning tricks and performing for tourists.
    • The magic shop taught him timing, audience management, and the crucial skill of holding attention.
  • More importantly, it taught him that he didn’t actually want to be a magician.
  • Comedy became both an escape from and an attempt to win approval from his father.

When Comedy Met Intellect

  • While studying philosophy at Cal State Long Beach, he started to rethink his approach to comedy.
    • He became obsessed with the idea:
      • What if you removed the punchline?
      • What if the audience never got the release of the laugh?
      • What if the tension just built and built until the absence of the joke became the joke?

18 Years to “Overnight Success”

  • The First 10 Years: Empty clubs, hostile audiences, driving hundreds of miles for five minutes of stage time.
    • He played folk music in coffeehouses, appeared at corporate events, performed wherever anyone would let him onstage.You have to love something so much that you’re willing to be terrible at it for years.

In this netherworld, I was free to experiment. These out-of-the-way and varied places provided a tough comedy education. There were no mentors to tell me what to do; there were no guidebooks for doing stand-up. Everything was learned in practice, and the lonely road, with no critical eyes watching, was the place to dig up my boldest, or dumbest, ideas and put them onstage.

  • The fake arrow through the head. The “happy feet.” The banjo. The balloon animals. It was comedy about comedy, performance about performance.

My most persistent memory of stand-up is of my mouth being in the present and my mind being in the future: the mouth speaking the line, the body delivering the gesture, while the mind looks back, observing, analyzing, judging, worrying, and then deciding when and what to say next.

  • If the audience didn’t laugh, that was part of the act. If they were confused, that was intended. He turned bombing into an art form.

Now that I had assigned myself to an act without jokes, I gave myself a rule. Never let them know I was bombing: This is funny, you just haven’t gotten it yet.

The Power of Naïveté

  • Sometimes, not knowing what the heck you’re doing is actually beneficial because you don’t know the rules—you have no boundaries.

Despite a lack of natural ability, I did have the one element necessary to all early creativity: naïveté, that fabulous quality that keeps you from knowing just how unsuited you are for what you are about to do.

Connecting the Dots

I’ve come across this idea before. Sara Blakely, the Founder of Spanx, attributes her success to the fact that she was naïve in business: she just called Neiman Marcus and scheduled a meeting, and apparently, that was considered “impossible” to do.

“Everyone said to ‘talk to the experts.’ But the truth is… if I had, @spanx probably wouldn’t exist. Sometimes the expert is YOU.”

The Price of Persistence

  • Determination trumps natural talent every time.
    • He was willing to fail repeatedly in public for over a decade.
  • Martin’s success wasn’t about natural talent, and he’s brutally honest about his limitations.

Be Delusional

  • You have to believe you’re doing something important if you’re making balloon animals for drunk people at 2 AM.
  • Many of the most successful people, like Tim Ferriss, mention this idea: You must have a bit of delusion in order to make it. Otherwise, you will talk yourself out of doing what it takes to make your dreams come true.

The Arena Problem

  • Martin went from intimate clubs where every gesture mattered to basketball arenas where he was “a white dot” to most of the audience.
  • The absurdist touches that killed in a 200-seat club became meaningless in a 20,000-seat venue.
  • At his peak, Martin describes surreal scenes of thousands of people following him through streets after shows.
  • The intimate connection with an audience that drove him to comedy was replaced by mob hysteria.

The Decision to Quit

In 1981, at the absolute peak of his fame, Martin walked away from stand-up.

Actionable Takeaways

(Practical lessons and actionable steps you can apply from the Born Standing Up book.)

  1. Reflect on the Struggle
    1. “I just believe that the interesting time in a career is pre-success, what shaped things, how did you get to this point?”
    2. Action: Journal on how you got to where you are today.
      1. List out the successes and failures, obstacles and triumphs.
      2. Then, map out where you want to go, and the challenges you hope to face (because facing and overcoming is always the best part, even though it might not feel like it in the moment).
  2. Get Closure
    • “If you have anything to work out with your parents, do it now. One day it will be too late.”

❓Final Question for You to Ponder

  1. If you knew you would never achieve any great success or financial reward, but you would still have just enough to survive and be okay, what one thing would you do? What’s the one thing that you love the process of so much that you just want to learn more and improve at?

My Final Take

  • Keep this for when you’ve run out of life-changing memoirs to read, and you feel the need to know more about Steve Martin.
