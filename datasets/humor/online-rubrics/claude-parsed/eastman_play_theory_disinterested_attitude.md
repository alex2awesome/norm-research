# Eastman's Play Theory: Humor as Disinterested Attitude

Max Eastman developed an early *play theory* of humor: humor is a special kind of play, in which we adopt a disinterested attitude toward what could otherwise be taken seriously, especially toward pains, frustrations, and disappointments.

## The defining formula

> "We come into the world endowed with an instinctive tendency to laugh and have this feeling of amusement in response to pains presented playfully."

Humor is not the absence of negative content; it is the *playful framing* of negative content.

## The four laws of comic art (from Enjoyment of Laughter, 1936)

Eastman articulates four constraints on humor production:

### Law 1: Be in fun
The audience must be in a *playful state of mind* before the comic stimulus arrives. If they are in earnest, no joke will land. The comedian's first job is to put them in fun.

### Law 2: Convey a sense of artifice or play
The humorous material itself must signal "this is play." Through tone, pacing, exaggeration, the speaker tells the audience: I am not serious about this. Don't take this as a sincere proposition.

### Law 3: Make the disappointment plain
The comic event must clearly involve a *disappointment* — a frustration of expectation, a fall from dignity, a thwarting of desire. Without identifiable disappointment, there is no comic substance.

### Law 4: Make the disappointment joyous
The disappointment must be presented in such a way that it produces *enjoyment* rather than distress. This requires:
- The right pace.
- The right surrounding affect.
- Triviality of stakes (or stakes that the audience can hold lightly).
- The signal that the disappointment is contained.

## Why play is the master frame

Eastman argues that humor's connection to play explains:

- Why children laugh at things adults wouldn't (children are more often in the play frame).
- Why mock aggression is funnier than real aggression.
- Why insult comedy works between friends but not strangers (the play contract is stronger).
- Why jokes fail at funerals (no play frame is available).

## The disinterested attitude

The crucial cognitive condition is *disinterestedness* — the audience temporarily releases their genuine investment in outcomes:
- The hero falls — but we don't really care about the hero.
- The institution is mocked — but we don't really care about the institution at this moment.
- The misfortune occurs — but we don't really feel it.

If the audience cares too much, the play frame collapses and humor fails.

## What this requires of the comic stimulus

1. **Setup must reinforce the play frame** — light tone, exaggeration, stylization.
2. **A real disappointment / negative event must occur** — without it, no comic substance.
3. **The audience's interest must not be too strongly engaged** — stakes are kept light or removed.
4. **Joy must be the actual response** — not pity, anger, or shock.

## What this excludes

- Serious propositions presented sincerely (no play frame).
- Real disasters affecting people we care about (interest defeats disinterestedness).
- Trivial bland content with no disappointment (no comic substance).

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the audience in fun? Has the speaker put them there?
- Does the material itself signal "this is play"?
- Is there an identifiable disappointment / fall / frustration?
- Is the disappointment small enough, or framed playfully enough, that the response is joyous rather than distressing?
- Has the audience's normal serious interest been suspended?
