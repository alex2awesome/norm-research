# Brett Goldstein's Substack: Comedy and Heart

Brett Goldstein (Roy Kent on Ted Lasso, stand-up, Films to be Buried With) writes occasionally on the relationship between comedy and emotional truth.

## The Core Argument
The funniest moments are NEVER just funny. They land harder when there's an emotional undertow — usually grief, longing, or shame — that the comedy is trying to hold up against. Pure jokes feel like dust in the mouth.

## The Heart-Joke Equation
A line lands harder when:
- The character means it sincerely (not winking).
- The audience knows the character has been hurt.
- The joke is the character's coping mechanism, not the writer's flourish.
- The laugh is followed (within a beat) by a SILENCE.

## On Writing Roy Kent
- Roy curses because he doesn't have the language for tenderness.
- The funniest Roy lines are the ones where his anger is misdirected love.
- The audience laughs because they recognize the displacement.

## On Stand-Up and Vulnerability
- Don't tell stories where you're the hero.
- Tell stories where you're the idiot — but where you're TRYING.
- The trying is the heart; the failing is the joke.

## Brett's Working Rubric
1. Is the comedic premise built on an emotional truth?
2. Is the character (or you) trying sincerely at something?
3. Is the joke the coping mechanism, not the punchline?
4. Is there a beat of silence available after the laugh?
5. Would a reader who took the line seriously still relate to it?
6. Does the laugh leave a small ache?
