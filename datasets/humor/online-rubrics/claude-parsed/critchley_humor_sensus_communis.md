# Critchley: Humor's Double Movement

Critchley's On Humour (2002) presents humor as a *double movement*: it both reaffirms the shared common sense of a community (sensus communis) and also explodes that common sense from within.

## The defining structure

Humor has a paradoxical double character:

### Movement 1: Affirming the shared world (sensus communis)
- A joke depends on a shared background of expectations, norms, and cultural meaning.
- The joke recalls us to "common sense" — to what we, as a community, take for granted.
- Without shared sensus communis, the joke can't land.
- Laughing together is a way of saying "yes, we share this world."

### Movement 2: Departing from / exploding the shared world (dissensus communis)
- The joke estranges us from what we take for granted.
- It defamiliarizes the everyday by showing it from a strange angle.
- It "explodes the sheer arbitrariness of social rites" through "miniature strategies of defamiliarization."
- For a moment, we see how things could be otherwise.

The two movements coexist in the same comic act.

## What jokes are *of*

Critchley argues jokes work on three core human anxieties:

### 1. Our animal body
We laugh at the body, at bodily functions, at the contrast between our self-image as rational and our reality as fleshy creatures.
- Jokes about food, sex, digestion, breath, decay.
- Jokes about pretentious behavior collapsing into bodily inelegance.

### 2. Our mortality
We laugh at death, at memento mori, at the gap between our lifeplans and our certain end.
- Gallows humor.
- Jokes about funerals, illness, aging.
- The pretensions of the living against the leveling fact of dying.

### 3. The mechanical / our resemblance to machines
Critchley follows Bergson here: we laugh when the human reveals itself as machine-like.
- Repetitive behavior.
- Predictable responses.
- Inflexibility where flexibility was needed.

These three sources are connected: the body and the mortality and the mechanical are all the not-quite-human aspects of our humanity.

## Ethical humor

Critchley argues that the highest humor is *ethical*:

- It draws attention to our common condition (mortal, embodied, fallible).
- It does so not by mocking specific others but by inviting us to laugh together at *us*.
- This laughter is **liberating** because it undoes pretension and reaffirms shared humanity.
- Such humor is different from the cruelty of mere mockery.

## The community-building function

Because humor depends on sensus communis:

- Communities define themselves partly by what they find funny.
- Sharing a sense of humor signals and reinforces belonging.
- A joke that fails marks the failure of shared background — it's not just unfunny but socially diagnostic.
- Outsiders to a community often can't access in-group humor; mastery of the group's humor is mastery of its world.

## Necessary conditions for Critchley-style humor

1. **A shared sensus communis** between speaker and audience.
2. **A movement of departure** — the joke must defamiliarize, not merely affirm.
3. **A return** — the joke must remain within the community's grasp; total alienation produces incomprehension.
4. **Reference to one of the core sources** — body, mortality, or the mechanical — to give the joke human weight.

## Diagnostic questions

- What sensus communis does the joke presuppose?
- Where does it depart from / defamiliarize that shared world?
- Does it return — does the audience come back to common sense after the comic departure?
- Does it touch on body, mortality, or the mechanical (Critchley's three sources)?
- Is the laughter ethical (liberating shared humanity) or cruel (mocking outsiders)?
