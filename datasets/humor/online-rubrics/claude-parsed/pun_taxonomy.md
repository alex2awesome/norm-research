# Pun Taxonomy

## Definition
Puns involve polysemy, homonymy, or phonological similarity to another word, for an intended humorous or rhetorical effect.

## Main Types

### Homophonic puns
- Words sound the same but spelled differently with different meanings.
- Example: "Atheism is a non-prophet institution."
- Two words share sound, differ in spelling and meaning.

### Homographic puns
- Words spelled the same but sound different and have different meanings.
- Example: "I used to be a banker, but I lost interest."
- Two words share spelling but differ in sound and meaning.

### Homonymic puns
- Same spelling AND same sound, different meanings.
- Combines homographs and homophones.
- Example: "Time flies like an arrow; fruit flies like a banana." (flies = verb / noun)

### Compound puns
- Multiple puns in the same sentence.
- Example: "Why do we still have troops in Germany? To keep the Russians in Czech."

### Recursive puns
- Second part requires understanding of first.
- Example: "A Freudian slip is when you say one thing but mean your mother."

### Visual puns
- Image-based wordplay.
- Common in cartoons, including New Yorker.

### Bilingual puns
- Use words from two languages.

## Why Puns Are (Sometimes) Funny
- Two scripts activated simultaneously (script opposition).
- Reader recognizes the dual meaning.
- "Aha" moment of seeing the linkage.

## Why Puns Are Sometimes Groan-Worthy
- Too obvious / forced.
- Pun is the only payoff (no additional insight).
- Pun "groan" is itself ritual response.

## Rubric
1. What kind of pun (homophone/homograph/homonym/compound/visual)?
2. Are both meanings active simultaneously?
3. Does the dual meaning illuminate something (insight) or just match (groan)?
4. Is the connection precise or strained?
5. Does the pun fit organically into the sentence?
