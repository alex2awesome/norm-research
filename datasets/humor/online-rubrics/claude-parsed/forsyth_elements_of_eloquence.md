# Mark Forsyth — The Elements of Eloquence

A guide to 39 rhetorical figures that make phrases memorable and often funny.

## Why Relevant to Humor
Many figures of rhetoric are inherently comic devices. They create rhythm, surprise, and density that make jokes land.

## Key Figures (selected)
- **Alliteration**: repeated initial sounds ("She sells sea shells").
- **Antithesis**: contrasting ideas in parallel ("To err is human; to forgive divine").
- **Anaphora**: repeated word at start ("We shall fight on the beaches…").
- **Epistrophe**: repeated word at end.
- **Symploce**: anaphora + epistrophe combined.
- **Polysyndeton**: many conjunctions ("and… and… and…").
- **Asyndeton**: no conjunctions ("I came, I saw, I conquered").
- **Hyperbole**: extreme exaggeration.
- **Litotes**: ironic understatement ("not bad" = excellent).
- **Paradox**: apparent contradiction with truth.
- **Chiasmus**: A-B-B-A structure ("Ask not what your country can do for you…").
- **Antimetabole**: words reversed ("Live to eat or eat to live?").
- **Zeugma**: one word governs two clauses oddly ("She broke his car and his heart").
- **Hendiadys**: one idea expressed by two words ("nice and warm").
- **Diacope**: phrase repeated with words in between ("Bond. James Bond.").
- **Tricolon**: three parallel parts ("Friends, Romans, countrymen").
- **Enallage**: deliberate grammatical error.
- **Anadiplosis**: end of one clause = start of next ("Fear leads to anger; anger leads to hate…").
- **Synaesthesia**: mixing senses ("loud color").
- **Epizeuxis**: immediate repetition ("alone, alone, all all alone").
- **Pleonasm**: redundancy for effect.
- **Aposiopesis**: trailing off…
- **Catachresis**: deliberate misuse of metaphor.

## Rubric (Rhetorical Polish)
1. Does the joke use a rhythmic rhetorical figure?
2. Is there parallel structure (chiasmus, tricolon)?
3. Are sound patterns deployed (alliteration)?
4. Is exaggeration or understatement strategic?
5. Does the line "snap" — feel quotable?
