# Norm Macdonald: What Is Funny (compiled craft talks)

Synthesis of Norm Macdonald's recurring craft observations from interviews with Conan, Letterman, Joe Rogan, and Howard Stern.

## Norm's Working Theory
The audience laughs at the moment they realize THEY were thinking the wrong thing. This is why obvious punchlines fail: the audience never had to be wrong. The joke must require a misdirection that the audience is willing to accept.

## The Long Joke as Anti-Joke
Norm's signature: tell a long, ramshackle setup that the audience assumes will pay off in a clever twist; deliver an utterly stupid or obvious punchline. The laugh is at the audience's own anticipation, betrayed.

## On the Moth Joke
- The setup is detailed, baroque, philosophical.
- The audience assumes the punch will match the elevation.
- The punch is "because the light was on."
- The laugh is at the GAP between effort and payoff — and at the audience's own complicity in expecting elevation.

## On What's Always Funny
- Surprise.
- A surprising idiot.
- A man saying he's not afraid (then revealing he is).
- The wrong word at the right time.
- A long joke with a stupid ending.

## On What's Never Funny
- A joke that explains itself.
- Cruelty without self-implication.
- Topical jokes, except as a vehicle for evergreen ones.
- Anything that announces "this is funny."

## Norm's Working Rubric
1. Will the audience be required to ASSUME wrongly?
2. Is the punchline simpler than the setup deserved?
3. Does the joke avoid winking at the audience?
4. Is there an idiot in the joke (often the speaker)?
5. Could the joke survive being told slowly?
6. Is the laugh partly at the audience's own anticipation?
