# Megan Amram: Joke-Writing Thread (synthesis)

Megan Amram is a TV writer (Parks and Rec, The Good Place) and one of the early "Twitter comedians." Her threads on joke construction emphasize the discipline of the 280-character form.

## The Twitter-Joke Anatomy
1. **Hook in line 1**: a sentence that makes scrolling stop.
2. **Premise in line 2**: the wrong assumption.
3. **Punch in line 3**: the pivot.
- Variations: a single line that does all three by structure.

## Why Most Tweet-Jokes Fail
- The hook is too clever; the premise can't catch up.
- The premise requires Twitter context only the writer has.
- The punch is at the start of the tweet (gone by retweet).
- The author thanked themselves at the end ("lol I'm sorry").

## Word Economy Rules
- 280 chars is too many. Aim for 140.
- Cut "just," "really," "very," "literally," "kind of."
- Replace "I think that..." with the assertion.
- Replace participial phrases ("having said that...") with sentences.

## The Reply Rule
- Don't reply to your own tweet to clarify the joke.
- If the joke needs clarification, it's not done.
- Delete and rewrite, don't append.

## On Industry Twitter
- The pros are reading. Treat every tweet as a writing sample.
- A great tweet has gotten people staffed.
- Conversely, a bad tweet has lost people staff.

## Amram-Inflected Working Rubric
1. Does line 1 stop scrolling?
2. Is the premise universal enough to land outside your bubble?
3. Is the punch in the FINAL clause, not the first?
4. Have you cut every filler word?
5. Did you avoid replying to clarify?
6. Could a TV showrunner read it as a writing sample?
