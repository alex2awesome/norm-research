# Attardo's Architecture of Long-Form Humorous Texts

Standard joke theories (SSTH, GTVH, Suls) handle short jokes with a single setup-punchline structure. But long humorous texts (short stories, novels, sitcom episodes, stand-up routines) need a richer apparatus. Attardo (2001) introduces several constructs to model how humor distributes across extended texts.

## Punch lines vs. jab lines

### Punch line
- Text-final.
- Disrupts the narrative.
- The text ends or is heavily punctuated by it.
- The classical joke ends here.

### Jab line
- **NOT text-final.**
- **Does not disrupt narrative flow** — the story continues after it.
- Ontologically similar to a punch line (introduces a script opposition).
- Lives within the body of a longer text.
- A long humorous text contains many jab lines and may end with one major punch line.

This distinction matters: most humor in extended texts is in jab lines, not punch lines.

## The text vector

The **text vector** is the linear sequence of the text, segmented (e.g., into 100-word chunks). Plotting jab/punch line density across the vector reveals the *humor topology* of the text — where humor is concentrated, where it's sparse.

A 12,500-word humorous story Attardo analyzed contained **253 jab/punch lines**, with at least three peak-density sections.

## The four organizational constructs

### 1. Strand
A sequence of related jab lines connected by topic, character, theme, or running gag. A strand can extend across many pages.
- Example: every time a particular character appears, a jab line about their idiosyncrasy.

### 2. Substrand
A subdivision within a strand — narrower thematic continuity.

### 3. Central vs. peripheral strand
- **Central strands** are tied to the main plot and characters; their jab lines are core to the work's humor.
- **Peripheral strands** are tangential threads — running gags, side characters, recurring imagery.
- The interplay between central and peripheral strands creates texture.

### 4. Stack
A *group of related strands* that interconnect (multiple themes converging on a common comic territory).

### 5. Comb
A strand with **more than 3 jab lines occurring relatively close to one another** in the text — a burst of comic density.

## Necessary structural elements for successful long-form humor

1. **Distributed jab lines** — humor must occur regularly enough that the audience does not lose the comic frame.
2. **Density variation** — combs (concentrated bursts) alternate with sparser sections to maintain rhythm.
3. **Strand coherence** — jab lines that belong to a strand reinforce each other (the audience anticipates the next instance, then enjoys the variation).
4. **Strand termination or culmination** — major strands often resolve in a punch line that integrates the running material.
5. **Central / peripheral balance** — the work has primary comic threads but is enriched by minor ones.

## Why this matters

A long humorous work is not "many jokes in a row." It is an *architecture*. The same set of jab lines can be funny or unfunny depending on how they're sequenced, which strands they belong to, and how density is distributed.

This explains:
- Why some sitcom episodes feel rich and others thin despite similar joke counts.
- Why a comedian's set order matters as much as joke quality.
- Why parody and satire work as sustained pieces, not just collections of one-liners.

## What this excludes

- Short jokes (covered by other models).
- Texts where humor is purely local and not structurally integrated.

## Diagnostic questions for any long humorous work

- What is the text's humor vector? Where are jab lines concentrated, where sparse?
- What are the major strands? Which are central, which peripheral?
- Are there combs (bursts of comic density) and where?
- How does the work balance density and rest?
- Does the ending punch line integrate the strands, or merely conclude the narrative?
