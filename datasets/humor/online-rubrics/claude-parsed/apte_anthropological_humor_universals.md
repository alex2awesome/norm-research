# Apte's Anthropology of Humor

Apte's 1985 monograph is the foundational cross-cultural study of humor. He surveys humor across societies to identify what is universal and what is culturally specific in joke types, joking relationships, and the social functions of laughter.

## Universal features Apte documents

Across all human societies surveyed:

1. **All cultures have humor.** No society lacks the phenomenon.
2. **All cultures have specific occasions where humor is expected** (festivals, rites of passage, certain phases of conversation).
3. **All cultures have specific occasions where humor is forbidden** (mourning, sacred rituals, formal authority interactions).
4. **All cultures have "joking relationships"** — formalized dyads (often kin-based) where mutual joking is socially required.
5. **All cultures have ethnic humor about neighboring groups.**
6. **All cultures have humor about bodily functions** (food, sex, excretion).
7. **All cultures have humor about authority figures** (in some controlled form).
8. **Children's humor develops in a similar sequence** across cultures (rhythm/repetition → incongruity → social/sexual).

## Joking relationships: a cultural-anthropological staple

Apte gives extensive attention to **formalized joking relationships** found across many societies:
- Cross-cousin joking (allowed obscene exchange between certain relatives).
- Brother-in-law / sister-in-law joking.
- Grandparent-grandchild joking.
- Tribal-pair joking (members of paired clans must joke with each other).

These are *required* humor relationships: failure to joke is itself a violation.

## Cultural variation Apte documents

What varies across cultures:

- **Targets considered legitimate** (in some cultures clergy are off-limits, in others they are common targets).
- **Modes of expression** (verbal wit vs. physical comedy weighted differently).
- **Acceptable level of obscenity** (some cultures' "polite" humor includes content others find shocking).
- **Public-private boundary** of humor (when can you joke in front of authorities, elders, opposite sex).
- **Status of humorists** (clowns/jesters may be sacred, profane, despised, or revered depending on culture).

## Apte's critique of universalist humor theories

Apte argues that incongruity, superiority, and relief theories — developed by Western philosophers — partly hold cross-culturally but also reflect Western priorities:
- The role of humor in *correcting* behavior (Bergson) is more salient in some cultures than others.
- The connection of humor to *sexuality and aggression* (Freud) may reflect a particular Western conception of repression.
- The aesthetic *appreciation* of humor as standalone art may be specifically Western.

A culturally-aware humor theory must specify what is structurally universal and what is socially variable.

## Three social functions of humor across cultures

1. **Social solidarity** — humor binds insiders.
2. **Social control** — humor enforces norms by ridiculing transgressors.
3. **Social subversion** — humor allows expression of otherwise forbidden views.

The same instance can serve any of the three depending on speaker, audience, and context.

## Implications for evaluating humor

A humor instance's funniness is not absolute but situated in:

- The audience's cultural background.
- The relationship structure between speaker and target (formal joking partners vs. strangers vs. authority).
- The occasion (license-permitting vs. license-restricting time/place).
- The cultural taxonomies of acceptable targets.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the humor occurring in a culturally license-permitting context?
- Is the speaker-target relationship one in which this kind of humor is allowed?
- Is the target one of the culturally legitimate kinds for this audience?
- Does the humor function for solidarity, control, or subversion in this case?
- What is universal in the joke's structure, and what is culturally specific?
