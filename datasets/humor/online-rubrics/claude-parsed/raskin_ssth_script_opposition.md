# Raskin's SSTH: The Two Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

Raskin (1985) proposed the first formal linguistic theory of joke humor with explicit necessary and sufficient conditions stated in semantic terms.

## What a "script" is

A script is "a large chunk of semantic information surrounding the word or evoked by it." It is a structured cognitive representation of typical situations: events, participants, props, sequences. A script is "the native speaker's knowledge of a small part of the world."
- The DOCTOR script: medical office, white coat, examination, diagnosis, prescription.
- The MARRIAGE script: ceremony, vows, rings, family.
- The DEATH script: corpse, funeral, mourning, burial.

## The two main hypotheses (necessary and sufficient conditions for a humorous text)

A text is a single-joke-carrying text if BOTH conditions hold:

### Condition 1: Script Compatibility
The text must be **fully or in part compatible with two different scripts**. Most of the text is ambiguous — it can be interpreted under either script.

### Condition 2: Script Opposition
The two scripts evoked must be **opposite** in a specific technical sense.

## Three levels of script opposition

Raskin identifies a hierarchy of oppositions, each more specific than the next:

1. **Most abstract:** REAL vs. UNREAL.
2. **Mid-level:** ACTUAL vs. NON-ACTUAL; NORMAL vs. ABNORMAL; POSSIBLE vs. IMPOSSIBLE.
3. **Most concrete (basic oppositions):**
   - GOOD vs. BAD.
   - LIFE vs. DEATH.
   - OBSCENE vs. NON-OBSCENE.
   - MONEY vs. NO MONEY.
   - HIGH STATUS vs. LOW STATUS.

These five basic oppositions, Raskin argues, capture the vast majority of all jokes.

## How a joke unfolds

1. The text is consistent with Script A from the beginning.
2. A *trigger* (often the punchline) reveals that Script B has also been compatible all along.
3. The audience switches from A to B (or holds both simultaneously, which is the joke).
4. The opposition between A and B (e.g., one is the GOOD reading, the other the BAD reading) is what gives the switch its humorous force.

## Necessary conditions in detail

- **Genuine compatibility with both scripts** — not a forced reinterpretation, but real semantic overlap.
- **Genuine opposition** — the two scripts must contradict on one of the recognized opposition axes; mere unrelatedness is not funny.
- **A trigger that licenses the switch** — usually localized in the punchline.
- **The audience must possess both scripts.** A joke fails when the listener doesn't know one of the scripts being invoked.

## What this excludes

- Texts compatible with only one script (no joke).
- Texts compatible with two scripts that are merely *different* but not *opposed* (clever but not funny).
- Texts requiring two scripts but lacking a trigger to license the switch.

## Diagnostic questions

- What two scripts does the text invoke?
- Is the text genuinely compatible with both?
- What is the opposition axis (real/unreal, good/bad, sex/non-sex, etc.)?
- Where is the trigger — what specific element forces the script switch?
- Does the audience plausibly know both scripts?
