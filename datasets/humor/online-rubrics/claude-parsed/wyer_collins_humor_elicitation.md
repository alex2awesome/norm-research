# Wyer & Collins's Comprehension-Elaboration Theory

Wyer and Collins (1992) provide a general information-processing theory specifying *when* humor is elicited from a stimulus, integrating cognitive load, elaboration, and processing goals.

## The three central determinants of humor

Humor experience depends on three factors:

### 1. Reinterpretation (the act of comprehension)
The stimulus must be **reinterpreted** in a way that diminishes the seriousness or importance of the initial concept. The recipient first encodes the situation under one schema, then re-encodes it under a second schema in which the initial concept becomes less consequential.

This *diminishment* condition is critical: a reinterpretation that makes the situation *more* important or grave is not humorous (it is dramatic insight).

### 2. Comprehension difficulty (cognitive demand)
The difficulty of identifying the alternative interpretation matters in a curvilinear way:

- **Too easy** — the joke is obvious, predictable; humor is low.
- **Sweet-spot difficulty** — the audience must work briefly but successfully; humor is maximal.
- **Too hard** — the audience can't find the alternative; humor fails (puzzlement).

### 3. Elaboration (cognitive elaboration of implications)
After the punchline is grasped, audiences elaborate by considering implications, connections, and downstream meanings. The more elaborable the punchline, the more sustained the humor.
- Jokes whose implications keep unfolding (you keep laughing as more facets appear) score high.
- Jokes that resolve to a single thin point score lower.

## The role of processing objectives

Crucially, the influence of these three factors depends on the recipient's **information-processing goal** at the moment:

- **Comprehension-set:** the recipient is trying to understand what is being said. Humor follows from successful reinterpretation.
- **Memory-set:** the recipient is trying to remember the content. Humor effects are different.
- **Communication-set:** the recipient is in active social exchange. Humor is heightened by social cues.

This explains why the same joke lands differently in different contexts.

## What is excluded

- Reinterpretations that increase rather than diminish significance.
- Stimuli where no alternative interpretation can be found.
- Stimuli processed under a strict goal where humor frame is unavailable (e.g., during serious analysis).

## Special cases the theory handles

- **Ethnic humor** — relies on stereotyped expectations and a target whose features are reinterpreted as diminishing.
- **Self-deprecating humor in social situations** — the speaker reinterprets their own behavior in a diminishing frame for audience benefit.
- **Unintended humor** — observers reinterpret a serious event under a diminishing frame the original actor did not intend.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the recipient available to reinterpret (right processing set)?
- Does the reinterpretation diminish the importance of the initial concept?
- Is comprehension difficulty in the sweet spot — neither trivial nor impossible?
- Does the punchline support continued elaboration of implications?
