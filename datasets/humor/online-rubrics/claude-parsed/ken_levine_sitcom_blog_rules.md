# Ken Levine's Sitcom Writing Wisdom

Ken Levine wrote for CHEERS, MASH, FRASIER, etc.

## Rule of 2's
On CHEERS, the writers' room rule:
- If any two writers pitched essentially the same punchline, they discarded it — even if funny.
- Reasoning: if two writers came up with the same joke, audience members likely will too.
- Predictable jokes don't land as hard.

## Two Questions for Every Joke
For every joke pitched, Levine asks:
1. Is it funny?
2. What would be funnier?
- Don't pat yourself on the back after the first test.
- Always push to make it funnier.

## "Like a Joke"
- A "Like a joke" has the rhythms of a joke but is not funny.
- Just a straight sentence delivered as a joke line.
- The cadence/structure tricks people, but there's no actual punch.
- Ruthless writers cut these.

## Sitcom Writing Terms
- **Beats**: events that occur in a scene.
- **Savers**: damage control jokes right after your real joke pitch dies.
- **Button**: final joke of a scene.
- **Swinging in on a rope**: side character enters, delivers a joke, leaves (used in CHEERS).
- **Blow**: the strongest joke that ends a scene.
- **Topper**: a tag that beats the previous joke.
- **Pitch**: an idea suggested in the room.

## Beating Jokes
Constantly try to "beat" (replace with funnier version) every joke before lock-in.

## Rubric (Levine Method)
1. Has the joke been tested against the "what would be funnier" question?
2. Could two writers easily pitch this — i.e., predictable?
3. Is it a real joke or a "like-a joke"?
4. Does the scene have a strong button?
5. Are there toppers?
