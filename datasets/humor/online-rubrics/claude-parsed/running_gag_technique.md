# Running Gag (Running Joke)

## Definition
A literary device: an amusing joke or comical reference that appears repeatedly throughout a work. Humor derives from repetition, ideally becoming funnier each time. Must repeat at least three times (less = Brick Joke or Chekhov's Gag).

## Sources of Humor
1. **Repetition itself** — the more it appears, the funnier (Pavlovian conditioning).
2. **Inappropriate context** — gag occurs where it really shouldn't.
3. **Bait and switch** — set up another occurrence, substitute something else.

## Verbal vs. Visual
Running gags can be:
- Verbal: a recurring phrase, joke, callback.
- Visual: a recurring image/sight gag.

## Effective Running Gags Need
- **Timing**: when does it return?
- **Repetition with variation**: not identical each time.
- **Setup**: audience must anticipate / recognize.
- **Escalation**: each occurrence raises stakes.
- **Final twist**: last appearance often subverts the pattern.

## Examples
- "And don't call me Shirley" (*Airplane!*)
- The Black Knight in *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* ("Tis but a scratch")
- "The Treehouse of Horror" episodes of *The Simpsons* (Halloween).
- Huck Finn's lies and Tom Sawyer's elaborate "rescue" schemes.
- A car joke recurring throughout a comedian's set.

## Why It Works
- Audience pattern recognition rewarded.
- Inside-joke effect (community feeling).
- Each occurrence is faster (no setup needed).
- Anticipation creates pre-laughter.

## Rubric
1. Does it appear at least three times?
2. Does each appearance vary slightly?
3. Does it escalate?
4. Is there a final subversion?
5. Does the audience anticipate it before it lands?
