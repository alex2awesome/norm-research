# Norrick on Joking in Everyday Conversation

Norrick's 1993 monograph is the foundational study of how humor actually operates in real conversation, as opposed to how isolated jokes are theorized. The unit of analysis is the joking *turn* in talk, not the joke text in isolation.

## Forms of conversational humor catalogued

### 1. Canned jokes
Pre-formed, stand-alone joke texts inserted into the conversation. Marked entry: "Did you hear the one about..." or "That reminds me of a joke."
- Conversation pauses for the joke.
- Joke completes; conversation resumes.
- Often function as conversational interludes rather than substantive contributions.

### 2. Personal anecdotes
Narratives of personally experienced events told for comic effect.
- Build conversational currency (the speaker presents themselves through the story).
- Funnier when artfully shaped (selection of detail, pacing).
- Truth claim is implicit: "this actually happened."

### 3. Spontaneous wordplay
Wit that exploits something just said in the conversation:
- Puns built on a previous turn's words.
- Re-construals of the literal/figurative.
- Allusion-triggers.
- Demonstrates quick uptake; closely tied to local context.

### 4. Irony and sarcasm
Saying the opposite of what is meant, often with tonal cues.
- Requires shared interpretive ground.
- Can be friendly or hostile depending on relationship and target.

### 5. Mutual teasing / banter
Reciprocal mock-aggression between participants.
- Marks closeness (only friends can banter without offense).
- Has its own conversational rhythm — turns get returned in kind.

## The structural-functional principles Norrick identifies

### Repetition as structural device
Joking turns frequently use *repetition*:
- A word or phrase from a prior turn returns with new framing.
- A whole structural pattern recurs (escalating list, rule-of-three).
- Repetition signals "this is a joke" by deliberately patterning what would otherwise be ordinary speech.

### Single-stage vs. multi-stage processing
- **Single-stage**: the comic recognition is immediate (a sharp wit).
- **Multi-stage**: the joke unfolds over multiple turns (a story building to a punch, an extended absurdity).
- Conversational humor mixes both — and the rhythm of single-stage jabs alongside multi-stage developments is part of skilled humor production.

### Timing in word play vs. narrative jokes
- Word play depends on tight timing — the comic word must arrive close to the trigger.
- Narrative jokes can extend longer but require pacing — too slow loses the audience, too fast collapses the buildup.

## Two functional axes Norrick highlights

### Aggression vs. rapport
Conversational humor can serve either:
- **Aggression** — putting down a target, asserting dominance, marking enemies.
- **Rapport** — building intimacy, signaling alignment, demonstrating shared sensibility.

The same surface joke can operate on either axis depending on relationship, tone, and uptake.

## Listener cues and uptake

Norrick emphasizes that conversational humor is not complete until the listener responds:
- A laugh.
- A returned witticism.
- An echoed phrase.
- A nod or smile.

If the listener fails to uptake (silence, deflection, return to seriousness), the humor was ineffective regardless of textual quality.

## Necessary conditions for successful conversational humor

1. **Recognized play frame** — both speaker and hearer are oriented to the comic mode.
2. **Local relevance** — the joke arises out of, and refers back to, the immediate conversation.
3. **Appropriate pacing** — timing matches the joke's structural type.
4. **Listener uptake** — the response completes the comic move.
5. **Functional fit** — the humor's aggressive/rapport function is appropriate to the relationship.

## What this excludes from analytic adequacy

Theories that look only at joke text in isolation cannot explain:
- Why the same joke is funny in one conversation and dud in another.
- Why "you had to be there" jokes work for participants but not retellers.
- Why timing and uptake matter as much as content.

## Diagnostic questions

- What conversational form is the humor (canned joke, anecdote, wordplay, irony, banter)?
- Is the comic move single-stage or multi-stage?
- Is the joke locally relevant — arising from the conversation?
- Does the listener uptake it?
- Is the function aggression or rapport — and is that function appropriate to the relationship?
