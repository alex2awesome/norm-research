# Bergson's Three Laws of Comic Construction

Bergson's central image is "something mechanical encrusted on the living" (du mécanique plaqué sur du vivant). From this image he derives three formal laws governing all comic construction.

## Why the mechanical is funny

Life is essentially **elastic, attentive, supple, and self-adjusting**. Whenever a person displays the opposite — rigidity, automatism, distraction, mechanical repetition where flexibility was needed — they become comic. We laugh as a *social corrective*: laughter is the punishment society inflicts on those who fail to be attentively alive.

## Three preconditions for the comic in general

Bergson lists three social-psychological preconditions:
1. **Insensibility (anesthésie momentanée du cœur)** — the audience must temporarily suspend sympathy. Comedy requires the heart to be still.
2. **Public/social setting** — laughter is essentially a group phenomenon; we laugh to rejoin or align with others.
3. **A human reference** — even comic landscapes or animals are funny because they evoke something human (a man-shape in a tree, a dog acting like its owner).

## The three formal laws

### Law 1: Repetition

A character, situation, or phrase repeats when life would have varied. The repeated element becomes increasingly mechanical-seeming each time.
- Verbal repetition: a catchphrase recurring in absurd contexts.
- Situation repetition: the same scene replays with different characters.
- Gesture repetition: a tic or mannerism returns at incongruous moments.

The funny element is that life is *supposed* to be unrepeatable — when it repeats, it has become a thing.

### Law 2: Inversion

A situation flips into its mirror image. Roles reverse.
- The thief robbed.
- The judge in the dock.
- The teacher being lectured by the pupil.
- The doctor falling ill from his own prescription.

The reversal exposes the rigidity of the original arrangement by showing it operating in reverse.

### Law 3: Reciprocal Interference of Series

A situation belongs simultaneously to two independent series of events, each consistent in itself, and is capable of two entirely different interpretations.
- Misunderstandings (X interprets it under series A, Y under series B; both keep talking past each other).
- Vaudeville plots where the wrong door is opened and two unrelated stories collide.
- The pun, in which a single utterance belongs to two different chains of meaning.

## Bergson's catalogue of types of comic

- **Comic of forms** (a face, a costume, a posture that has become rigid).
- **Comic of movement** (an awkward, mechanical, repetitive bodily action).
- **Comic of situation** (the three laws applied to plot).
- **Comic of words** (the three laws applied to language).
- **Comic of character** (the three laws applied to personality — vanity, miserliness, distraction).

## What is excluded by Bergson

- Anything that arouses pity or affection — sympathy kills laughter.
- The genuinely flexible, the truly attentive person.
- Pure spectacle disconnected from human reference.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is there rigidity, automatism, or absent-mindedness in a place where life called for adaptive response?
- Does one of the three laws (repetition, inversion, reciprocal interference) structure the comic event?
- Is sympathy temporarily suspended?
- Does the laughter function as social correction — making the rigid person realize they were being mechanical?
