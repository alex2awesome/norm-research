# New Yorker Caption Contest: Strategy Analysis (synthesis from winners' blogs)

Synthesis from caption contest winners' blogs (Lawrence Wood, Roy Futterman, Larry Wood, etc.) on what wins.

## The Cartoon-Caption Equation
A winning caption must:
1. Be unmistakably anchored to the cartoon image.
2. Identify the SECOND most obvious interpretation (not first or third).
3. Use the smallest number of words.
4. Land on the funniest word at the end.
5. Be tonally consistent with the New Yorker's voice.

## On the "Second-Best" Strategy
- The first interpretation is what most submitters will write.
- The third interpretation is too obscure for editors.
- The second-best is the sweet spot: slightly unexpected, clearly readable.

## On Voice
- The New Yorker voice is mildly weary, observational, slightly literate.
- A caption written in TV-sitcom voice will be cut.
- A caption written in extreme literary voice will be cut.

## On Word Count
- Most winning captions: 4-12 words.
- Each unnecessary word is a deduction.
- "I think we should..." is dead weight; cut to "We should..."

## On Cartoon Type
- Animals doing human things: most-submitted, hardest to win.
- Two figures in office: medium difficulty.
- Surreal scenes (alien landscapes, anthropomorphized objects): harder, higher win odds for clever captions.

## Submission-Strategy Patterns from Winners
- Submit weekly. Volume matters.
- Read finalists historically; absorb the editorial taste.
- Don't pun unless the pun is essential.
- Avoid the obvious caption everyone submits.
- Imagine the LEAST funny person you know writing a caption; don't write theirs.

## Caption Contest Working Rubric
1. Does the caption REQUIRE the image to make sense?
2. Did you choose the "second-best" interpretation?
3. Is the word count under 12?
4. Is the funniest word last?
5. Is the voice mildly weary, observational?
6. Have you submitted weekly to absorb editor taste?
