# Yus on Humor as Relevance-Theoretic Manipulation

Yus extends Sperber & Wilson's Relevance Theory to humor: jokes work because the speaker manipulates the *inferential strategies* hearers normally use to find optimally relevant interpretations. The same strategies the brain uses for serious communication are weaponized for comic effect.

## The relevance-theoretic premise

In ordinary communication, the hearer:
1. Makes hypotheses about the speaker's intended meaning.
2. Selects the **most accessible** interpretation that yields enough cognitive effects for the processing effort.
3. Stops when an optimally relevant interpretation is found.

The speaker, knowing this, can engineer the interpretive path so it leads predictably to a wrong-but-natural reading, then a punch reveals the right reading.

## The garden-path joke structure

Most verbal jokes exploit the standard hearer strategy:

1. **Setup primes high-accessibility interpretation A.** A is what the hearer most naturally reaches.
2. **Hearer commits to A** and proceeds to derive cognitive effects from it.
3. **Punchline is incompatible with A.** The hearer must abandon A.
4. **Punchline forces selection of low-accessibility interpretation B**, which the setup also supported but the hearer didn't reach.
5. **Re-interpretation under B yields the joke effect.**

The hearer realizes the speaker *knew* A would be reached and built the joke to exploit it.

## Beyond simple incongruity-resolution: cognitive overload

Yus argues that for many jokes, what produces laughter is not the resolution of a single incongruity but a **cognitive overload of weakly communicated assumptions**. The punchline implies many things simultaneously — about the characters, the situation, social norms, stereotypes — and the proliferation of weakly held implicatures is itself the source of comic pleasure.

This explains:
- Why "deep" jokes (with many resonances) feel funnier than thin jokes.
- Why translated jokes often lose force (assumptions don't carry).
- Why insider jokes are extra funny to insiders (more accessible weak assumptions).

## Yus's typology of jokes by interpretive structure

### Type 1: Single-covert interpretation
- One interpretation A is salient throughout.
- Punchline reveals interpretation B that was always available but covert.
- Hearer realizes B fits all along.

### Type 2: Multi-graded interpretations
- Several interpretations are simultaneously available, ranked by accessibility.
- Punchline reveals that a low-ranked interpretation is the intended one.
- Pleasure from being made to traverse the ranking.

### Type 3: Weakly-implicated propositions
- Punchline doesn't have a single intended interpretation but a halo of weak implications.
- Hearer enjoys the spread of meanings.
- Ironic and rich literary humor often takes this form.

## Necessary conditions for relevance-theoretic humor

1. **The setup makes interpretation A maximally accessible** without requiring it.
2. **Interpretation B is genuinely supported by the linguistic content** but is less accessible.
3. **The punchline blocks A and forces B**, or the punchline triggers a cloud of weak assumptions.
4. **The hearer recognizes the speaker's manipulation** — the joke depends on attributing intentional design to the speaker.

## Why the speaker's intent matters

A spontaneous coincidence is not funny in the same way an intentional joke is. Relevance-theoretic humor requires the hearer to model that the speaker *deliberately constructed* the misleading path — that's what makes the punchline a victory of speaker over hearer's ordinary processing.

## Diagnostic questions

- What is the most accessible interpretation A from the setup?
- What is the alternative interpretation B that the punchline forces?
- Is the punchline blocking A and forcing B, or is it spreading weak assumptions?
- Does the hearer model the speaker's design (intentional manipulation)?
- Are there many weak implicatures supporting "depth," or just one twist?
