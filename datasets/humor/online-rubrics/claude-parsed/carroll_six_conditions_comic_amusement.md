# Carroll's Six Necessary Conditions for Comic Amusement

Noël Carroll, working in the analytic philosophy tradition, refines the incongruity theory by specifying six jointly necessary conditions for the mental state of *comic amusement*.

## The six conditions

A subject S is in a state of comic amusement at object O if and only if:

### 1. Object is a perceived incongruity
S regards O as **incongruous** — as departing from some norm, expectation, schema, or pattern.

### 2. Non-threatening
S regards O as **not threatening** to S's safety, well-being, or interests. If O is felt as a real threat, fear or anxiety crowds out amusement.

### 3. Not anxiety-producing
Beyond physical threat, S is not anxious about O. Anxiety in any form (social, moral, existential) blocks comic amusement.

### 4. Not annoying / not vexing
S does not find O irritating, frustrating, or distasteful in a way that produces negative affect competing with amusement.

### 5. No genuine problem-solving attitude is enlisted
S is not engaged in trying to *seriously* resolve, fix, or address the incongruity. The mind plays with it rather than working on it.

### 6. Enjoyment is taken in the pertinent incongruity, with experience of levity
S **enjoys** the incongruity itself — and the enjoyment has the phenomenal quality of *levity* (lightness, buoyancy), not weighty insight.

## Why all six are jointly necessary

- Without (1): there is nothing to be amused at — only mere experience.
- Without (2): the incongruity becomes scary, not funny.
- Without (3): the incongruity becomes worrying, not funny.
- Without (4): the incongruity becomes irritating, not funny.
- Without (5): the incongruity is being seriously addressed, not enjoyed.
- Without (6): the cognitive shift may produce admiration, awe, or insight — not amusement.

## The "pertinent incongruity" requirement

Not just any incongruity counts. The incongruity must be:
- **Pertinent** — it must concern features that are central to one's understanding of the object, not arbitrary peripheral oddities.
- **Held as the focus of the enjoyment** — S takes pleasure *in this very mismatch*, not in something tangential.

This rules out cases where one is "amused" by something but the supposed incongruity is irrelevant to what is actually amusing.

## The community-construction function

Carroll emphasizes that comic amusement plays a **constructive social role**. By laughing together, audiences:
- Affirm shared norms (the incongruity is recognized only against shared expectations).
- Build cohesion (we are the people who find this funny).
- Demarcate communities (those who don't find it funny mark themselves as outside).

A successful joke therefore depends on shared background norms and shared aesthetic sensibility.

## Carroll's position on humor and ethics

Carroll defends *moderate comic moralism*:
- Some moral defects in a joke can reduce its funniness (e.g., when the violated moral is one the audience strongly holds).
- But moral defects do not always reduce funniness — context, audience, and performance can buffer them.
- Audience composition is crucial: jokes that work in-group can fail out-group precisely because the violated norm matters more outside.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is there a perceivable incongruity?
- Is the audience free of threat, anxiety, vexation, and serious problem-solving toward it?
- Is the enjoyment focused on the *pertinent* incongruity?
- Does the experience have the quality of levity?
- Is the relevant background norm shared by the audience?
