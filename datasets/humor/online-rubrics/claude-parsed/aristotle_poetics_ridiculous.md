# Aristotle — The Ridiculous (Poetics)

## Definition of the Ridiculous
> "The Ridiculous may be defined as a mistake or deformity not productive of pain or harm to others; the mask, for instance, that excites laughter, is something ugly and distorted without causing pain."

## Earliest Statement of Benign Violation Theory
- "Ugly and distorted" = the violation.
- "Without causing pain" = the benign condition.
- 2,300+ years before McGraw & Warren.

## Comedy vs. Tragedy
- **Comedy** imitates men "worse than the average."
- **Tragedy** imitates men "better than they are."
- Comic characters are not necessarily evil — just ridiculous and laughable.

## The Ludicrous as Subdivision of the Ugly
> "The Ludicrous being merely a subdivision of the ugly. It consists in some defect or ugliness which is not painful or destructive."

## The Lost Second Book
- *Poetics* originally divided into two books.
- First book (surviving): tragedy and epic.
- Second book (lost): comedy.
- Centuries of speculation about its contents.
- Inspired Eco's *Name of the Rose*.

## Implications

### For "What Makes a Joke Funny"
1. Some defect or ugliness must be present.
2. The defect must not cause pain or harm.
3. Audience must perceive both at once.

### Aristotle's Comic Hierarchy
- Lower characters than us (we feel superior).
- But not so low we feel pity.
- Distortion that's safe to laugh at.

## Connection to Modern Theory
Aristotle's "mistake or deformity not productive of pain" maps directly onto:
- McGraw & Warren's Benign Violation Theory.
- Hobbes's "sudden glory."
- Bergson's "mechanical inelasticity."
- Suls's incongruity-resolution.

## Rubric (Aristotelian)
1. Is there a defect or distortion?
2. Is it not painful or harmful?
3. Are the characters somewhat below us in nobility?
4. Is the audience invited to feel superior, not pitying?
5. Is the ugliness aesthetic, not real?
