# Plato on the Ridiculous (Philebus 48-50)

In the Philebus, Plato develops the earliest sustained philosophical analysis of laughter. He treats comic enjoyment as a *mixed* state in which malice and pleasure are intertwined.

## Core criteria for what is ridiculous

A subject is genuinely ridiculous when ALL of the following obtain:

- **Self-ignorance (agnoia heautou)** — the target imagines themselves to be wealthier, more beautiful, or more virtuous than they really are. Without this delusion of self, the target is not ridiculous, only pitiable.
- **Powerlessness of the target** — the self-deceived person must be too weak to retaliate. A self-deceived tyrant is feared, not laughed at. Only a *harmless* fool elicits comic pleasure.
- **A third-party spectator** — the laughter occurs from outside, comparing the target's self-image to reality.

## The mixed-pleasure structure

- **Pleasure** comes from perceiving the target's self-ignorance.
- **Pain (envy/malice)** is mixed in because we delight at another's misfortune (epichairekakia / Schadenfreude).
- The comic feeling is therefore *morally suspect*: pure laughter is impossible because it requires malicious enjoyment.

## What does NOT count as ridiculous

- A person who knows their own ignorance and admits it (Socratic figure).
- A self-deceived person who is dangerous or strong.
- Misfortune that does not involve self-deception (this is purely tragic).

## Audience-side conditions

- The spectator must feel themselves to be *not* in the same condition as the target (a comparative judgment).
- The spectator must be willing to entertain a slightly malicious feeling.

## Republic warnings

In Republic III (388e) and X, Plato adds that violent laughter destabilizes the soul, undermines the dignity of guardians, and should be excluded from the education of rulers. Comic poets should be tightly controlled in the ideal state.

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the joke target someone who falsely overestimates themselves?
- Is the target effectively powerless?
- Does the laugh require us to enjoy something morally bad in the target's situation?
- Would the joke survive if the target became aware of their own flaw?
