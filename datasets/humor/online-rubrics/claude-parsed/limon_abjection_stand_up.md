# John Limon — Stand-up Comedy as Abjection

## Central Thesis
Stand-up is best defined by its fascination with the **abject** — Julia Kristeva's term for those aspects of oneself that are obnoxious to one's sense of identity but impossible to jettison once and for all.

## What Is the Abject?
- Blood, feces, urine, vomit.
- Bodily processes we'd rather disown.
- Shame, embarrassment, awkwardness.
- The parts of self we deny but can't escape.
- The borders of "I" and "not-I."

## Stand-Up's Relation to Abjection
- The comedian gives voice to what shouldn't be said.
- Brings the disowned to the stage.
- Audience laughs because the laugh expels the abject.
- Communal exorcism.

## Historical Trajectory
- 1950s/60s: Jewish, male, heterosexual norm (Bruce, Reiner, Brooks, Nichols, May).
- Toward present: Black, female, queer, Protestant comedians "repossess" what was abjected.
- "Comedification" of America = expansion of who gets to abject what.

## Application to Humor Quality
- Funny stand-up forces the audience to confront something abject.
- Bad stand-up evades the abject (sanitized, safe).
- Greatness = navigating closer to taboo without crossing into sheer disgust.

## Rubric (Limon Lens)
1. Does the joke confront something abject?
2. Does the comedian expose something usually disowned?
3. Is the audience implicated in the abjection?
4. Is the joke close enough to taboo to feel real?
5. Does the laughter expel rather than celebrate the abject?
