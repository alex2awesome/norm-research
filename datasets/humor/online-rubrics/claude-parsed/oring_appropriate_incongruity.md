# Oring's Appropriate Incongruity

Folklorist Elliott Oring refines incongruity theory by adding a critical qualifier: not all incongruities are funny. The ones that are funny are those exhibiting what he calls **appropriate incongruity** — perceived connections between categories that *should* be incongruous but are nevertheless seen as fitting.

## The defining formula

> Humor arises from the **perception of an appropriate incongruity** — that is, an incongruity that, when perceived, is recognized as in some sense fitting, apt, or meaningful.

## Why "appropriate" is needed

Pure incongruity is too broad: it covers surprise, mystery, error, and shock. Oring argues that what distinguishes humor specifically is that the incongruity, once perceived, has a kind of **rightness** to it — the audience says "yes, that *does* fit, in a strange way."

The recognition has both:
- The **incongruity moment** ("these don't go together").
- The **appropriateness moment** ("but they do, in this clever sense").

The combination — held simultaneously — produces the comic experience.

## Necessary conditions

1. **A perceived incongruity** — categories, expectations, frames in conflict.
2. **A perceived appropriateness** — a sense that the conflicting elements actually do connect on some non-obvious dimension.
3. **The connection is non-trivial** — too obvious a fit produces no comic spike; the appropriateness must be *just barely* discoverable.
4. **The perceiver enjoys the play** of the incongruity-appropriateness pair, not merely solves it.

## Examples illustrating appropriateness

- A pun joins two senses of a word: incongruous (different meanings), appropriate (same word).
- A successful metaphor joins two domains: incongruous (categorically different), appropriate (some hidden similarity).
- A clever caricature exaggerates a feature: incongruous (distorted face), appropriate (we recognize the person all the more for the distortion).

## What pure incongruity (without appropriateness) produces instead

- **Bewilderment** — an incongruity with no perceptible connection.
- **Surprise** — an incongruity that resolves into a different ordinary frame.
- **Horror** — an incongruity that connects via a threatening dimension.
- **Mystery** — an incongruity that may yet have appropriateness, but it has not been perceived.

## What pure appropriateness (without incongruity) produces

- **Aesthetic recognition** — pleasure of well-fitting elements (no incongruity, no humor).
- **Insight / understanding** — the connection between distant ideas (without the comic edge).

## Oring's larger frame: humor as cultural commentary

In Engaging Humor (2003), Oring shows how appropriate incongruity functions in:

- **Ethnic humor** — the appropriate fit of stereotype to behavior (which is what makes such humor sociologically charged).
- **Jewish humor** — the joining of sacred and profane categories.
- **Frontier humor** — the appropriate union of high pretension and low reality.
- **Internet humor** — the sudden appropriate fits across genres and contexts memes enable.

## What this analysis demands of a humor instance

For a candidate humor instance, identify:

1. The categories or frames that are incongruous.
2. The dimension along which they are nevertheless appropriate.
3. Whether the appropriateness is non-trivial and discoverable.
4. Whether the perceiver enjoys holding both simultaneously.

## Diagnostic questions

- What incongruity is being perceived?
- What is the appropriateness — the dimension on which the incongruous elements nevertheless connect?
- Is the appropriateness non-trivial (not too easy, not impossible)?
- Does the audience enjoy the held tension, or does it resolve into mere understanding?
