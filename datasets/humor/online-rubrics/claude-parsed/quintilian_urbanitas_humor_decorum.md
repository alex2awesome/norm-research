# Quintilian on Humor as Rhetorical Skill

Quintilian inherits Cicero's framework but introduces decorum, urbanitas, and a tripartite ranking of wit. He devotes an entire chapter (VI.3) to forensic and conversational humor.

## The three sources of laughter (VI.3.22)

Laughter is derived from one of three places:
1. **From the physical appearance of one's opponent** (face, gesture, dress).
2. **From his character as revealed in his words and actions.**
3. **From external sources** (circumstances, accident, surroundings).

All forms of raillery reduce to one of these heads.

## The categorization of sayings (VI.3.20)

Sayings (dicta) are classified as:
- **Severa (serious)**
- **Hilara (humorous)**
- **Media (intermediate)** — the ones blending both registers, often the most useful for the orator.

## Urbanitas — the central concept

Urbanitas denotes "language with a smack of the city in its words, accent and idiom" plus a "tincture of learning derived from associating with well-educated men." It is:
- The opposite of rusticitas (provincial coarseness).
- The mark of one who can speak "with humour and appropriateness" in any setting.
- Always governed by *aptum* (the appropriate) — the right joke to the right audience at the right moment.

## What separates the orator's wit from the buffoon's

- **The orator never sacrifices dignity** for a laugh.
- **No joke aimed at deep misfortune** — pity defeats laughter.
- **No joke aimed at deep crime** — indignation defeats laughter.
- **No joke that the audience feels is cruel.**
- **No joke that requires the orator himself to look ridiculous** unintentionally.

## The danger zones (VI.3.28-35)

Quintilian warns that wit can ruin a case if directed at:
- One's own client.
- Powerful judges or persons present.
- Things sacred or universally revered.
- Misfortune the audience pities.

## Specific techniques Quintilian catalogs

- **Saying something contrary to expectation** (Cicero's inopinatum, refined).
- **Reproof under the guise of praise; praise under the guise of reproof.**
- **Pretending not to understand** (dissimulatio).
- **Quoting an opponent's own words back at him in altered context.**
- **Proverbs applied to new situations.**
- **Ambiguity, but used sparingly and with judgment.**
- **Comparison (mocking comparison to a worse exemplar).**
- **Etymological play.**
- **Self-deprecation when it shows confidence rather than weakness.**

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the joke arise from one of the three sources (appearance, character, external)?
- Is it apt to audience, occasion, and speaker's standing?
- Does dignity survive? Does the orator look like a wit, not a buffoon?
- Does the joke risk evoking pity or indignation that would defeat it?
