# Bain on the Ludicrous as Degradation

Bain's account, developed in The Emotions and the Will, refines the superiority/relief tradition by specifying that the comic depends on the *degradation of something dignified or important*.

## The defining formula

> "The occasion of the ludicrous is the degradation of some person or interest possessing dignity, in circumstances that excite no other strong emotion."

## The two key conditions

### 1. There must be dignity to degrade
The target must possess (or claim to possess) some form of dignity, importance, or seriousness. The downfall of someone already low produces no laughter; only the fall of the high.

Forms of dignity that can be degraded:
- Personal dignity (status, manner, self-presentation).
- Institutional dignity (church, government, profession).
- Subject-matter dignity (sacred topics, weighty themes).
- Logical dignity (the form of correct argument).
- Aesthetic dignity (the elevated style).

### 2. The degradation must occur "in circumstances that excite no other strong emotion"
This is Bain's safety condition. The fall must not also produce:
- Pity (if the target's suffering is real and great).
- Indignation (if the target is unjustly treated).
- Fear (if the fall threatens the audience).
- Disgust (if the fall is repugnant beyond comic).
- Sorrow (if the fall causes genuine grief).

When any of these emotions arise, they swamp the comic effect.

## Why degradation is the key

Bain identifies the comic as essentially a *contrast between heights and depths*. The audience perceives the dignified frame, then perceives the lowered actuality. The greater the height fallen from, the greater the comic force, *provided* the safety condition holds.

## What is excluded

- Degradation that wounds (excites pity → not comic).
- Degradation that outrages justice (excites indignation → not comic).
- Mere lowness without prior height (no contrast → flat).
- Mere height without fall (sublime, not comic).

## The connection to superiority and relief

Bain bridges Hobbes (superiority) and Spencer (relief):
- The comic *is* a kind of superiority feeling, but specifically over the dignified-fallen.
- The comic *is* a release of mobilized respect, but only when it has been mobilized against something supposedly important and is then deflated.

## Examples illustrating the principle

- A bishop slips on a banana peel — comic (high dignity, harmless fall).
- A child slips on a banana peel and is hurt — not comic (no dignity to lose, real pain).
- A pretentious speaker mispronounces a foreign word — comic (claimed dignity deflated).
- A humble person mispronounces — much less comic (no dignity claim to puncture).
- A sacred text quoted in a degrading context — comic (high dignity), unless audience is too pious (indignation).

## Bain's procedural test

For any candidate humor instance:
1. Identify what dignity / importance is in play.
2. Identify the degrading event.
3. Check: do the circumstances excite pity, indignation, fear, disgust, or grief?
4. If yes → not comic; if no → comic in proportion to the height fallen from.

## Diagnostic questions

- What dignity (personal, institutional, intellectual, aesthetic) is being degraded?
- How great is the height being fallen from?
- Do the circumstances admit any other strong emotion (pity, anger, fear, disgust)?
- Is the audience's relation to the dignity such that they can enjoy its degradation rather than mourn it?
