# Roz Chast — New Yorker Cartoonist's Process

## Core Aesthetic
Storyteller hunting for the "sweet spot where the ridiculous and the uncomfortable converge." A good joke needs an **emotional aspect**.

## Source Material
Pours all her "anxieties, fears, superstitions, failures, furies, insecurities, and dark imaginings — the kit and caboodle of her psyche" into her work.

## Workflow
- Submits 6–8 cartoons per week to The New Yorker.
- Magazine picks at most one.
- Keeps "rejects" in file cabinets.
- Sometimes redraws and resubmits.

## Visual Hallmarks
- Interior scenes.
- Lamps, accentuated wallpaper.
- Cluttered domestic spaces.
- Hand-lettered, looser drawing style.

## Themes
- Existential dread.
- Neurotic interior monologues.
- Aging parents (*Can't We Talk About Something More Pleasant?*).
- Modern anxieties.
- Catastrophizing.

## Influence
- Charles Addams (whose dark cartoons inspired her).
- "Subversive without being directly confrontational."

## Why Her Work Lands
- Recognition: anxious readers see themselves.
- Specificity: the precise neurotic thought is named.
- Self-mockery: she's first to laugh at her own fears.
- Drawn texture: imperfection signals "real."

## Rubric
1. Is there an anxious emotional core?
2. Does the cartoon name a specific neurosis?
3. Is the visual cluttered/domestic to ground the absurd?
4. Does the reader feel "seen"?
5. Is there subversion without confrontation?
