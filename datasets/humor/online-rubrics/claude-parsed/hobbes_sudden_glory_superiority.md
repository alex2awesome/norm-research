# Hobbes on Laughter as Sudden Glory

Hobbes's account in Leviathan (Part I, ch. 6) is the canonical statement of the **superiority theory** of humor.

## The defining formula

> "Sudden glory is the passion which maketh those grimaces called laughter; and is caused either by some sudden act of their own, that pleaseth them; or by the apprehension of some deformed thing in another, by comparison whereof they suddenly applaud themselves."

## The four necessary components

1. **Glory** — a feeling of eminency, superiority, or self-satisfaction.
2. **Suddenness** — the realization must be abrupt; gradual recognition does not produce laughter.
3. **Comparison** — either to others' infirmities (defects, deformities, follies) or to one's own former, weaker self.
4. **No present dishonor attached to the comparison** — "men laugh at the follies of themselves past, when they come suddenly to remembrance, except they bring with them any present dishonor."

## What Hobbesian humor positively requires

- **A target with a perceptible defect** (intellectual, moral, physical, social).
- **The laugher's awareness of being above the target** in that respect.
- **A spike, not a slow build** — laughter is short and sudden, like a flash.
- **No identification with the target** — the moment one identifies, laughter stops.

## Hobbes's psychological diagnosis of laughers

> "It is incident most to them that are conscious of the fewest abilities in themselves; who are forced to keep themselves in their own favour, by observing the imperfections of other men."

Therefore: heavy laughter at others is, for Hobbes, a sign of *pusillanimity* (small-mindedness). The truly great find their abilities by self-comparison with the best, not by mocking the weak.

## What is excluded by Hobbes

- Laughter at jest where no target is being lowered.
- Laughter that includes self-comparison where one *loses* (this would be shame, not glory).
- Sympathetic or affectionate laughter that does not involve a sense of superiority.

## Hobbes's noble exception

It is laudable to laugh at *absurdities* themselves "abstracted from persons," because no individual is being humiliated.

## Diagnostic questions

- Who or what is being looked down upon by the laugh?
- Is the laugher's sense of eminence sudden and felt as a spike?
- Could the joke be told in a way that does not lower someone — and would it still work?
- Does the laughter say more about the laugher's insecurity than about the target?
