# Cicero's Two-Branch Taxonomy of Jokes

In De Oratore Book II, Cicero puts the first systematic Latin theory of wit into the mouth of Caesar Strabo. Wit (facetiae) is divided into two great branches.

## The fundamental division

1. **Cavillatio (de re / facetiae in re)** — humor of *thing*, situation, or sustained discourse. Pervades a whole speech, character sketch, or anecdote. Cannot be fully extracted in a single line.
2. **Dicacitas (de dicto / facetiae in dicto)** — humor of *word*, the witticism, the sharp short remark. Lives or dies in a single phrase.

## Subtypes of verbal wit (de dicto)

- **Ambiguum** — the pun or double meaning; admired technically but the lowest form ("not so much witty as adroit").
- **Inopinatum** — saying what is contrary to expectation; thwarted expectation makes us laugh.
- **Allegoria / per metaphoram** — extended figurative play.
- **Ironia** — saying the opposite of what one means.
- **Verba immutata** — substituting one word for another with surprising effect.
- **Salty proverb or quotation deployed unexpectedly.**
- **Letter-changes / paronomasia** — slight phonetic alteration that produces a new sense.

## Subtypes of situational wit (de re)

- **Narratio facete** — the well-told humorous anecdote with vivid characterization.
- **Imitatio** — mimicry of voice, manner, or character.
- **Caricature / contortio personae** — exaggeration of a real person's traits.
- **Comparison of unlikes** — yoking the dignified and the trivial.
- **Dissimulatio** — pretending ignorance or innocence to expose absurdity (Socratic irony).
- **Pretended seriousness about trivial things, or trivializing of the serious.**
- **Telling a fable/story aimed at a present situation.**

## Cicero's necessary conditions for good wit

- **Brevitas** — the dictum must be brief; explanation kills the joke.
- **Surprise** — "we expect one thing and another is said; here our own disappointed expectation makes us laugh" (II.284).
- **Pertinence** — the joke must arise out of the matter at hand, not be dragged in.
- **Relevance to character of speaker** — the orator's wit must suit his dignity.

## What an orator should NOT joke about

- Things universally regarded as wretched or criminal (you lose sympathy).
- The deeply pious (you appear impious).
- Persons truly beloved by the audience.
- Trivialities so small that the joke is contemptible.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the wit verbal (lives in word choice) or situational (lives in the matter)?
- Does the joke achieve the violation-of-expectation effect Cicero names?
- Does brevity carry it, or does it require explanation?
- Does the joke fit the speaker's dignity and the occasion?
