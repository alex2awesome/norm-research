# Curcó: Humor as Metarepresentation Play

Curcó (1995, 1997) argues that being humorous is *not* a property of texts. It is a property of the **mental representations the hearer is led to entertain** during processing — and specifically of how those representations are manipulated as metarepresentations of various orders.

## The defining claim

A text is humorous when it leads the hearer to construct **metarepresentations** (representations of representations) — typically of beliefs the hearer attributes to a character, the speaker, or a hypothetical agent — and to perceive an incongruity at the metarepresentational level.

## What is a metarepresentation?

A representation of a representation. Examples:
- "He thinks that p" — my representation of his belief.
- "She believes that he believes that q" — second-order.
- "The character would not know that not-p" — modeling a character's epistemic state.

## How metarepresentations produce humor

Many jokes work by setting up a structure where:

1. The hearer constructs a metarepresentation: "the character believes X."
2. The hearer simultaneously holds the actual situation: "but in fact not-X."
3. The contrast between the metarepresentation (character's belief) and reality is the source of comic effect.

The hearer is thus simultaneously holding two representations — the character's mistaken view and the true state — and enjoying the discrepancy.

## Higher-order cases

- **Irony** — the speaker uses an utterance attributed to someone (or to themselves in another voice) and dissociates from it. The hearer must metarepresent the attributed view and the speaker's distance from it.
- **Sarcasm** — the speaker says X while metarepresenting a critical attitude toward someone who would say or believe X.
- **Misattributed belief comedies** — the audience watches characters operating on false beliefs the audience knows to be false.

## The theory-of-mind connection

Because humor depends on metarepresentations, humor is **a direct consequence of theory of mind**. Without the capacity to attribute beliefs and intentions to others, much humor is inaccessible.

This predicts:
- Children develop humor capacities in step with theory-of-mind milestones.
- Autism, which involves ToM differences, is associated with altered humor profiles (less appreciation of social-deceptive humor, intact appreciation of structural humor).
- Higher-order humor (irony, sarcasm) requires more developed ToM than lower-order humor (slapstick).

## Necessary conditions

1. **At least one metarepresentation is being constructed** by the hearer.
2. **A second representation (or reality) contrasts with it.**
3. **The hearer simultaneously holds both** with sufficient cognitive grip to perceive the contrast.
4. **The hearer enjoys, rather than is distressed by, the contrast.**

## Implications for humor design

A humor designer can engineer the metarepresentational structure:
- Choose a character whose beliefs the audience can clearly model.
- Set up a discrepancy between character belief and audience knowledge.
- Maintain the discrepancy long enough for the audience to feel its weight.
- Trigger the moment of perception — often via dramatic irony, narrator hint, or character action.

## What this excludes

- Stimuli that produce no metarepresentation (purely structural surprises with no agent).
- Stimuli where the audience can't reach the required metarepresentational level (audience's ToM capacity insufficient).
- Discrepancies that cause distress rather than amusement (the contrast feels threatening or sad).

## Diagnostic questions

- What metarepresentation does the joke ask the hearer to construct?
- What is the contrasting representation/reality?
- Is the audience capable of holding both simultaneously?
- Does the hearer enjoy the contrast, or does it produce some other affect?
- What order of metarepresentation (1st, 2nd, 3rd) is required?
