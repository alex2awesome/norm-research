# Oscar Wilde — Epigram and Paradox

## Wilde's Distinctive Style
Built on **paradox** — the apparent contradiction that contains truth. Words were sacred to him; each word perfectly placed; each phrase linked to the next.

## Structural Elements

### Paradox and Contrast
- Setup invokes a familiar form.
- Punch substitutes a paradoxical term.
- Example: "He atones for being occasionally somewhat over-dressed, by being always absolutely over-educated."

### Symmetry
- Parallel grammatical structures.
- Sound symmetry (rhythm, alliteration).

### Inversion
- Subverts a familiar idiom or proverb.
- "I can resist anything except temptation."
- "Work is the curse of the drinking classes."
- "The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it."

### Familiar-Then-Surprising
Epigrams "invoke a response and then frustrate it."
Example from *Importance of Being Earnest*: a widow's "hair has turned quite gold from grief." (Familiar construction, surprising hair color.)

## Epigram vs. Aphorism
- **Epigram**: short, witty, often satirical or humorous.
- **Aphorism**: short, pithy, but not necessarily humorous.
- Wilde's aphorisms are typically also epigrams.

## Famous Wildean Patterns
1. **Definition reversal**: "A cynic is a man who knows the price of everything and the value of nothing."
2. **Recommended vice**: "I can resist everything except temptation."
3. **Snobby observation**: "Anyone who lives within their means suffers from a lack of imagination."
4. **Self-deprecating brag**: "I have nothing to declare except my genius."

## Rubric (Wildean Wit)
1. Is there a paradox or contradiction at the core?
2. Does it subvert a familiar phrase, idiom, or sentiment?
3. Is the language tight, balanced, quotable?
4. Does it imply a worldview or stance?
5. Does it frustrate the reader's first reading and then resolve via wit?
