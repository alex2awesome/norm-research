# Davies on the Universal Stupid/Canny Pattern of Ethnic Humor

Christie Davies's comparative analysis of ethnic jokes from dozens of cultures revealed a striking near-universal pattern. Almost all ethnic humor across the world organizes around two basic comic types: **the stupid** and **the canny**.

## The empirical observation

When Davies catalogued the ethnic targets of jokes globally, he found:

- **Jokes about "stupid" minorities** vastly outnumber any other type.
- **Jokes about "canny" or stingy minorities** are the second most common type.
- Together, stupid + canny jokes account for the majority of all ethnic humor worldwide.
- The same script (e.g., light-bulb jokes) is told about different targets in different countries — the structure is constant; the named target varies.

## Who gets called stupid

The "stupid" target is typically:
- A **rural, peripheral, or lower-status group** in relation to the joke-tellers' urban/central position.
- Often a group geographically *adjacent* but seen as lagging.
- Examples: Polish jokes (US), Belgian jokes (France), Irish jokes (England), East Frisian jokes (Germany), Newfie jokes (Canada).
- The same target gets jokes about *failing at simple tasks*, *not understanding modern technology*, *literal misinterpretation*.

## Who gets called canny

The "canny" target is typically:
- A **traditionally commercial, mercantile, or financial-specialized group**.
- Often a diasporic minority associated with trade.
- Examples: Jewish jokes (Europe/US), Scottish jokes (England), Genoese jokes (Italy), Aberdeen jokes within Scotland.
- The jokes feature *miserliness*, *cleverness in money matters*, *prioritizing material gain over other values*.

## Davies's deflationary insight

Crucially, these jokes are **NOT really about intelligence or greed** — they are about **attitudes toward material gain and modern industrial life**:

- The "stupid" person fails to look after their own material interest.
- The "canny" person is so obsessed with material interest that they sacrifice all other values.

The two stereotypes are mirror-image moral failures defined against an implicit middle: the joke-teller's group, which (claims to) balance gain-seeking with other values appropriately.

## The conditions for the pattern

Davies identifies the social conditions that produce the stupid/canny ethnic joke pattern:

1. **A modernizing/industrializing society** with a working capitalist economy.
2. **An identifiable peripheral group** seen as falling behind modernity.
3. **An identifiable diasporic group** seen as exemplifying market values too fully.
4. **The joke-tellers** placed between, defining themselves implicitly against both extremes.

Where these conditions don't obtain, the pattern doesn't form.

## What the joke-teller is really doing

Davies argues that ethnic stupid/canny jokes are largely **NOT expressions of hostility** toward the named target. They are expressions of the joke-teller's **anxiety about their own position** in an economic order that requires balancing material gain against other values.

This is supported by:
- The same person often tells jokes about *both* stupid and canny groups.
- The targets shift over generations as social conditions change.
- The jokes are popular among people who have positive social relations with the named groups.

## What this excludes

- Genuine hate humor that does not follow the stupid/canny pattern (this functions differently).
- Jokes about majority dominant groups (the structure presupposes targeting peripheral or diasporic minorities).

## Implications for evaluating ethnic humor

To analyze a piece of ethnic humor:

1. Identify whether it follows the stupid pattern, the canny pattern, or neither.
2. Identify the social position of the target relative to the joke-teller.
3. Consider whether the joke is structurally about the joke-teller's anxieties as much as about the target.
4. Distinguish from genuine hostility-driven humor, which has different structural markers.

## Diagnostic questions

- Is the target peripheral/rural (stupid pattern) or diasporic/commercial (canny pattern)?
- Does the joke express failure-of-material-interest or excess-of-material-interest?
- Does it project the joke-teller's anxieties about their own position?
- Is the same script told about different targets in other cultures?
