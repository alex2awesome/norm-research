# Inside Jokes: Using Humor to Reverse-Engineer the Mind

By Matthew Hurley, Daniel Dennett, Reginald Adams Jr. (MIT Press)

## Evolutionary Function of Humor

Humor evolved out of a computational problem: when our ancestors got open-ended thinking, natural selection couldn't directly order the brain to find and fix all our time-pressured mistakes and near-misses. So she had to "bribe the brain with pleasure."

> "We find them funny. This wired-in source of pleasure has been tickled relentlessly by humorists over the centuries, and we have become addicted to the endogenous mind candy that is humor."

## What Makes Humor Funny (in this theory)

A funny event is one in which:
1. An **active belief** is committed to working memory (often covertly).
2. The belief is **discovered to be false** (mistaken).
3. The belief was committed **with no negative emotional valence** — it was a "playful" or "low stakes" mistake.
4. The discovery happens before the belief causes problems.

This is "epistemic mirth" — the pleasure of discovering that a covert belief you just made is wrong.

## Sophisticated Incongruity Theory
- A descendant of incongruity theories.
- Adds: the belief must have been actively, covertly committed.
- The discovery of the bug must be epistemic ("aha, I was wrong") rather than threatening.

## First-Person and Second-Person Humor
- First-person humor: you find your own mistaken belief and laugh.
- Second-person humor: you watch someone else commit and discover the bug (e.g., audience watching a sitcom character).

## Rubric (Inside Jokes Theory)
1. Did the audience commit to a covert belief during the setup?
2. Does the punchline reveal that belief was wrong?
3. Was the wrong belief "low-stakes" enough to be playful?
4. Is the discovery surprising (rather than predictable)?
5. Is there an "aha" moment of epistemic mirth?
