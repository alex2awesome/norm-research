# Veatch's N+V Theory: Three Joint Conditions for Humor

Veatch (1998) offers a tightly specified theory: humor occurs in a subjective state of "affective absurdity" defined by **three jointly necessary and sufficient conditions**.

## The three conditions

### N — Normal
The perceiver's view of the situation **is** simultaneously a normal interpretation: nothing is felt to be wrong from one perspective.

### V — Violation
The perceiver simultaneously holds that some moral or affective principle that they personally are committed to is being **violated** by something in the situation.

### Simultaneity
N and V must be held **at the same time**. If V dissolves, only N remains and the situation is unremarkable. If N dissolves, only V remains and the situation is offensive, frightening, or distressing.

## What "moral attachment" means in V

The violated principle must be one to which the perceiver is *personally affectively committed* — a "subjective moral principle." This is not an abstract ethics; it is the perceiver's lived sense that something *ought to be* a certain way.

- Things one cares about strongly: family, religion, sexual taboos, decency, fairness, dignity.
- Or smaller-scale norms: politeness, conventions of speech, expected logical patterns.

## The attachment-strength prediction

A person's reaction to a violation depends on how *attached* they are to the violated principle:

- **No attachment** → they don't notice the violation; the situation is unremarkable.
- **Slight attachment** → V holds, N also holds, humor occurs.
- **Strong attachment** → V dominates; N collapses; the situation is offensive, threatening, or painful.

This explains individual variation: dark jokes are funny to those slightly attached to the violated norm and offensive to those strongly attached.

## What N+V excludes

- Pure surprise without violation (no V → no humor).
- Pure offense without normal interpretation (no N → no humor).
- Resolution-only experiences where violation has been completely defused (V is gone).

## Comparison to other theories

- Subsumes incongruity theories (the V is structural incongruity, the N is resolution).
- Subsumes superiority (V can be dignity-violation of the target).
- Subsumes relief (N is the safety reframing of an emotionally aroused V).
- Anticipates and largely overlaps with McGraw & Warren's later "benign violation."

## Practical predictions

- The same joke will produce different humor responses across audiences depending on their attachment to the violated principle.
- A teller can engineer N by signaling "this is just a joke," cueing the perceiver to keep one foot in the safe interpretation.
- A joke that worked at one time can stop working when audiences become more attached to the principle being violated (cultural shift in taboos).

## Diagnostic questions

- What principle is being violated?
- Is there simultaneously a normal interpretation that keeps the perceiver from being upset?
- What is the perceiver's attachment to the principle? Calibrated for slight rather than strong attachment?
- Could a small change in audience commitment flip humor into offense?
