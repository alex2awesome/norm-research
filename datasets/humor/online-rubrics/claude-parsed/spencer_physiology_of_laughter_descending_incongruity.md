# Spencer on Laughter as Nervous Energy Discharge

Spencer's 1860 essay is the founding statement of the **relief theory** in modern form, and introduces the crucial concept of *descending incongruity*.

## The hydraulic premise

Spencer treats the nervous system as analogous to a steam boiler: emotional and intellectual activity generates nervous energy that must discharge somewhere. Laughter is one channel of discharge — specifically the channel that opens when energy gathered for a serious purpose is suddenly rendered superfluous.

## The defining formula: descending incongruity

Laughter occurs when **consciousness is unawares transferred from great things to small** — when the mind has been girded for something significant, weighty, or important, and the actual object turns out to be petty, insignificant, or trivial. The energy mobilized for the larger object overflows into bodily movement (laughter).

## Why the direction matters

- **Descending incongruity** (great → small) → laughter.
- **Ascending incongruity** (small → great) → wonder, awe, surprise — but not laughter.
- The asymmetry is critical. A ridiculous thing dressed up as something grand is funny; a grand thing emerging from something ridiculous is sublime.

## Necessary conditions for laughter

1. **Buildup of attention or emotion at one level** (the "great thing" frame).
2. **Sudden revelation that the actual object is at a much lower level.**
3. **No serious affect competing for the discharged energy** (no fear, no love, no concentrated effort).
4. **The path of least resistance** must be the muscles of laughter (face, diaphragm, vocal organs) rather than productive action.

## Spencer's example

A poem opens describing a tender, sympathetic scene; the reader's emotion is mobilized; then the final line reveals it is about a trivial mishap. The mobilized pity has nowhere to go; it discharges as laughter.

## What this excludes

- Laughter at simple delight or mere joy (Spencer treats these as different physiological events).
- Laughter at ascending surprise (these are admiration, not the comic).
- Laughter where the "fall" is into something genuinely painful (the energy is captured by the new pain).

## Spencer's insight that survived

Even after the hydraulic model fell from neuroscience, the **direction-of-incongruity principle** remains a robust observation: descents from the dignified to the petty are reliably comic, while elevations are not. This is empirically visible in nearly every comic structure: pomp punctured, the high made low, the elevated shown to be ordinary.

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the setup mobilize attention/emotion at a high or weighty level?
- Does the punchline drop the object to a much lower level?
- Is the descent (great→small), not the ascent (small→great)?
- Is there mobilized energy with no serious outlet, available to discharge as laughter?
