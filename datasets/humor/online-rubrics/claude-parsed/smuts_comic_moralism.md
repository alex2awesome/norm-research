# Smuts: Why Moral Flaws Reduce, Never Enhance, Amusement

Smuts argues against **comic immoralism** (the view that moral flaws can make jokes funnier) and defends **symmetric comic moralism**: genuine moral flaws never enhance amusement, but sometimes detract; conversely, moral virtues sometimes enhance amusement (in satire), but never detract.

## The structural claim

For any humor instance, the moral status of its content matters in a one-directional way:

- **Moral flaws** in the joke (cruelty, dishonesty, malice toward undeserving targets) → never increase funniness; can decrease it.
- **Moral virtues** displayed by the joke (justice, kindness in target selection, fair treatment, moral courage in satire) → can increase funniness; never decrease it.

There is no symmetric "naughtiness bonus" that immoral content adds.

## Distinguishing apparent flaws from genuine flaws

Critics of moralism point to dark, transgressive jokes that audiences find very funny. Smuts replies:

- Many such jokes are not actually morally flawed — the violation is benign in Smuts's sense (no real victim, target is hypothetical, audience is in protected play frame).
- When jokes have *genuine* moral flaws (real victim being mocked for unjust suffering), audiences with intact moral sensibilities find them less funny than they would otherwise.
- The illusion that immoral jokes are funnier comes from confusing transgression-thrill with the moral-flaw effect.

## The transgression-thrill confound

Smuts identifies a confound: jokes that *break taboos* feel "edgier" and may produce a thrill of forbiddenness. But:

- Taboo-breaking is not the same as moral-flaw status.
- Many taboo-breaking jokes are morally fine (they violate convention but harm no one; targets are powerful, deserving, or hypothetical).
- The thrill comes from convention-breaking, not from moral flaw.

When a taboo-breaking joke is also genuinely morally flawed, audiences *with developed moral sense* still find it less funny than the same joke without the moral flaw — controlling for the transgression element.

## Empirical and analytic support

Smuts argues from cases:

- **A racist joke** about an oppressed group versus the same joke structure with a target who is "stuck-up CEOs" — the latter is funnier to most modern audiences. The moral flaw subtracts.
- **A satirical takedown** of a corrupt politician versus the same joke structure attacking a private citizen for the same behavior — the satirical version is funnier. The moral virtue enhances.

## Carroll's response and Smuts's defense

Noël Carroll defends *moderate* comic moralism — moral flaws can sometimes detract but the relation is context-sensitive. Smuts argues for the stronger *symmetric* claim that moral flaws *systematically* reduce funniness once we control for transgression-thrill and the audience's moral sensibilities.

## What this implies for humor evaluation

When evaluating a joke's funniness, distinguish:

1. **Structural funniness** — the comic mechanism (incongruity, resolution, surprise).
2. **Transgression contribution** — taboo-breaking that adds edginess but is morally innocent.
3. **Moral status** — whether genuine moral flaws are present (real victim, undeserved harm, gratuitous cruelty).

A morally flawed joke is structurally funny *despite* its flaw, not *because* of it. Removing the flaw (e.g., changing the target to one who deserves it) typically yields a *funnier* joke for most audiences.

## Implications for joke design

A humor designer aiming for maximum amusement should:

- Choose targets whose denigration is morally appropriate (powerful, deserving, hypothetical, or self).
- Avoid genuine victims of unjust suffering as targets.
- Use transgression for edginess, but separate transgression from moral wrong.
- Recognize that satire (moral virtue in target selection) enhances funniness, not just admiration.

## Diagnostic questions

- Does the joke have a real victim of unjust suffering as its target?
- If so, is the joke funnier or less funny for it (controlling for transgression-thrill)?
- Could the same comic structure be redirected to a more deserving target? Would that be funnier?
- Is the joke satire (moral virtue) or aggression (moral flaw)?
- Does the audience's moral sensibility increase or decrease the response to this joke?
