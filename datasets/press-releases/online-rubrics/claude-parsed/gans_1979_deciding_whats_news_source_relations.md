# Gans (1979) — source power and "enduring values" in the press release

Gans's ethnography of CBS, NBC, *Newsweek* and *Time* yielded two enduring frames for press-release pickup: (i) source power asymmetries, (ii) eight "enduring values" journalists deploy when assessing newsworthiness.

## Gans on source power (release rubric)
Sources compete to access journalists; access depends on:
1. **Incentives and pressure.** Does the source bring a pre-existing access relationship (regular briefing, embedded correspondent, established beat presence)?
2. **Power of the source.** Officials, large corporations, and major institutions are reported on at much higher rates than non-elite sources of equal substantive newsworthiness.
3. **Ability to supply suitable information.** Releases that match the format the news organization can use win pickup.
4. **Geographic and social proximity.** Sources physically and socially close to the newsroom are picked up more readily; releases optimized for the issuer's local press cluster have higher conversion.
5. **Past performance.** Sources who have proved reliable in the past are favored; releases benefit from issuer reputation, not just current text quality.
6. **Suitability to the news genre.** A release that fits the network's evening-news arc, or the magazine's weekly cycle, is favored over generic copy.

## Gans's eight "enduring values" (apply as release evaluation criteria)
A pickup-worthy release tends to align with at least one of these journalist values:
1. **Ethnocentrism.** Frames events in terms of the audience's national/local self-image.
2. **Altruistic democracy.** Showcases public-spirited behavior or, conversely, public corruption.
3. **Responsible capitalism.** Highlights either responsible business behavior or its failures (this is the value most invoked in business press releases).
4. **Small-town pastoralism.** Connects national stories to community-scale narratives.
5. **Individualism.** Foregrounds individual heroes, victims, or wrongdoers.
6. **Moderatism.** Avoids extremism; pitches the "reasonable" middle.
7. **Social order.** Focuses on threats to and restorations of social order.
8. **National leadership.** Highlights leaders, especially in their official capacities.

## Practical release-rubric criteria distilled from Gans
1. **Value-anchoring lead.** Does the lead invoke one of the eight enduring values explicitly?
2. **Named individual story.** Is there at least one named individual (researcher, patient, executive) whose story personalizes the announcement (individualism)?
3. **Order/disorder framing.** For crisis-related releases, does the language frame the event as either disorder (problem) or restoration (solution) — both newsworthy postures?
4. **Pre-existing relationship signal.** Does the release reference prior accurate dealings with the receiving outlet (e.g., "as we briefed you in [date], the trial has now reported")?
5. **Issuer-rank signal.** Is the issuer's institutional rank made obvious (university name, regulatory authority, market-cap rank)?
6. **Locality-fit option.** Is the release distributable in *both* a national and a locally-customized version (small-town pastoralism)?

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Gans's frame predicts pickup increases sharply when the release simultaneously (a) signals the issuer's institutional power, (b) anchors to one or more enduring values, and (c) supplies a personalized narrative thread for the journalist.
