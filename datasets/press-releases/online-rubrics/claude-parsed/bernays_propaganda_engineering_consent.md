# Bernays — Propaganda (1928) & Engineering of Consent — strategy rubric

Where *Crystallizing Public Opinion* gives the operational principles, *Propaganda* and the later *Engineering of Consent* essay supply the strategic framework that determines whether a release should even exist. Pickup-worthiness here is a function of campaign architecture, not just text.

1. **Engineering posture.** Is the release the product of "thorough knowledge of the situation and the application of scientific principles" — i.e., grounded in research on the receiving audience — rather than a generic announcement?
2. **Group-leader leverage.** Does the release work *through* opinion leaders and intermediary group leaders (medical-society heads, academic deans, civic-association presidents) rather than appealing directly to the mass?
3. **Motive-aligned themes.** Are the themes in the release tied to motives the audience already holds (health, status, security, family) rather than to the issuer's internal motives (revenue, reputation)?
4. **Manufactured organisation of consent.** Has a coalition, council, association, or coordinated event been assembled so the release can announce a *collective* position rather than a single voice?
5. **Symbolic compression.** Are abstract programs reduced to single concrete symbols (a torch, a march, a "first of its kind") that journalists can carry in a headline?
6. **Indirection.** Does the release advance the client's interest by means of an *apparently independent* third action (a survey, a contest, an award, a holiday)?
7. **Continuous campaign frame.** Is this release a planned beat in an ongoing campaign rather than an isolated event? Bernays argues sustained engineering, not one-shot announcements, moves opinion.
8. **Accounting for the "invisible government."** Does the release acknowledge — and try to mobilize — the network of intermediaries (associations, institutes, academic figures) who actually shape what filters through?
9. **Necessity claim.** Bernays insists "conscious and intelligent manipulation of organized habits" is *necessary* in democratic society. Pragmatically: does the release treat the public as competent receivers whose attention must be actively earned, or as passive recipients?
10. **Ethical screen.** Bernays acknowledges propaganda's ethical valence depends on point of view — does the release pass the test that the *advocacy* side could be defended in public if its mechanisms were disclosed?

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Engineering-of-consent thinking predicts that the highest-pickup releases are those that (a) come from a coalition rather than a single firm, (b) align with motives editors already attribute to their audiences, (c) bring a pre-staged endorsing voice along with the announcement.
