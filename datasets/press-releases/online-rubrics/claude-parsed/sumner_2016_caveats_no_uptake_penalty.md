# Sumner et al. (2016) — Caveats are free; exaggeration buys nothing

Follow-up to Sumner et al. 2014 BMJ. Analyzed 534 press releases from leading peer-reviewed journals plus their associated papers and 582 news stories. Tested directly: *do exaggerations buy more news pickup?* Answer: no. *Do caveats reduce pickup?* Answer: no.

## Headline empirical findings (release-rubric implications)
1. **Exaggeration does not increase uptake.** Releases without exaggerated *advice* averaged 3.5 news stories each; releases *with* exaggerated advice averaged 3.0.
2. **Causal-claim exaggeration does not increase uptake.** Releases without exaggerated causal language averaged 3.1 news stories; releases with exaggerated causal language averaged 3.0.
3. **Caveats do not reduce uptake.** Press releases that included caveats showed no statistically detectable reduction in news-story uptake.
4. **Caveats sharply increase fidelity downstream.** Including caveats raised odds of caveats appearing in news (OR 9.6 for advice, 9.5 for causal claims).

## Rubric criteria (operational)
1. **No-exaggeration rule.** Does the release avoid stronger advice, causal, or generalization claims than the abstract supports? (Per 2014 BMJ paper categories — see `sumner_2016_exaggerations_caveats_plos_one.md` for the full taxonomy.)
2. **Explicit caveats.** Does the release include the limitations of the study (sample size, study type, generalisability) in a marked passage rather than buried at the end?
3. **Cost-of-uptake-myth.** Does the release writer act on the (false) belief that hyping the finding will get more pickup? The Sumner data refutes this and so a rational writer should default to fidelity.
4. **Numeric framing.** Are effects given in absolute as well as relative terms? Does the release indicate the *baseline rate*?
5. **Mechanism honesty.** If the underlying paper proposes a mechanism but does not test it, does the release flag this rather than presenting the mechanism as established?
6. **Population fidelity.** Does the release name the population studied (age, sex, country, comorbidities) rather than implying broader applicability?

## Pickup-worthiness implication
The Sumner-2016 finding undercuts a widely-held PR-office belief that fidelity sacrifices uptake. The empirical evidence: it does not. So the rubric collapses to: **prefer fidelity; caveats are free; the marginal pickup return on exaggeration is zero or negative.**
