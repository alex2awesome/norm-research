# Boorstin (1962) — The Pseudo-Event as press-release archetype

Boorstin coined the term "pseudo-event" for events manufactured *in order to* be reported. The press release is the canonical pseudo-event artifact: it announces the existence of an event whose only reason to exist was the announcement.

## Boorstin's four characteristics of a pseudo-event (release-rubric)
1. **Planned, planted, or incited (not spontaneous).** Does the release report something that was deliberately arranged for the purpose of being covered? (A meeting set just so a press release can describe a "summit"; an award created so the recipient can be named in a release.)
2. **Planted primarily to be reported / reproduced.** Is the existence of the event predominantly a function of its expected media coverage rather than serving an independent operational purpose?
3. **Ambiguous relation to underlying reality.** Boorstin's diagnostic question: "What does it mean?" The pseudo-event invites speculative interpretation precisely because its substantive content is thin.
4. **Self-fulfilling prophecy.** The event becomes "real" *because* it is reported (a market mover, a "trending topic," a candidate "with momentum"). The release helps create the very condition it claims to describe.

## Operational pickup criteria from Boorstin's framework
1. **Reproducibility / repeatability.** Pseudo-events are designed to be repeated (annual rankings, monthly reports, "year-in-review" releases). Calendar-locked recurrence increases predictable pickup.
2. **Convenience for the reporting medium.** Boorstin: the pseudo-event "is arranged for the convenience of the reporting or reproducing media." Pickup correlates with how well the release respects deadlines, formats, and crew availability.
3. **Pre-distributed text.** Boorstin notes that pseudo-events are often written up *before* they happen — the "news holdback." Releases issued in advance under embargo, with the event scripted to fit the release, score highest.
4. **Profitability for the medium.** Pseudo-events fill space at low cost. A release that reduces the marginal cost of column-inches gets picked up.
5. **Mediocrity tolerance.** Boorstin observes pseudo-events are "mediocre" — not too challenging, not too dull. A release pitched at this central tendency converts more reliably than one demanding cognitive effort.
6. **Dramatic enticement.** Pseudo-events incorporate spectacle (a ribbon-cutting, a chart "milestone," a "first," a celebrity sighting).

## Critical caveat (Boorstin's normative point)
Boorstin treats the pseudo-event as a *symptom of cultural decay*: the displacement of "true" news by manufactured news. A useful pickup-rubric distinguishes:
- **High-pickup, low-public-value** releases: pure pseudo-events with no underlying news (rankings of arbitrary criteria; "study finds 7 in 10 Americans prefer X").
- **High-pickup, high-public-value** releases: events that satisfy pseudo-event mechanics *and* report substantive new information (a peer-reviewed study, a quarterly earnings result).
The Boorstinian rubric is neutral on whether pickup is good — it predicts which releases will *be picked up*, not which *deserve to be*.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Pseudo-event characteristics predict pickup independent of substantive newsworthiness. A press release optimized along the four characteristics above will be picked up at higher rates; *but* the long-run credibility cost is the same one Boorstin warns of — eventual reader cynicism about the entire genre.
