# Friedman, Dunwoody & Rogers — scientist-journalist relations rubric

Edited volumes synthesizing AAAS-symposium contributions on what makes science news work. Provide foundational criteria for press releases bridging the scientist–journalist gap.

## Volume 1 (1986) themes — release rubric
1. **Asymmetry of professional incentives.** Scientists are rewarded for caveat-rich rigor; journalists for clear narratives. The release must satisfy both: sufficient caveat to defend to scientists, sufficient narrative clarity to be usable by journalists.
2. **Translation function.** The release is a translation artifact. The PR-officer should be a *bilingual* writer fluent in scientific accuracy and journalistic style.
3. **Independent expert availability.** Pickup-worthy releases supply contact info for at least one independent expert who can comment to journalists with appropriate distance from the issuer.
4. **Direct-author availability windows.** Releases should commit to scientist availability for a defined window (e.g., "Dr. Y available for media calls between 9 a.m.–5 p.m. ET on [date]").
5. **Materials for the visual journalist.** Photos of researchers, lab images, sample animations — Friedman et al. emphasize visual material as a chronic gap in scientist-supplied releases.
6. **Background packets.** A FAQ-style backgrounder for the topic (separate from the release) increases pickup by giving the reporter context to ground the new finding in.

## Volume 2 (1999) — Communicating Uncertainty rubric
7. **Explicit uncertainty disclosure.** For new or controversial science, the release should state uncertainty *quantitatively* (95% CI, range of estimates) rather than rhetorically ("possibly", "might").
8. **Consensus-vs-frontier signal.** Does the release make clear whether the finding is at the consensus core, at the frontier, or in active controversy? Outlets serving informed audiences (NYT science, Nature News) screen for this.
9. **Replication and prior-evidence context.** The release should anchor the new result in prior literature ("This is the [N]th study to find...", "First study to test [intervention] in [population]").
10. **Dissent acknowledgment.** Releases on contested findings should acknowledge dissenting views; pretending unanimity invites correction stories.
11. **Range of plausible outcomes.** For predictive findings (climate, epidemiology, economic forecast), the release should report the range, not the central estimate alone.
12. **Time-horizon clarity.** "By 2050" or "within 5 years" or "in this decade" — be explicit about the time horizon of any predicted effect.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Friedman et al.'s volumes ground a multi-decade rubric for science releases: pickup at quality outlets correlates with visible respect for both communities — sufficient scientific care to satisfy scientist sources; sufficient narrative clarity, visuals, and access to satisfy journalist users. The 1999 uncertainty-focused volume makes the additional point that controlled uncertainty disclosure (with quantified ranges) increases pickup at quality outlets and reduces correction risk.
