# Hijmans, Pleijter & Wester (2003) — what Dutch science news reveals about release pickup

Content analysis of 624 scientific-research news items in 96 editions of major Dutch newspapers, supplemented by interviews with 10 editors and science reporters. Findings about source disclosure and methodological reporting are directly applicable to release-rubric design.

## Headline empirical findings
1. **60% of science news items contained no reference to a source** (no press release citation, no journal article cited, no scientific report mentioned). Source-blank items were dominant.
2. **On dedicated science pages, 65% of items did cite a journal article** — a sharp difference from general news pages.
3. **Quality papers and dedicated science sections did not supply more or better methodological information** than general papers — contrary to a quality-correlated hypothesis.

## Hijmans-Pleijter-Wester rubric criteria
1. **Self-attributed citation.** Because most science news items omit source attribution, releases that *embed the citation* in the lead (e.g., "according to a study published today in Nature, ...") increase the probability the citation survives into final copy.
2. **Methodological summary in pre-canned form.** A 1-2 sentence methods summary in lay language at the top of the release, ready for direct copy-paste, has higher survival than buried methods.
3. **Page-target awareness.** Releases pegged for science-section pickup should give scientific detail; releases pegged for general-news pickup should foreground impact in plain language. Both versions should ideally be supplied.
4. **Editor-interview alignment.** Hijmans et al.'s editor interviews indicated editors choose science items by perceived audience interest more than methodological depth. The release lead should signal "interesting to your reader" before "scientifically rigorous."
5. **Standalone interpretability.** Because newsrooms cannot easily reach scientific authors, releases must be interpretable without a follow-up call.
6. **Multilingual robustness.** In Dutch (and other small-language) markets, science news is typically picked up via wire translation. Releases should pre-supply a version in English suitable for wire translators.
7. **Routine-channel match.** Dutch newspapers rely heavily on ANP (national press agency). Releases that reach ANP in publishable form get amplified; releases that go only direct to newspapers reach a smaller pool.
8. **Limitation transparency.** Even though omitted limitations don't hurt pickup, *included* limitations have been found in subsequent literature to raise downstream caveat reproduction without reducing uptake (cf. Sumner 2016).

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Hijmans et al.'s findings imply that pickup is driven less by scientific quality of the release than by (i) ease-of-publication, (ii) audience-interest signal in the lead, and (iii) wire-channel routing. The methodological richness that scientists value is largely *not* what determines pickup in the Dutch press.
