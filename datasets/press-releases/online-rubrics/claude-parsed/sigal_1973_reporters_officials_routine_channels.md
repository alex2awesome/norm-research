# Sigal (1973) — Routine-channel theory and the press release

Sigal's empirical study of *New York Times* and *Washington Post* front pages found that *the bulk of news comes through "routine channels"* — official proceedings, press releases, press conferences, briefings, non-spontaneous events — rather than through reporter initiative. The press release is the canonical routine-channel artifact, and this analytic frame yields a pickup rubric.

## Sigal's three sourcing channels (and their implications for releases)
1. **Routine channels** (~60% of front-page stories): official records, press releases, press conferences, briefings, scheduled non-spontaneous events. *A pickup-worthy release is one designed to slot into a routine channel a beat reporter already monitors.*
2. **Informal channels** (~15%): leaks, off-the-record briefings, background sessions. *A press release competes with informal channels by offering on-the-record specificity that the leak cannot.*
3. **Enterprise channels** (~25%): reporter's own observation, interviews initiated by the reporter, original research. *A pickup-worthy release lowers the cost-of-enterprise enough that the reporter chooses the routine path.*

## Sigal's rubric criteria (operationalized)
1. **Channel-fit.** Does the release land in a recognized channel (PR newswire, beat-reporter inbox, scheduled briefing, AP "day book") rather than in a generic blast?
2. **Official-source authority.** Sigal showed that ~58% of sources on Times/Post front pages were US officials. Does the release foreground a *titled official* or institutional spokesperson whose authority a reporter can stake a story on?
3. **Routine event scaffolding.** Is the release pegged to a calendared occasion (hearing, anniversary, scheduled report release, conference) so the reporter has a temporal hook?
4. **Reduction of reportorial uncertainty.** Does the release pre-resolve the editor's "is this real?" question (institutional letterhead, named author, peer-reviewed citation, embargoed copy of underlying document)?
5. **Beat alignment.** Sigal found beats organize what is even visible to a paper. Is the release distributed to the *correct* beat (health, business, courts, science) and pegged to that beat's evaluative criteria?
6. **Bureaucratic legitimacy.** Pickup is higher when the release comes from an institution whose ongoing existence the beat already covers (e.g., NIH vs. an unknown advocacy group of the same scientific quality).
7. **Pseudo-event vs. spontaneous event posture.** Sigal (following Boorstin) distinguishes pseudo-events (staged for coverage) from spontaneous events. A release that *transforms* a real event into a coverable one (rather than purely manufacturing one) scores higher on long-run credibility.
8. **Repeat-game posture.** Routine channels reward sources who don't burn reporters. Does the release reflect a sustained, accurate sourcing relationship rather than a one-off attempt to crash the channel?

## Pickup-worthiness implication
By the routine-channel frame, the predictable failure mode is a release from a non-recurring, non-official source pushed through a generic wire — which is invisible because no beat has it on a routine watch.
