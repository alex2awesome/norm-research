# Lewis, Williams & Franklin (2008) — empirical pickup determinants

Lewis et al. content-analyzed 2,207 newspaper items (Guardian, Times, Independent, Daily Telegraph, Daily Mail) and 402 broadcast items (BBC, ITN, Sky) across two sample weeks in 2006. Findings supply empirical predictors of which press-release-derived stories are run, and from which sources.

## Headline empirical findings (proportions reproduce in subsequent literature)
- ~60% of press articles had PR materials in their construction (with full or partial dependence).
- ~19% of articles were *entirely* derived from PR/wire material with no additional reporting.
- Wire copy (PA in UK) was a major second-order source; many "PR" stories arrived at the paper via wire, with PR origins obscured.
- Health, business, and entertainment beats showed the highest PR dependence; politics and crime showed the lowest.

## Rubric criteria implied by the findings
1. **Wire-friendliness.** Does the release reach the wire (PA / AP) and is it formatted so the wire desk will run it largely intact? Wire pickup is the strongest single predictor of newspaper printing.
2. **Beat targeting.** Health, business and entertainment desks accept PR copy at much higher rates. Is the release pegged to a beat with high known PR uptake, with framing tuned to that beat's editorial conventions?
3. **PR-origin laundering tolerance.** Lewis et al. found newspapers often run PR-sourced material *without attribution to PR origin*. Releases that read as if a journalist could have written them are far more pickup-worthy than overtly promotional copy.
4. **One-source sufficiency.** When a release supplies enough quotes, statistics, and context that a reporter can file from the release alone, pickup is far higher. The benchmark: can a sub-editor publish in 15 minutes?
5. **Press-association rewriting tolerance.** Releases optimized for the wire's rewrite step (clean leads, clear attribution, no hyperlinks, no embedded HTML) survive the PA/AP transformation more intact.
6. **Two-week news-cycle calibration.** Releases pegged to ongoing news themes captured within the sample weeks (e.g., a current health-policy debate) had higher pickup than orphan announcements.
7. **Source authority.** Government departments, large corporations, and major universities had disproportionately high uptake; small NGOs and grassroots groups had near-zero pickup despite comparable newsworthiness — a subsidy-asymmetry signal.
8. **Reduction-to-headline test.** Stories with a clean single-sentence summary in the lead were converted to wire copy more reliably than discursive releases.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Lewis et al.'s data make pickup substantially predictable from *channel* (wire vs. direct), *beat* (health/business > politics), *source authority* (institutional rank), and *editorial-readiness* (publishable in 15 minutes). The text quality of the underlying release matters but is dominated by these four structural factors.
