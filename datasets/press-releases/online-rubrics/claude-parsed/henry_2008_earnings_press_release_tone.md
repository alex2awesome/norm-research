# Henry (2008) — Tone, length, and number-density in earnings press releases

Henry uses computer-based content analysis on a corpus of US earnings press releases plus event-window stock returns. The work establishes that *how* an earnings release is written, not just *what* it announces, moves prices — implying that pickup-worthiness for financial releases is partly a textual-style optimization.

## Headline empirical findings
1. **Tone (positive/negative word frequency) significantly predicts investor reactions** holding earnings constant. Positive-tone releases earn higher abnormal returns at the announcement window.
2. **Length, percentage of numbers vs. words, and word complexity** also predict investor reaction.
3. **Mechanism: prospect theory.** Positive framing causes investors to evaluate results against a more favorable reference point; negative framing the reverse.

## Henry rubric criteria for earnings / financial press releases
1. **Tone calibration.** Use Loughran-McDonald-style financial-domain dictionaries to audit pre-publication tone; aim for tone that matches the underlying performance narrative without engineering false optimism that exposes the issuer to SEC tone-vs-fact challenge.
2. **Number density.** Releases with a higher percentage of numerical content are processed by analysts as more substantive. Pickup at financial wires correlates with numeric density.
3. **Word complexity.** Financial press releases that score lower on complexity metrics (Fog Index, average syllables/word) are picked up at higher rates by general-business outlets.
4. **Headline tone–payload alignment.** Headline tone should match earnings reality; mismatched headlines (positive tone over a miss) trigger downward correction stories.
5. **Lead specificity.** First sentence should report the key headline number (revenue, EPS, change vs. consensus) in absolute and percentage terms.
6. **Reference-point disclosure.** State the comparison reference (Y/Y, Q/Q, vs. consensus, vs. guidance) explicitly so investors can apply the prospect-theory frame without inventing one.
7. **Forward-looking language calibration.** Forward-looking statements should use the issuer's standard safe-harbor language verbatim, not innovate.
8. **Length discipline.** Henry's data suggests there is a length sweet spot for earnings releases (≈800–1500 words for mid-cap; ≈1500–2500 for large-cap). Excessively long releases reduce per-section pickup.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Henry's findings imply that for earnings press releases, pickup is partly a function of writing style: tone, complexity, length, and number-density predict both wire-pickup rate and immediate market reaction. A pickup-worthy earnings release matches its tone to its earnings reality, packs numerical specificity in the lead, and lands at the genre-typical length.
