# Schlesinger (1990) — Source-strategy framework against the "primary definers" thesis

Schlesinger critiques Stuart Hall et al.'s "primary definers" model (in which dominant institutions automatically set the news agenda) and argues for analyzing news as an outcome of *strategic competition* among sources of unequal but contestable resources. Direct implications for release-rubric design.

## Schlesinger's core claims
1. **Sources are not equally positioned**, but neither are they passively dominant; they compete strategically.
2. **The "primary definers" thesis is too static** — it cannot explain how non-elite sources sometimes win agenda contests.
3. **Source-strategy** (planning, timing, coalition-building, framing) determines which competing release lands.
4. **Internal divisions within elite institutions** open access for counter-sources who exploit them.

## Schlesinger rubric criteria for releases
1. **Strategic-competition awareness.** A release should be designed knowing that competing sources will issue counter-releases. Anticipate the most likely counter-frame and respond pre-emptively.
2. **Coalition-building signal.** Releases that bring multiple distinct sources into alignment (cross-institution co-sign, multi-stakeholder endorsement) outperform releases from a single source.
3. **Internal-elite-division exploitation.** Where elite institutions are divided (a regulator vs. an industry, two academic disciplines), a release that surfaces and uses the division gains traction even from a less-resourced position.
4. **Strategic timing.** Schlesinger emphasizes timing as a key strategic resource. Releases timed to compete for an editor's attention slot (after a competing release fails, before a competing release lands) earn disproportionate pickup.
5. **Resource-substitution.** A release from a resource-poor source can substitute *strategic intelligence* for institutional resources: knowing the editor's deadline, the beat's current obsession, the rival source's vulnerabilities.
6. **Frame contestability.** Schlesinger rejects the assumption that the dominant frame will automatically win. A release with a *more usable* frame for the journalist will displace a frame from a more-resourced source if the alternative is better made.
7. **Beat-network entry.** A release should target *one* beat at high quality before attempting cross-beat distribution; Schlesinger shows successful entry to one beat opens others.
8. **Accountability-trail.** Releases that generate accountability questions (who funded it, who vetted it) on the more-resourced rival earn the contest by shifting the journalist's burden.
9. **Long-run reputation building.** Pickup is partly a function of repeated honest dealings; one accurate release builds the credit balance that allows the next release to earn quick acceptance.
10. **Counter-rival source-package.** Releases competing against a major-institution release should bring a *complete* counter-package: counter-data, counter-expert, counter-narrative, counter-image. Partial counter-packages get filtered out.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Schlesinger's source-strategy frame predicts that pickup is *not* deterministically a function of issuer rank. Less-resourced issuers can win contested news agenda by playing strategic competition well: better timing, better coalitions, better usable frames, exploitation of elite divisions, and complete counter-packages. The "primary definers" thesis is false: a well-strategized release can outperform a poorly-strategized release from a more-resourced source.
