# Borchelt & Hudson (2008) — Past the deficit model: engagement-style press releases

Borchelt (NIH/NCI Communications) and Hudson (Genetics & Public Policy Center, Johns Hopkins) reject the linear *deficit model* (more facts → more understanding → more support) and argue for a two-way engagement model in which the scientist's understanding of the public must change too. Direct rubric implications for science press releases.

## Borchelt-Hudson rubric criteria
1. **Anti-deficit framing.** Does the release avoid the implicit message "if only the public understood X, they would support Y"? Releases written from the deficit posture trigger reader rejection on contested topics.
2. **Public-values acknowledgment.** Does the release acknowledge — not dismiss — the values, concerns, and prior beliefs of the target public (religious, cultural, economic)?
3. **Bidirectional posture.** Does the release indicate that the issuing institution is *learning* from public input, not only teaching?
4. **Trust-deficit recognition.** The Borchelt-Hudson cited 2004 Genetics & Public Policy Center survey found ~40% of US residents do not trust scientists "to put society's interest above their personal goals." Releases should anticipate this trust deficit rather than assume institutional credibility.
5. **Disclosure of researcher motives.** Releases should disclose the researcher's professional and financial stakes, defusing the trust-deficit issue.
6. **Acknowledgment of past failures.** On topics where science has previously over-promised (e.g., gene-therapy hype, neuroimaging overreach), the release should acknowledge this history rather than ignore it.
7. **Plain-language ethical frame.** Releases on ethically charged topics (genetics, AI, embryos) should use plain language about the ethical issues rather than technical hedging that feels evasive.
8. **Public-input invitation.** Where appropriate, the release should indicate channels for public comment, deliberation, or feedback rather than treating publication as a one-way broadcast.
9. **Trusted-translator availability.** Releases should name and supply contact for community-trusted translators (clergy, patient-advocate groups, community physicians) when possible — not only the issuing institution's spokespeople.
10. **Real-world consequence framing.** Frame the finding's practical implication for ordinary readers' lives rather than for the scientific community's internal status.
11. **Avoidance of jargon-as-authority.** Releases should not use technical vocabulary as an authority signal; this reinforces the deficit-model dynamic and reduces trust.
12. **Process-transparency.** Disclose research process (who was involved, how decisions were made, what alternatives were considered).

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Borchelt-Hudson's frame predicts that release pickup is increasingly conditional on the receiving outlet's perception that the issuing institution is engaging in good faith with public concerns rather than top-down lecturing. Quality outlets serving diverse audiences explicitly screen against deficit-model framing on contested-science topics. Engagement-style releases earn deeper, more sustained, and more trusted coverage even at the cost of some sensational pickup.
