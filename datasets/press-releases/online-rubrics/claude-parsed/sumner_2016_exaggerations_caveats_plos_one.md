# Sumner et al. (2016) - Exaggerations and Caveats in Press Releases

## Research Scope
This 2016 PLOS ONE study examined 534 journal press releases, their corresponding peer-reviewed papers, and 582 associated news articles from major outlets like Lancet, BMJ, Science, and Nature publications from 2011.

## Coding Scheme - Exaggeration Categories Tracked

### 1. Health Advice (Four-Level Scale)
Researchers coded advice across four levels:
- None
- Implicit (e.g., "these findings suggest that mid-late childhood may be the best")
- Explicit (non-reader directed)
- Explicit (to reader/public)

Press releases containing stronger advice than journal articles were classified as exaggerated.

### 2. Causal Claims (Seven-Point Determinism Scale)
Statements in titles and opening sentences coded with seven strength levels:
- No statement
- Explicit statement of no relation
- Correlational language ("associated with")
- Ambiguous phrasing ("linked to")
- Conditional causation ("might increase")
- Potential causation ("can increase")
- Unconditional causation ("increases")

Focus was on studies using correlational designs.

### 3. Human Inferences from Non-Human Research
Categorized whether findings from animal, cellular, or simulation studies were presented as:
- Explicitly non-human
- Implicitly human
- Explicitly human

## Key Findings

**Exaggeration Rates:**
- Advice: 23% of press releases (95% CI: 17-28%)
- Causal claims: 21% of press releases (95% CI: 15-27%)
- Non-human research: Lower rates than university sources

**Press Release-to-News Transmission:**
Exaggerations in press releases strongly predicted exaggerations in news coverage (odds ratios: 2.4 for advice; 10.9 for causal claims).

**Newsworthiness Effect:**
Contrary to assumptions, exaggerated press releases were *not* associated with increased news coverage or story volume.

**Caveat Impact:**
- Present in only 15% of advice statements and 14% of causal claims
- Caveats in press releases strongly predicted their appearance in news (odds ratios: 9.5-9.6)
- No evidence that caveats reduced journalistic interest

## Implicit Rubric for Press Releases
- Match advice level to source paper
- Match causal language strength to source paper
- Make non-human research scope explicit
- Include caveats and limitations
