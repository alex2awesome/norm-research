# Woloshin, Schwartz et al. (2009) — Academic medical center press releases: a quality audit

Random sample of 200 press releases issued by 20 leading US academic medical centers. Established that university PR offices systematically fall short on basic disclosure quality.

## Headline empirical findings
1. **44% promoted animal or laboratory research; of those, 74% claimed relevance to human health** despite the underlying study not testing it.
2. **23% of releases on primary human research omitted study size.**
3. **34% of releases failed to quantify results** (no effect sizes given).
4. **29% of releases were judged to exaggerate research importance**; 26% of investigator quotes were judged to overstate importance.
5. **Press releases issued by 20 academic medical centers** frequently lacked the basic detail needed to evaluate the science.

## Woloshin–Schwartz rubric criteria
1. **Animal-to-human flag.** If the underlying study used animal models or in vitro cells, the release must say so prominently in the headline or first sentence and not silently imply human relevance.
2. **Sample size disclosure.** Total N (and N per arm if randomized) belongs in the lead paragraph.
3. **Effect-size quantification.** Both *relative* and *absolute* effect sizes; no claim of "increased" or "reduced" without numbers.
4. **Study-design disclosure.** RCT, observational cohort, case-control, retrospective, single-arm pilot, or in vitro — clearly labeled.
5. **Funding and conflict-of-interest.** Funding source(s) and investigator financial conflicts disclosed at the top, not buried.
6. **Investigator-quote restraint.** Quotes should not contain interpretive claims that exceed the data. Co-authors should review release quotes before issuance.
7. **Translational caveat.** For early-stage findings, the release must include a one-line caveat about the path-to-clinic distance ("This is an early-stage finding; clinical application would require [N] more years of testing.").
8. **Limitations paragraph.** Reproduce the published paper's limitations section verbatim or in lay summary; do not omit.
9. **Replication / consensus context.** State whether this is the first study, a replication, or one of many; what the broader evidence base says.
10. **Cost / availability disclosure.** For interventions, state cost (where known) and availability (FDA-approved, in trials, lab-only).

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Woloshin & Schwartz's data show that academic medical center releases frequently fail basic quality criteria but are picked up at high rates anyway because the institutional brand carries weight. This is a credibility-debt structure: pickup is high in the short run but generates downstream correction stories and erodes trust in the institution. A pickup-worthy *and* accurate release passes the 10 criteria above and is increasingly preferred by quality outlets that explicitly screen against these failure modes (HealthNewsReview review program; major-paper science desks).
