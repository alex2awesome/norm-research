# Smith — Becoming a Public Relations Writer: format-rules rubric

Smith's textbook supplies "the most comprehensive list of public relations writing formats" with stepwise rules for each. The release-and-related-genres rubric distilled from Smith:

## Smith's release-format rules
1. **News release vs. media advisory distinction.** A news release announces *news* (a happening, a finding, an action). A media advisory invites *attendance* (a press conference, an event). Mixing the two confuses the editor and reduces pickup.
2. **Fact sheet vs. backgrounder.** A fact sheet is bulleted, telegraphic, scannable; a backgrounder is narrative, contextual. The release should be supplemented by the appropriate companion document.
3. **Inverted-pyramid lead discipline.** First sentence answers the W's; subsequent paragraphs add detail in descending importance.
4. **Quote sourcing.** Direct quotes belong to named, titled individuals — never to "a spokesperson" or "an executive." Position the title for the journalist (e.g., "Lead author Dr. Y, Professor of Z at University W").
5. **Local-interest tagging.** Releases distributed nationally should be tagged with local-interest variants for community papers.
6. **Date and time stamping.** Both date of issue and embargo time (if any) prominently displayed.
7. **Photo/video inclusion.** Smith treats visual integration as a baseline requirement, not an optional add-on.
8. **Type of release identification.** Smith distinguishes types (announcement, response, feature, hometown, financial, crisis); each has format conventions.

## Smith's "ethics and legal" rubric (chapter coverage)
9. **Truth in publicity.** Statements presented as fact must be verifiable; opinions belong in quotes.
10. **Avoidance of deceptive practices.** No fake quotes, no astroturfing, no concealed sponsorship.
11. **Privacy considerations.** Named individuals quoted in a release have given express consent; minors and patients are protected.
12. **Defamation risk audit.** Releases naming third parties should be screened for libel risk (especially competitor mentions).
13. **Copyright clearance.** Quoted text from published sources, photographs, and graphics are licensed.
14. **Disclosure obligations.** SEC, FTC, FDA, and parallel regulatory disclosure rules followed where applicable.

## Smith's "emerging media" rubric (newer editions)
15. **Multi-platform format.** Releases should have versions for blog, website, email, social (Twitter/X, LinkedIn, Facebook), photo (Instagram), video (YouTube/TikTok).
16. **Hashtag coordination.** Suggested hashtags supplied, with the issuer's own social plan synchronized.
17. **Embed-friendly.** Tweets, videos, and quotes should be supplied as embeddable content blocks.
18. **Mobile-first formatting.** Release pages must render correctly on phone screens.
19. **Search-engine signaling.** Headline should signal the news in search-friendly form (key terms in first 60 characters).
20. **Wiki-citation consideration.** For releases that may seed Wikipedia entries, supply RFC-style citation-friendly facts and verifiable secondary sources.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Smith's textbook codifies the modern-media format expectations: release-as-canonical-document, with supplementary formats for each downstream channel. A pickup-worthy release in the 2020s satisfies both classical newsroom intake (Wilcox-Cameron canon) and emerging-media format expectations (multi-platform, embed-friendly, mobile-first). Smith's coverage of ethics and legal rules underscores that pickup is also a function of risk-reduction: a release that exposes the publishing outlet to legal risk gets demoted.
