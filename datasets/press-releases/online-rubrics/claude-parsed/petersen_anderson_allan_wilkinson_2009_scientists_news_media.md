# Petersen, Anderson, Allan & Wilkinson (2009) — scientists' diagnosis of media coverage

Mixed-method study of scientists working in nanotechnology and the journalists covering it. Provides scientist-side criteria for what makes a release succeed without compromising scientific accuracy in an emerging-technology / risk domain.

## Headline empirical findings
1. **Scientists viewed nanoparticle safety as the dominant risk issue; media gave it scant attention.** A misalignment between scientific salience and media salience that releases must bridge.
2. **Both scientists and journalists acknowledged difficulties in communicating risk under uncertainty.** Releases that try to suppress uncertainty fail one community; releases that overstate uncertainty fail the other.
3. **A single high-profile public statement (Prince Charles, April 2003) catalyzed 15 months of subsequent UK coverage.** Pickup is path-dependent on a triggering event the release can be tied to.
4. **Scientists worried that early framing in the press becomes "locked in"** — releases at the formative stage of a technology shape its long-run public reception.

## Petersen-Anderson-Allan-Wilkinson rubric criteria for emerging-tech / risk releases
1. **Scientific-salience signal.** Does the release make the *internally salient* scientific concern (e.g., nanoparticle safety) explicit even if not media-salient? This pre-empts later corrections.
2. **Calibrated uncertainty language.** Does the release use precise probability/effect-size language ("4× higher in vitro; not yet tested in vivo") rather than rhetorical hedging ("may", "could")?
3. **Path-dependence awareness.** Does the release reference a current public event (a regulatory decision, a public-figure statement, a recent incident) it is tied to, increasing pickup via news-cycle anchoring?
4. **Frame-locking caution.** For early-stage technologies, does the release avoid metaphors (e.g., "Frankenstein", "miracle cure") that may lock in public framing prematurely?
5. **Scientist-journalist co-development.** Does the release reflect prior scientist–journalist conversation? Petersen et al. found scientists who had time to brief journalists got more accurate coverage.
6. **Risk-benefit balance.** Does the release present *both* benefits and risks at proportional length? Asymmetric release (benefits-only) leads scientists to disengage from future PR cooperation.
7. **Independent regulator citation.** For technologies under regulatory review, does the release cite the relevant regulatory authority's current stance?
8. **Release-author transparency.** Is the release identified as institutional PR or scientific commentary? Scientists in the study objected to PR releases that mimic scientific commentary.

## Pickup-worthiness implication
Pickup of emerging-tech releases is high under sensational framing but degrades long-run scientist cooperation and triggers later corrective coverage. Petersen et al.'s findings imply a pickup-worthy *and* scientifically-defensible release uses the criteria above; outlets known to specialize in tech/risk coverage (Nature News, Wired, NYT science) prefer these markers.
