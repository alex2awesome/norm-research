# Ivy Lee (1906) Declaration of Principles — first ethical rubric for press releases

Lee's one-page Declaration was sent to American newspaper editors when his agency, Parker & Lee, opened. It is the first written articulation of the standards that distinguish a press release from a "press-agentry" puff. The document maps directly to a release-worthiness rubric:

1. **Openness about source.** "This is not a secret press bureau. All our work is done in the open." Does the release disclose, on its face, who issued it and on whose behalf?
2. **Supplying news, not advertisement.** "We aim to supply news." Does the release contain genuine news rather than concealed promotional copy formatted to look like a news item?
3. **Accuracy.** "Our matter is accurate." Are the facts in the release verifiable and free of exaggeration?
4. **Editorial-verification offer.** Lee offered help "to verify directly any statement of fact." Does the release make sources, raw data, or contact information available so an editor can independently check claims?
5. **Named, identifiable issuer.** Does the release name the principal (the corporation, candidate, agency) on whose behalf it is sent — never anonymous, never falsely sourced?
6. **Public-interest test.** Information is offered only on subjects "of value and interest to the public to know about." Does the release pass a public-interest threshold, or is it of interest only to the issuer?
7. **Prompt accessibility.** "Details on any subject treated will be supplied promptly." Does the release indicate channels for follow-up (named spokesperson, phone, address) rather than dead-end attribution?
8. **Editorial cooperation.** Lee committed to assisting editors "cheerfully" with additional information. Does the release support, rather than substitute for, independent reporting (e.g., supplemental backgrounders, image rights, expert availability)?
9. **No suppression of unfavorable matter.** Implicit in "in the open": does the release acknowledge, rather than conceal, material context that an editor would want?
10. **Signed responsibility.** Lee insisted releases be signed by an accountable individual, not issued anonymously. Is there a named, contactable PR officer of record?

## Pickup-worthiness implication
A release that fails Lee's transparency tests — anonymous, promotional, inaccessible to verification — scores poorly even if it is well-written. A release that names the issuer, supplies verifiable facts, and gives an editor a clear path to follow up scores well by the original ethical standard of the field.
