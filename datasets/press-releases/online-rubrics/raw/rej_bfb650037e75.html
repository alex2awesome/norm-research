Designers and social media experts split on Twitter’s X rebranding

Feature

Some called the move “another misstep in a series of bad decisions,” while others said that “blue bird under Musk just represents failure and sadness.”

by Campaign US Staff July 25 2023

  • Share article on Twitter
  • Share article on Facebook
  • Share article on LinkedIn

Over the weekend, Elon Musk and Linda Yaccarino rebranded Twitter as X. 

Tweets, retweets and quote tweets remain on the platform for now, but Musk has stated his intention to replace any trace of the blue bird app with “x’s,” going so far as to remove Twitter’s logo from its San Francisco headquarters.

x’s

— Elon Musk (@elonmusk) July 24, 2023

Campaign US fielded design and social media experts to share their thoughts on the X rebranding.

Question: Twitter has an iconic brand with massive cultural cache. Its blue bird logo is ubiquitous online, and actions on the platform have become verbs. How does its rebrand to X affect the platform's brand equity? What does that mean for the platform's future?

Barry Lowenthal, president, Inuvo

While I don’t have confidence that Musk has the marketing chops to rebrand Twitter, or even make it profitable, the blue bird under Musk just represents failure and sadness. Rebranding it to X could be an opportunity for him to make his company stand for something new. I wonder what ChatGPT thinks of X?

Jon Judah, chief strategy officer, Huge

I think what's interesting or even ironic here is that choosing “X” – something often seen as a generic placeholder –  is seemingly intended to make the platform broaden its appeal. But because Elon's own personal brand has been so tied up in its messaging and actions, it constrains its potential. 

The man loves “X” – SpaceX, X.com, Model X – so I think it will be difficult to separate the new identity from his personal brand in the near term. Brands are more than just new logos, they're promises built over consistent actions, and while Elon has made good on many of his promises with the platform, he's also been wildly inconsistent, as evidenced by his changing of tune on moderation policies, for example. In order for the platform to meaningfully grow again in the future, X must find more meaning outside of Elon's own actions and build real trust and equity with its users.

Aaron Hall, group director of naming, Siegel + Gale

Elon Musk's decision to rename his newest purchase from Twitter to X is another misstep in a series of bad decisions since he bought the brand. That's not to say that changing the name of a company is bad. But usually, a name change, especially one involving one of the world's most recognizable brand names, justifies the use of finesse, time and strategy. Clearly, the overnight transition to "X" involved none of these.

This rebrand feels more like a move to bolster Elon's ego than a signal of the "everything app" he promises it represents. Perhaps it's even his attempt to put a knife in the fried bird so that people stop comparing the mangled version of today's Twitter to its halcyon days. Today's malfunctioning app is now just X, entirely of Elon's making. He's hoping we forget what we once had as we stop using the terms "tweet" and "twitter."

There's also the issue that "X" is so overused and dated that it means everything and nothing at all. It feels like exactly what it is: A naming trend from a bygone era used by a nerdy Gen Xer from that same era who doesn't know how to brand anything, so he just slaps the letter "X" on it.

Jenn Szekely, president, Coley Porter Bell

They say a logo should not just be about a change of symbol, but a symbol of change. Musk’s announcement of the X name and logo is clearly signaling there is a new direction afoot. The job now is telling the story of this change, both visually and verbally.

It’s a big leap to move from a visual identity that represents everyone having a voice to an X, with some of the harshest angles in the alphabet, coupled with stark black and white. But in the hands of the right branding team, the grander vision for the company can be told in a way that both communicates the new vision and at the same time, retains some of the equity and strengths of the Twitter brand.

There was a time when online calls (and then video calls) were synonymous with Skype. Like Twitter, the name became a verb that was easy to say – but technology changes, competition arises and people move on. We don’t even think about Skype today. While it will take a minute for people to embrace saying X’s over tweets, brands have overcome their name awkwardly used as a verb. For instance, Teams or WhatsApp do not roll off the tongue, but we say them anyway.

Steven Denekas, global SVP of creative, Dept

Twitter is the newest brand that's made the bold move of evolving and embracing change while the world watches and expresses their frustration with that change. Yes, people will leave the platform. But at the end of the day, this gives the new organization something to believe in and rally behind together moving forward – something to own. To me, that's what anyone would say about the move if it were anyone but Elon Musk, but he stirs up emotions so culturally we want to attack. 

If I take my personal subjectivity out of the equation, this move is a moment for a brand to become who it is in its soul, whether the world likes it or not. At least we'll all have a rationale for whether we stay or leave Twitter based on either our shared or divergent values. So it's really an inflection moment for all of us.

Ross Clugston, chief creative officer, Design Bridge and Partners

Musk never intended to acquire Twitter and keep the status quo — it’s not his style, and anyone who’s feigning shock at X wasn’t paying attention. In fact, if you really look closely, each of his businesses has a deceptively single-minded purpose. But the same can’t be said for Twitter. Musk’s attempts to try to step away from the platform’s baggage and legacy are honestly to be expected at this point — that’s the only way to move forward. 

Now, as to how X might somehow help him connect his other entities in the future — specifically Starlink and SpaceX, as well as traffic management with the Boring Company and Tesla — is yet to be seen, but I’d be surprised if that’s not also part of the plan.

Orlando Baeza, chief marketing officer, Flock Freight

Without a doubt, it tanks the brand equity in Twitter pretty significantly, overnight. However, that might be ok, if that product, brand and maybe business model are going to be reimagined alongside the brand identity. It's hard to see how you make that decision without big developments to the product and business model coming next. So maybe it's too early to tell. Elon has proven before that he can deliver. I'm not quick to write him off. So I think this is the first move of more to come.

Andrew Miller, chief growth officer, Interbrand

Twitter’s rebrand to X.com is a haphazard mess, a case example of exactly what not to do when renaming a company and introducing a new brand. Typically, corporate entities considering a name or visual design change undergo deep self-examination, spend time with consumers and seek to understand what people need now and next. All of this informs a strategic story about where the brand and business are headed, and the launch of the new brand follows an orchestrated migration path from the current state to the new entity. 

X.com is anything but thoughtful and intentional. Instead, it appears to reflect one man’s penchant for the letter X, and a simplistic promise of being an “everything app.” While it is viable to argue that Twitter needs a rebrand to reinvigorate a long-struggling business, making a hasty change that throws out the baby with the bathwater is disheartening, and is likely to destroy more brand and business value in the process. 

Twitter has an ownable language strategy (tweets and retweets) and is represented by a distinctive and highly recognizable symbol. All of that brand equity is shown the door in favor of a generic X. It is quite telling that when asked how users should talk about retweets, Musk’s answer was “that whole concept should be rethought.” Brand changes require detailed planning – for X.com, is there any discernible plan other than petulance? Rebrands should be a symbol of change, not simply a change of symbol.

Jake Hancock, senior partner, brand strategy, Lippincott

The majority of reactions are shock at the overnight abandonment of the immense equity the Twitter brand has built over the years. It feels wasteful and reckless, but the strongest emotion might be sadness. Losing the blue bird is like a favorite show being canceled too soon or even the loss of a loved one. The public mourning is proof that Twitter stood for something deeply meaningful to many people. 

The problem is that this meaning isn’t what Elon Musk ever wanted his brand to mean. Those mourning the loss of Twitter seem to have missed Musk’s not-so-subtle promises to alter the very fabric of the business. The chaotic, attention-grabbing move to X proves that a brand revolution can be a much swifter way to change expectations than words and experience alone. It is far harder to redefine a brand like Twitter, with deeply ingrained associations, than it is to create new meaning with a fresh slate. And in many ways, X, with all its challenges, legal hurdles and messiness, is a perfect name to embody the Musk brand of rebellion. If you don’t like it, Musk’s Twitter was never for you. 

We’re just finally seeing its external identity reflect the change that’s been a long time coming. In other words, Twitter didn’t die this weekend; it died when it was bought by a man with a disruptive agenda. And X is a better brand to drive that agenda.

Jeffrey Tousey, CEO and founder, Beekman Social

Any time a company initiates a massive rebrand, there is going to be risk involved and inevitable user drop-off. We have seen major organizations undergo brand evolutions successfully; however, the strategy, story and intent behind the rebrand is critical.

Elon Musk and his team don't appear to be controlling the narrative in a strategic way. The meaning behind the "X" is unclear. It feels vague. The brand is polarizing users in what feels like an "us vs. them" sentiment. Twitter had become a huge household name, so to toss that brand equity out the window -- on top of all the other controversies and operational platform issues plaguing the company -- seems reckless and awkward. I believe we will see a core group of diehard users ride the transition, while mass users will likely respond differently, and potentially take their business elsewhere.
