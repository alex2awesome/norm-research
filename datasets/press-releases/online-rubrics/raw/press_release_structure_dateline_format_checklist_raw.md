        <p><a href="http://guides.newswire.com/g/how-to-write-a-press-release/"><img decoding="async" class="alignright" style="margin-left: 20px;" src="https://www.newswire.com/blog/wp-content/uploads/2016/06/Guide-Writing-Press-Release.jpg" alt="get-started-newsroom" width="400" height="400" align="right" /></a></p>
<p><i><span style="font-weight: 400;">What is AP Style?</span></i><span style="font-weight: 400;"> For anyone unfamiliar, AP Style stands for Associated Press Style and refers to the standardization of content that allows media around the world to have consistent and uniform structure for news writing, grammar, abbreviations and titles. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">AP style is the gold standard with which all news writing is measured. And, just as the <a href="https://www.newswire.com/blog/how-to-write-a-press-release-format">format of a press release</a> is important, so too is the content. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">In fact, according to a USC Annenberg study, 88% of public relations professionals and 80% of marketers believe digital storytelling is the future of communications. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">As the focus on crafting compelling press releases intensifies, business professionals need to hone their skills and leverage AP Style to their advantage. </span></p>
<h2>The AP Press Release Advantage</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">If your content is written in AP Style, there’s a better chance the media will gravitate to your story, </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">especially when that </span><a href="https://www.newswire.com/press-release-optimizer"><b>press release writing</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> follows a standardized format. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">That’s because adhering to the wording and format they’re most familiar with will demonstrate your professionalism and understanding of the information they need to develop stories of their own.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Now, you landed on this blog because you want to know more about how to format a press release dateline with AP Style. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">And, that’s exactly what we’re going to help you with.</span></p>
<h2>What is a Dateline?</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The dateline is found at the beginning of the press release. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">In this example, a press release about our </span><a href="https://www.newswire.com/media-suite"><b>Media Suite</b></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> demonstrates where the dateline appears. </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">The highlighted portion in the image below is the dateline.</span></p>
<h2>Why is the Dateline Important?</h2>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">A proper dateline is important because it’s the first thing a reader and any journalist considering picking up your story will see. They want real, current news, and not something weeks out of date.</span></p>
<h2>Where Are Datelines Located in a Press Release?</h2>
<p>The dateline is found at the beginning of the press release. The highlighted portion in the image below is the dateline. </p>

<h2><b>How to Write a Professional AP Style Dateline</b></h2>
<p>When writing a professional AP Style dateline, the AP Stylebook says it should contain<span style="font-weight: 400;"> “a city name, entirely in capital letters, followed in most cases by the name of the state, county, or territory where the city is located.”</span></p>
<h2>AP Style Press Release Rules</h2>
<p>Below are AP Style press release rules to consider when creating your dateline.</p>
<p>Some large metropolitan areas do not require the state. These include:</p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">ATLANTA</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">BOSTON</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">CHICAGO</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">SAN FRANCISCO</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">SAN DIEGO</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">WASHINGTON</span></li>
</ul>
<p><b>If they do require a state, it’s important to use the correct AP style state abbreviation:</b></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">KANSAS CITY, Mo. (Missouri)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">PORTLAND, Ore. (Oregon)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">PORTLAND, Maine</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The AP Style abbreviations are not the same as postal ones and are not always consistent. </span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Here is a </span><a href="http://apstylebook.blogspot.com/2009/06/state-names.html"><span style="font-weight: 400;">quick list</span></a><span style="font-weight: 400;"> you can refer to. </span></p>
<p><b>Important abbreviations to remember include:</b></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Calif. (usually CA)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Colo. (CO)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Conn. (CT)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Fla. (FL)</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Despite having a US Postal Service abbreviation, there are eight state names that are </span><b>never abbreviated in AP Style,</b><span style="font-weight: 400;"> even if they’re accompanied by a city name.</span></p>
<p><b>They are:</b></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Alaska</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Hawaii</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Idaho</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Iowa</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Maine</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Ohio</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Texas</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Utah</span></li>
</ul>
<h3><b>AP Style Dates</b></h3>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The dates should always use Arabic numerals, without the st, nd, rd or th. (Ex: 2, 3, 25, not 2nd, 3rd, 25th).</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">The names of months should always be capitalized and spelled out when being used alone, or with the year alone. For example, January and January 2022.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When a month is used with a specific date, use only Jan., Feb., Aug., Sept., Oct., Nov., and Dec. should be abbreviated, for example, Jan. 31.</span></p>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">When a phrase lists only a month and year, they don’t need to be separated by commas.  </span><span style="font-weight: 400;">However, when you list a month, date, and year. The year should be separated by a comma.</span></p>
<ul>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Jan. 31, 2022 (BOSTON)</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Jan. 7, 2022– BOSTON</span></li>
<li style="font-weight: 400;" aria-level="1"><span style="font-weight: 400;">Jan. 7, BOSTON</span></li>
</ul>
<p><span style="font-weight: 400;">Because content tends to stay visible on the Internet long after it’s published, it’s best to include the year so there is no possible confusion about when the press release or news story was published.</span></p>
