			<h1 class="entry-title"><strong>Tips For Evaluating Health News Stories</strong></h1>
							<div class="entry-meta">
					<span class="posted-on">On <a href="https://sciencecommunicationbreakdown.wordpress.com/2022/09/13/tips-for-evaluating-health-news-stories/" rel="bookmark"><time class="entry-date published" datetime="2022-09-13T18:22:09+00:00">September 13, 2022</time><time class="updated" datetime="2022-09-13T20:16:48+00:00">September 13, 2022</time></a></span><span class="byline"> By <span class="author vcard"><a class="url fn n" href="https://sciencecommunicationbreakdown.wordpress.com/author/shiplives/">Matt Shipman</a></span></span>				</div><!-- .entry-meta -->
					</header><!-- .entry-header -->

		<div class="entry-content">
			
<p class="wp-block-paragraph">News stories that talk about health and medicine can offer valuable insights into our understanding of the human body, its failings, and emerging treatments related to our well-being. But health stories can also be confusing or misleading. That means that we should all be critical consumers of health news.</p>



<span id="more-3285"></span>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">For many years, HealthNewsReview.org was a valuable resource for helping the public navigate the health news landscape. Unfortunately, a lack of funding led to HealthNewsReview closing up shop in 2018 – and the site has now gone offline. I’m writing this because I want to share one of its most valuable tools.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Specifically, I am going to share the criteria that HealthNewsReview used to evaluate health news stories. These are guidelines that anyone can use to assess health news stories and, with some alterations, news releases related to health research.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">I’m including the criteria below, taken verbatim from the HealthNewsReview site (<a href="https://web.archive.org/web/20220707134559/https:/www.healthnewsreview.org/about-us/review-criteria/">retrieved via the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine</a>).</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">As HealthNewsReview wrote: <em>Our review criteria consist of 10 different elements that we think all health care news stories and all health care news (press) releases about interventions should include. We think these criteria address the basic issues that consumers need to know in order to develop informed opinions about these interventions–and how/whether they matter in their lives.</em></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #1 Does the story adequately discuss the costs of the intervention?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Our long-term experience with thousands of stories shows that the cost criterion gets the most unsatisfactory scores. News stories don’t help patients and consumers if they don’t tackle costs.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #2 Does the story adequately quantify the benefits of the intervention?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">New healthcare treatments should work well. When reading a story, people want to know: How effective is the intervention? Do the numbers back it up?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #3 Does the story adequately explain/quantify the harms of the intervention?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">In the news, if you’re only hearing about the potential benefits of a test or treatment, run for the hills. What are the harms, and how often do they occur?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #4 Does the story seem to grasp the quality of the evidence?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Ideally, healthcare interventions are subjected to rigorous testing to prove they work. When reading about a new intervention, device or diagnostic tool, people should come away with a sense of how rigorous the evidence is for the intervention.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #5 Does the story commit disease-mongering?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sometimes new conditions or diseases actually may just be opportunities to medicalize (and monetize) normal states of health. Readers aren’t well-served by stories that exaggerate or oversell conditions. [Note: Examples of this include turning risk factors into diseases, with the implication that, then, these must be treated (e.g., low bone mineral density becomes osteoporosis); or medicalization of normal states (aging, baldness, wrinkles, shyness).]</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #6 Does the story use independent sources and identify conflicts of interest?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Health care is rife with conflicts of interest. Readers deserve to know if the sources in the story have any conflicts of interest. (For example, do researchers stand to benefit financially from the treatment they are promoting? Are they paid consultants for the company that manufactures a relevant pharmaceutical?) Readers also deserve to hear the opinion of objective, independent third-party experts, who are vital to backstopping exaggerated claims.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #7 Does the story compare the new approach with existing alternatives?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Just because something is “new” in health care doesn’t mean it’s better. When reading about a new drug, test, surgery, etc., readers should come away informed that there are other options on the table.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #8 Does the story establish the availability of the treatment/test/product/procedure?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">New medical treatments often get lots of attention before they’re ever made available to the public. After reading a story about a new intervention, a person should be able to answer: Is it actually available to me?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #9 Does the story establish the true novelty of the approach?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Often something that’s lauded as new or novel really isn’t. Or it may be new, but it’s not better. So, when reading about a “new” medical intervention, people deserve to know: What is truly new and different about this idea?</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Criterion #10 Does the story appear to rely solely or largely on a news release?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Sometimes news stories rely solely or largely on a PR news release–and never disclose that the information came from a news release. Readers deserve to know if a story includes no original reporting.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>News Release Review Criteria</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The 10 criteria used to review news releases are almost identical to those above.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Criterion #6 is slightly different for news release reviews. It gives this guidance: <strong>Does the news release identify funding sources &amp; disclose conflicts of interest?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Potential conflicts of interests should be disclosed in an editor’s note included in the release.&nbsp; The funding for the project must also be disclosed along with any relationship that the investigators may have with that funder.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Another significant difference for #6 regards the use of independent sources: Unlike news stories, news releases are not expected to feature comment from an objective expert.</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">The other key difference is Criterion #10.&nbsp; That final criterion for news release reviews is this:</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph"><strong>Does the news release include unjustifiable, sensational language, including in the quotes of researchers?</strong></p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Red flags rise up in our eyes when we see terms such as:</p>



<ul class="wp-block-list">
<li>Breakthrough</li>



<li>could become the new standard of care</li>



<li>cure</li>



<li>first-of-its-kind</li>



<li>game-changer</li>



<li>Holy Grail</li>



<li>Magic</li>



<li>Miracle</li>



<li>simple blood test</li>



<li>this might/may/could lead to (which means, of course, that “might/may/could not” applies as well)</li>
</ul>



<p class="has-text-align-center wp-block-paragraph">***</p>



<p class="wp-block-paragraph">Okay, those are the HealthNewsReview criteria. And while they were developed to help readers assess health news stories and news releases, many of them are also useful tools for assessing stories about other fields of research. How much will that neat new tech cost? When will it become available? Do the researchers have relevant conflicts of interest? These are all good questions, and a useful guide for readers who want to assess news through a critical lens. I would like to close this post by thanking the founder of HealthNewsReview.org, Gary Schwitzer. He created an immensely valuable resource, and I am grateful for it. I was fortunate enough to work as a contributor to HealthNewsReview, and I learned a great deal from him and his assembled team of editors and experts. I’d also like to thank my primary editor there, Kevin Lomangino. Thanks for everything, Kevin.</p>
