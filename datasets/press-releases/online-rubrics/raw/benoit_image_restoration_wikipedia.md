					<div id="mw-content-text" class="mw-body-content"><div class="mw-subjectpageheader">
</div><div class="mw-content-ltr mw-parser-output" lang="en" dir="ltr"><div class="shortdescription nomobile noexcerpt noprint searchaux" style="display:none">Theory in crisis communication</div>
<p>Introduced by <a href="/wiki/William_Benoit" title="William Benoit">William Benoit</a>, <b>image restoration theory</b> (also known as <b>image repair theory</b>) outlines strategies that can be used to restore one's image in an event where reputation has been damaged. Image restoration theory can be applied as an approach for understanding both personal and organizational crisis situations. It is a component of <a href="/wiki/Crisis_communication" title="Crisis communication">crisis communication</a>, which is a sub-specialty of <a href="/wiki/Public_relations" title="Public relations">public relations</a>. Its purpose is to protect an individual, company, or organization facing a public challenge to its reputation.
</p><p>Benoit outlines this theory in <i>Accounts, Excuses, and Apologies: A Theory of Image Restoration Strategies</i>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Basic_concepts">Basic concepts</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Basic concepts"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Two components must be present in a given attack to the image of an individual or organization: 
</p>
<ol><li>The accused is held responsible for an action.</li>
<li>the act is considered offensive.</li></ol>
<p>Image restoration theory is grounded in two fundamental assumptions.
</p>
<ol><li><b>Communication is a goal-directed activity</b>. Communicators may have multiple goals that are not collectively compatible, but people try to achieve goals that are most important to them at the time, with reasonable cost.</li>
<li><b> Maintaining a favorable reputation is a key goal of communication</b>. Because face, image, or reputation is valued as important, individuals or organizations are motivated to take action when it is compromised.</li></ol>
<p>Perception is fundamental to image restoration, as the accused actor will not engage in a defensive strategy unless the perception exists that he is at fault. The actor who committed the wrongful act must decide on the strategy of the best course based on their specific situation. Factors such as credibility, audience perceptions, and the degree of offensiveness of the act must be taken into account.
</p><p>Some image repair factors include
</p>
<ol><li>Crisis responsibility – how much a company or person is responsible for in a crisis</li>
<li>Crisis type – what type of crisis is happening</li>
<li>Organizational reputation – what is the known reputation and behavior of a company or a person<sup id="cite&#95;ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li></ol>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Theoretical_framework">Theoretical framework</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Theoretical framework"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>The importance of image is one of the key factors in a brand, or persons, business. This is one of the most important factors when it comes to conflict management and resolution. The theory of image restoration builds upon theories of <a href="/wiki/Apologetics" title="Apologetics">apologia</a> and accounts. Apologia is a formal defense or justification of an individual's opinion, position, or actions,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> and an account is a statement made by an individual or organization to explain unanticipated or transgressive events.
</p><p>Benoit claims that these treatments of image restoration focus on identifying options rather than prescribing solutions. He grounds image restoration theory on a comprehensive literature review of apologia and accounts theories.
</p><p>Specific influences of image restoration theory include Rosenfield's (1968) theory of analog, Ware and Linkugel's (1973) theory of apologia;<sup id="cite&#95;ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Kenneth_Burke" title="Kenneth Burke">Kenneth Burke</a>'s (1970) theory of goals and purification; Ryan's (1982) kategoria and apologia; Scott and Lyman's (1968) analysis of accounts; <a href="/wiki/Goffman" class="mw-redirect" title="Goffman">Goffman</a>'s (1967) remedial moves; Schonbach's (1980) updated analysis of Scott and Lyman's (1968) theory; and Schlenker's (1980) analysis of impression management and accounts.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Typology_of_strategies">Typology of strategies</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Typology of strategies"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<table class="wikitable">

<tbody><tr>
<th>Strategy</th>
<th>Explanation
</th></tr>
<tr>
<td><b>Denial</b></td>
<td>The accused may <i>simply deny</i> that the act occurred, or <i><a href="/wiki/Scapegoating" title="Scapegoating">shift the blame</a></i> to the 'real' culprit.
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><b>Evading responsibility</b></td>
<td>When unable to deny performing the act in question, the accused may attempt to <a href="/wiki/Evasion_(ethics)" title="Evasion (ethics)">evade</a> responsibility. This strategy has four components.
<ul><li><b>Provocation</b>: the actor may claim that the act was committed in response to another wrongful act.</li>
<li><b>Defeasibility</b>: the actor <a href="/wiki/Plausible_deniability" title="Plausible deniability">pleads a lack of knowledge</a> or control over important factors related to the offensive act.</li>
<li><b>Unaccountability</b>: the actor may make an excuse for factors beyond their control.</li>
<li><b>Bona fide</b>: the actor asks not to be held fully responsible based on their <a href="/wiki/Good_faith" title="Good faith">good, rather than evil motives</a> in committing the act.</li></ul>
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><b>Reducing offensiveness</b></td>
<td>The accused may attempt to reduce the degree of negative feeling experienced by the audience. This strategy has six components.
<ul><li><b>Bolstering</b>: used to mitigate the negative effects by reinforcing the audience's positive idea of the accused. They may remind the audience of prior good acts.</li>
<li><b>Minimization</b>: attempts to <a href="/wiki/Downplaying" class="mw-redirect" title="Downplaying">convince the audience that the act in question is less serious</a> than it appears.</li>
<li><b>Differentiation</b>: the act is <a href="/wiki/Whataboutism" title="Whataboutism">distinguished from other, more offensive acts</a> in order to lessen the audience's negative sentiment by means of comparison.</li>
<li><b>Transcendence</b>: the act is placed in a broad context to place it in a different, less offensive frame of reference.</li>
<li><b>Ad hominem</b>: the actor <a href="/wiki/Ad_hominem" title="Ad hominem">attacks their accusers</a> or questions the credibility of the sources of the accusations.</li>
<li><b>Compensation</b>: the actor offers to redress the victims of their action in order to offset the negative sentiment that is being held against them.</li></ul>
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><b>Corrective action</b></td>
<td>The accused claims that they will correct the problem. This can involve restoring the situation to its prior state, or promising to make changes to prevent its reoccurrence.
</td></tr>
<tr>
<td><b>Mortification</b></td>
<td>The accused admits responsibility and asks for forgiveness.
</td></tr></tbody></table>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Case_studies_by_Benoit">Case studies by Benoit</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Case studies by Benoit"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Based on several case studies by Benoit and his colleagues,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> a number of prescriptive recommendations were cited for the use of crisis strategies:<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<ol><li>The dominant recommendation is for an organization to immediately admit fault/accept responsibility.</li>
<li>Corrective actions should be taken and an organization need to publicize those actions.</li>
<li>Bolstering, which is directly related to the charge, is the most effective strategy.</li>
<li>If the organization is innocent, denial is an effective strategy.</li></ol>
<p>"Image restoration theory is the dominant line of research generating these recommendations. The most common recommendations suggest using the mortification and corrective action crisis response when an organization is guilty."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Limitation_of_Image_repair_theory_–_Coombs"><span id="Limitation_of_Image_repair_theory_.E2.80.93_Coombs"></span>Limitation of Image repair theory – Coombs</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=6" title="Edit section: Limitation of Image repair theory – Coombs"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Even though image restoration theory represented the use of mortification (accepting responsibility) and corrective action, there might be alternative recommendations. For instance, his studies using <a href="/wiki/Situational_crisis_communication_theory" title="Situational crisis communication theory">situational crisis communication theory</a> found no support for always using mortification and corrective action. Also, the mortification and corrective action strategies had no greater effect than a simple bolstering strategy in a criminal violation crisis such as racial discrimination (Coombs, 2006<sup id="cite&#95;ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>). This theory can not be predicted.
</p><p>Additionally, in terms of the limitation of case studies in image restoration theory, Coombs <sup id="cite&#95;ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> argued that closer scrutiny with insights should be taken before offering strategies to crisis managers as facts. To gain additional insights into the use of crisis responses, he pointed out many similar crises should be examined for patterns of strategy use and effect, and "a large number of cases could be coded and subjected to log-linear analysis to identify patterns." (Coombs, 2006, p.&#160;191-192)<sup id="cite&#95;ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>19<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="The_Cola_Wars">The Cola Wars</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Image_restoration_theory&amp;action=edit&amp;section=7" title="Edit section: The Cola Wars"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p><a href="/wiki/Coca-Cola" title="Coca-Cola">Coca-Cola</a> and <a href="/wiki/Pepsi_Stuff" title="Pepsi Stuff">Pepsi's</a> longstanding competition<sup id="cite&#95;ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>20<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> reached its peak when Coke and Pepsi placed advertisements in Nation's Restaurant News with unmistakable attacks from both sides.
</p><p>Benoit analyses advertisements from both companies from 1990–1992 to address the persuasive strategies of Coke and Pepsi to determine recommendations for image restoration following an attack. He advises that companies should avoid making false claims, provide adequate support for claims, and develop themes throughout a campaign, and avoid arguments that might backfire.
</p>
