				<h2><a href="https://www.badscience.net/2014/12/my-bmj-editorial-on-academic-press-releases-routinely-misleading-the-public/" rel="bookmark" title="Permanent Link to My BMJ editorial: how can we stop academic press releases misleading the public?">My BMJ editorial: how can we stop academic press releases misleading the public?</a></h2>
				<p class="meta">
										December 10th, 2014  by Ben Goldacre 
					  in <a href="https://www.badscience.net/category/uncategorized/" rel="category tag">bad science</a> |   <a href="https://www.badscience.net/2014/12/my-bmj-editorial-on-academic-press-releases-routinely-misleading-the-public/#comments">9 Comments &#187;</a>									</p>
				<div class="entry">
					<p><a href="http://www.badscience.net/wp-content/uploads/Screenshot-2014-12-10-14.47.53.png"><img decoding="async" class="right size-medium wp-image-3316" src="http://www.badscience.net/wp-content/uploads/Screenshot-2014-12-10-14.47.53-249x300.png" alt="Screenshot 2014-12-10 14.47.53" width="160" srcset="https://www.badscience.net/wp-content/uploads/Screenshot-2014-12-10-14.47.53-249x300.png 249w, https://www.badscience.net/wp-content/uploads/Screenshot-2014-12-10-14.47.53.png 513w" sizes="(max-width: 249px) 100vw, 249px" /></a>There is an excellent research paper published today in the BMJ, showing that academic press releases routinely exaggerate scientific findings and mislead the public.</p>
<p>This is something I&#8217;ve often covered. In <a href="http://www.theguardian.com/commentisfree/2011/aug/05/bad-science-adjusting-figures">this story</a>, for example, the BMJ&#8217;s own press release about their own paper was hopelessly and entirely misleading. And after <a href="http://www.theguardian.com/science/2011/jul/29/duchennes-muscular-dystrophy-surrogate-outcomes">this story</a>, featuring a misleading press release from Great Ormond Street Hospital, the head of that institution wrote a paranoid and misguided defense (which I have proudly reprinted, in my <a href="http://www.amazon.co.uk/Think-Youll-Find-More-Complicated/dp/0007462484/?tag=bs0b-21">new book</a>, in full).<span id="more-3311"></span></p>
<p>Anyway, I wrote an editorial in the BMJ to accompany this new research paper, and I make a series of recommendations about how we can stop academics and their institutions misleading the public. These ideas revolve largely around transparency and shame. The research paper is free to access, my editorial is paywalled for now, but <a href="http://www.bmj.com/content/349/bmj.g7465.full?ijkey=pdGfXk42ClHVR4e&amp;keytype=ref">this link</a> will let you read it for free:</p>
<p><a href="http://www.bmj.com/content/349/bmj.g7465.full?ijkey=pdGfXk42ClHVR4e&#038;keytype=ref" class="autohyperlink">www.bmj.com/content/349/bmj.g7465.full?ijkey=pdGfXk42ClHVR4e&#038;keytype=ref</a></p>
<p>As a related note, I&#8217;m sad to see journalists like <a href="http://www.badscience.net/2009/07/steve-connor-is-getting-eggy/">Steve Connor</a> apparently accepting this paper as an <a href="http://www.independent.co.uk/news/science/bad-science-reporting-blamed-on-exaggerations-in-university-press-releases-9913336.html">excuse for bad science</a> reporting. I&#8217;m with Ed Yong, in this <a href="https://twitter.com/edyong209/status/542598409379934208">excellent comment</a>: readers don&#8217;t expect journalists to be secretaries. Readers expect journalists to add value by critically appraising the evidence for claims.</p>

<p>That occasion concerned memoirs from a former child soldier from Sierra Leone, Ismael Beah, published as a book &#8220;A long way gone&#8221;. </p>
<p>At the time, as far as Australia was concerned, Sierra Leone was a small third world country half a planet away, no one had heard of, and the book was hard to get.</p>
<p>So it was surprising when the national broadsheet newspaper spent a week headlining Beah as a fraud &#8211; and even went to the trouble of sending a special invetigating team to dig dirt.  </p>
<p> I  happened to have read Beah&#8217;s book, and let&#8217;s just say, the newspaper&#8217;s reporting was  very one sided, and unbalanced (we later learned, this is not uncommon, from that paper). I therefore took the paper to task, in the interests of accuracy, and balanced reporting. I fairly quickly received threatening emails from its senior reporter. </p>
<p>Sought help of the uni PR office. You would think that getting the truth out, or showing there was more than one way to read a book &#8211; would be important. But the PR office said &#8211; drop it. The  newspaper and its reporter have big reputations and are very influential. The Uni needs to keep on good terms with them. </p>
<p>So I dropped it. </p>
<p>The press office was presumably  doing as University management instructed. In terms of the press office&#8217;s field of responsibility, their strategy was sound. But for a scientist/academic, it was a shock. A shock, that uni management do not value truthfulness and accuracy above all else, as we do. They see other things, as more important. Truthfulness  is optional. It can be dispensed with, should need arise.</p>
