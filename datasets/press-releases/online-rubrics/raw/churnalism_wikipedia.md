<p><b>Churnalism</b> is the production of low-quality or unoriginal <a href="/wiki/News_article" class="mw-redirect" title="News article">news articles</a>, generally by <a href="/wiki/Paraphrase" title="Paraphrase">paraphrasing</a> other sources or <a href="/wiki/Press_release" title="Press release">press releases</a> to <a href="/wiki/Freeriding_(economics)" class="mw-redirect" title="Freeriding (economics)">avoid the need</a> for time-consuming research or <a href="/wiki/Fact-checking" title="Fact-checking">fact-checking</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-SJ&#95;1-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SJ-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Churnalism is often the result of understaffed journalists who do not have the resources for original news-gathering and checking sources.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Because it can quickly be churned out (and thus is less expensive to produce), churnalism has become more common due to the revenue lost with the rise of Internet news and decline in advertising, with a particularly steep fall in late 2015.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-greens&#95;4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-greens-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The term is a <a href="/wiki/Portmanteau" title="Portmanteau">portmanteau</a> of "churn" and "journalism", referring to the perceived "churning out" of content by the press; its creation has been credited to <a href="/wiki/BBC" title="BBC">BBC</a> journalist Waseem Zakir.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Harcup&#95;5-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Harcup-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>

<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Prevalence">Prevalence</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Churnalism&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Prevalence"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In his book <i><a href="/wiki/Flat_Earth_News_(book)" title="Flat Earth News (book)">Flat Earth News</a></i>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Davies&#95;FlatEarth&#95;2008&#95;6-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Davies_FlatEarth_2008-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> the British journalist <a href="/wiki/Nick_Davies" title="Nick Davies">Nick Davies</a> reported a study at <a href="/wiki/Cardiff_University" title="Cardiff University">Cardiff University</a> by <a href="/wiki/Justin_Lewis_(media_scholar)" title="Justin Lewis (media scholar)">Justin Lewis</a> and a team of researchers<sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> which found that 80% of the stories in Britain's quality press were not original and that only 12% of stories were generated by <a href="/wiki/Reporter" class="mw-redirect" title="Reporter">reporters</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-SJ&#95;1-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-SJ-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The result is a reduction of quality and accuracy, as the articles are open to manipulation and distortion.
</p><p>A 2016 study of 1.8 million articles published by the U.S. and international editions of the <a href="/wiki/HuffPost" title="HuffPost">HuffPost</a> found that only 44% were written by staff journalists and thus could be considered original reporting.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The journalist Waseem Zakir has been credited for coining the term <i>churnalism</i> while working for the BBC in 2008<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Harcup&#95;5-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Harcup-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> (however, Zakir himself recollects it being a decade earlier).<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="previously cited a dead, unarchived Twitter post (March 2026)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> According to Zakir, the trend towards this form of journalism involves reporters becoming more reactive and less proactive in searching for news: "You get copy coming in on the wires and reporters churn it out, processing stuff and maybe adding the odd local quote. It's affecting every newsroom in the country and reporters are becoming churnalists."
</p><p>An editorial on the matter in the <i><a href="/wiki/British_Journalism_Review" title="British Journalism Review">British Journalism Review</a></i> saw this trend as terminal for journalism, "a harbinger of the end of news journalism as we know it, the coroner's verdict can be nothing other than suicide."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Others, such as <a href="/wiki/Peter_Preston" title="Peter Preston">Peter Preston</a>, former editor of <i><a href="/wiki/The_Guardian" title="The Guardian">The Guardian</a></i>, see the issue as over-wrought, saying that there was never a <a href="/wiki/Golden_age" class="mw-redirect" title="Golden age">golden age</a> of journalism in which journalists were not subject to such pressures.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Nick Davies and <a href="/wiki/Roy_Greenslade" title="Roy Greenslade">Roy Greenslade</a> gave evidence on the matter to the <a href="/wiki/Culture,_Media_and_Sport_Committee" title="Culture, Media and Sport Committee">Culture, Media and Sport Committee</a> in 2009.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Churnalism does not only occur in newspapers. For example, <a href="/wiki/Chris_Anderson_(writer)" title="Chris Anderson (writer)">Chris Anderson</a>'s wide use of "writethroughs" in his book <i><a href="/wiki/Free:_The_Future_of_a_Radical_Price" class="mw-redirect" title="Free: The Future of a Radical Price">Free: The Future of a Radical Price</a></i> has been labelled churnalism.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Economic_causes">Economic causes</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Churnalism&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Economic causes"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Traditional newspapers have cut staff as their advertising revenue has declined because of competition from other media such as television and the <a href="/wiki/Internet" title="Internet">Internet</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>13<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> They no longer have sufficient staff to generate news stories by making the rounds of civic and business activities. <a href="/wiki/Local_newspaper" class="mw-redirect" title="Local newspaper">Local newspapers</a> and <a href="/wiki/Trade_magazine" title="Trade magazine">trade magazines</a> are commonly produced by only one or two staff and these rely upon stories which are increasingly brought to them by <a href="/wiki/Public_relations" title="Public relations">public relations</a> representatives, according to a senior <a href="/wiki/Public_relations_professional" class="mw-redirect" title="Public relations professional">public relations professional</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>14<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> When the matter was debated at the <a href="/wiki/Foreign_Press_Association" title="Foreign Press Association">Foreign Press Association</a>, it was agreed that there was a relationship between the numbers of PR staff employed and journalists unemployed.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-FPA&#95;15-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-FPA-15"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>15<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> There was a particularly steep fall in UK advertising revenue in the six months to March 2016, with the <i><a href="/wiki/Daily_Mail_%26_General_Trust" class="mw-redirect" title="Daily Mail &amp; General Trust">Daily Mail &amp; General Trust</a></i> issuing a warning to investors after its newspaper division reported a 29% fall in profits largely to a 13% decline in print advertising revenue; news <a href="/wiki/Media_commentator" class="mw-redirect" title="Media commentator">media commentator</a> Roy Greenslade said in response to this "print cliff fall" that newspapers had no future.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-greens&#95;4-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-greens-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Other commentators have said the modern journalism is increasingly being performed in a cheaper, high-volume way, describing the resulting product with derogatory terms such as <i>newszak</i> (combination of "news" and "<a href="/wiki/Elevator_music" title="Elevator music">muzak</a>"),<sup id="cite&#95;ref-16" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> <i><a href="/wiki/Infotainment" title="Infotainment">infotainment</a></i> and <i>junk-food journalism</i>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Speed">Speed</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Churnalism&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Speed"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In their book <i><a href="/wiki/No_Time_to_Think" title="No Time to Think">No Time to Think</a></i>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> authors <a href="/wiki/Howard_Rosenberg" title="Howard Rosenberg">Howard Rosenberg</a> and <a href="/w/index.php?title=Charles_S._Feldman&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Charles S. Feldman (page does not exist)">Charles S. Feldman</a> emphasised the prioritization of speed in degrading the quality of modern journalism. An example is given of the BBC guide for online staff which gives advice to ensure good quality but also the contradictory advice, "Get the story up as fast as you can&#160;... We encourage a sense of urgency—we want to be first."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-WISSB&#95;19-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-WISSB-19"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>19<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Combating_churnalism">Combating churnalism</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Churnalism&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: Combating churnalism"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Some organizations and tools have arisen to combat churnalism. In April 2013, the American <a href="/wiki/Sunlight_Foundation" title="Sunlight Foundation">Sunlight Foundation</a>, a non-profit organisation that advocates for openness and transparency, in partnership with the UK's <a href="/wiki/Media_Standards_Trust" title="Media Standards Trust">Media Standards Trust</a>, launched churnalism.com, an online tool to discover churn. It used a database of known press releases and compared the text of a submitted URL to determine what percentage of it was derived churn.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>20<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p><i><a href="/wiki/The_Register" title="The Register">The Register</a></i> commented that some level of "churnalism" is both normal and healthy for news organisations, but said it considered the Media Standards Trust linked to campaigns supported by "wealthy and powerful individuals and celebrities" in favour of "state control of the media" in the UK, and claimed there was significant irony in the Sunlight Foundation tool launch announcement itself being "uncritically churned by many of the usual suspects".<sup id="cite&#95;ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>21<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>In Australia, <a href="/wiki/ABC_(Australian_TV_channel)" class="mw-redirect" title="ABC (Australian TV channel)">ABC</a>, the nationwide <a href="/wiki/Public_broadcasting" title="Public broadcasting">public TV service</a>, airs a highly critical weekly 15-minute programme, <i><a href="/wiki/Media_Watch_(TV_program)" title="Media Watch (TV program)">Media Watch</a></i>, which regularly exposes churnalism, <a href="/wiki/Plagiarism" title="Plagiarism">plagiarism</a>, <a href="/wiki/Media_bias" title="Media bias">media bias</a> and unethical behaviour by journalists and <a href="/wiki/Talk_radio#Australia" title="Talk radio">radio talk-back</a> hosts.
</p>
