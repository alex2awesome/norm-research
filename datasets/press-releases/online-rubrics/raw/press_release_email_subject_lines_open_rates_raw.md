<li><strong>Subject lines with 9–13 words</strong> and 71+ characters have the <strong>highest open rates (up to 40.71%).</strong></li>
<li><strong>Phrases like “Study finds” and front-loaded brackets</strong> boost open and reply rates significantly.</li>
<li><strong>Exclusives and publication mentions</strong> drive higher engagement, yet they remain underutilized by most PR professionals.</li>
<li><strong>Title case outperforms sentence case</strong>, and higher reading levels (10th grade+) correlate with better open rates.</li>
<li><strong>Using journalist names in a subject line can negatively</strong> impact performance—focus on relevance to the beat instead.</li>

<p>As publications continue to cut staff and more SEOs flock to digital PR, the competition for journalists’ attention is fierce.</p>
<p>Even if you are <a href="https://www.buzzstream.com/blog/newsjacking/">newsjacking</a> a trending topic, chances are several PR teams are doing the same.</p>
<p>To put yourself in the best position to succeed, you need data to inform your decisions.</p>
<p>To get to the bottom of what makes the best subject line, I’ve analyzed over 6 million email subject lines from digital PR campaigns.</p>
<h3>A Quick Note on My Analysis</h3>
<p>I’m prioritizing open rates as the leading metric of success. I polled PRs, and there was a split on where the focus should be:</p>
<div class="screen"><img decoding="async" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/sl-poll.png" alt="what metrics do you most care about for a big subject line study?" /></div>
<p>Realistically, click data is the ideal metric; however, click tracking is not something PRs typically include in their emails, as it may lead to the email being flagged as spam (or the journalist adding the click tracking link to their story).</p>
<p>Although there are concerns about inflated open rate metrics due to Apple MPP and Gmail image caching, I still lean on open rates as the leading indicator in this study, given the large sample size. (Though as you will see, in most cases, open rate and reply rate align.)</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="What_is_the_Ideal_Word_Count_for_a_PR_Email_Subject_Line"></span>What is the Ideal Word Count for a PR Email Subject Line?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>Based on our research, <strong>the 9-13 word range achieves the best open rate at 40.20%,</strong> although it’s only slightly higher than the 14-30 word range with 39.97%.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-word-count.png" alt="email subject line word count - subject lines longer than nine words get better open rates" /></p>
<p>Keep in mind that depending on the email client and platform, recipients can only see a set number of words before the subject line gets cut off.</p>
<p>So, let’s quickly look at the breakdown of the character usage in email subject lines:</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-character-count.png" alt="email subject line char count - subject lines with more than 70 characters receive the best open rates" /></p>
<p>The ideal length, based on open rates, is more than<strong> 71 characters, with an open rate of 40.71%.</strong></p>
<p>(We do see a spike in reply rate of 8.61% with the smaller subject lines, but upon closer examination, the number of emails sent at 0-40 characters represents just 1.48% of the total sample size.)</p>
<p>Seventy-one characters align with the average 14-30 word count.</p>
<p>Why does this work so well?</p>
<p>If we look at the breakdown of character limits based on <a href="https://www.emailtooltester.com/en/blog/email-subject-lines-character-limit/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Email Tool Tester data</a> as they relate to average number of words seen, this 14-word, 71+ character aligns with ithe deal viewing window for desktop Gmail recipients:</p>

<p>To further support this, we surveyed our digital PR users’ data to understand where and who they email; <a href="https://www.buzzstream.com/blog/site-pr-engagement-study/">Gmail addresses were most frequently used.</a></p>
<p><strong>Therefore, it appears that optimizing for desktop on Gmail yields the best engagement.</strong></p>
<p>However, there are some strategic ways around character limits.</p>
<p>One way is to front-load your subject line with the most eye-catching information.</p>
<p>In the next section, I decided to look at some phrase patterns.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="How_Should_You_Phrase_Your_Email_Subject_Lines"></span>How Should You Phrase Your Email Subject Lines?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>Upon reviewing our data, I noticed some clear patterns in the subject lines.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-phrase-patterns.png" alt="email subject line phrase patterns - study finds get the best opens and replies" /></p>
<p>In our data, subject lines <strong>starting with “Study finds” achieve the best open rates and reply rates,</strong> at nearly 41% and 1.25%, respectively.</p>
<p>Second was “Data reveals” with a 37.55% open rate and a 0.88% reply rate.</p>
<p>The phrasing is one thing, but I&#8217;ve also noticed that our users tend to use structural patterns, such as brackets or special punctuation, in email subject lines.</p>
<p>Next, we’ll see how that kind of structure impacts opens and replies.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="How_to_Structure_PR_Email_Subject_Lines"></span>How to Structure PR Email Subject Lines?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>Based on our data, the <strong>subject lines that led with brackets saw the best open rates (52.08%) and reply rates (2.24%).</strong></p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/email-subject-line-structure.png" alt="email subject line structure - leading with bracketed text gets the highest opens and replies" /></p>
<p>Even trailing with a bracket seemed to help open (49.42%) and reply rates (1.60%).</p>
<p>The brackets may serve as a visual cue to help stand out in a crowded inbox.</p>
<p>This made me think of some other visual cues that I’ve tried in the past, which led me to consider emojis.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="Do_Emojis_Impact_Subject_Line_Engagement"></span>Do Emojis Impact Subject Line Engagement?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>And while emoji usage accounted for only a small percentage of our data, approximately 4%, it seemed to perform slightly better than others.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-emoji-1.png" alt="email subject line emoji usage - emails with emojis in the SL get higher opens and replies" /></p>
<p>I was expecting to see a more drastic difference between emoji usage vs non-usage, but they don’t seem to have a significant impact on journalists.</p>
<p>If you’re interested, the beer emoji performed the best (53.57%):</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-emoji.png" alt="emoji usage in email subject lines - beer glasses get the highest open rates" /></p>
<p>So, perhaps there are some stories and industries where it’s worth testing.</p>
<p>Emojis aside, there are some other ways to stand out in an inbox, and that’s personalization.</p>
<p>Let’s look at that next.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="Does_Personalizing_Email_Subject_Lines_Help_Stand_Out"></span>Does Personalizing Email Subject Lines Help Stand Out?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>When we say the word “personalization”, many factors come to mind. I wrote about 8 different <a href="https://www.buzzstream.com/blog/personalized-email-outreach/">personalized email outreach techniques</a> used within the email body copy.</p>
<p>For subject lines, the most common two ways of personalizing that I’ve come across are <strong>mentioning the recipient’s name or publication within the subject line</strong> (or both).</p>
<h3>Mentioning a Website/Publication</h3>
<p>When mentioning the publication, rates appear to skyrocket with a 50.81% open rate compared to 39.25% when there isn’t any mention.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-website-name.png" alt="email subject lines mentioning a website name - mentioning a website in the subject line performs better" /></p>
<p>However, only 1% of our users mention a website in their outreach subject lines. So, there may be a missed opportunity here (albeit a small sample size).</p>
<p>The ones that did seem to include it when they mention “exclusives”, which we’ll get to later in this analysis.</p>
<h3>Mentioning a Person’s Name</h3>
<p>Based on our data, <strong>mentioning a name has a lower performance rate (38.45%)</strong> compared to subject lines that focus on the story.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/subject-line-person-name.png" alt="email subject lines mentioning a person's name - not mentioning the name performs better" /></p>
<p>Mentioning a name can come off as over-personalized and too templated, which may put journalists off.</p>
<p>In our dataset, less than 1% of users put the name into the subject line. The ones that are mentioned are again mentioned in conjunction with “[Exclusive]”.</p>
<p>It seems like the Exclusive is a way to get journalists’ attention, so let’s look at that next.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="Do_Exclusives_Perform_Better"></span>Do Exclusives Perform Better?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>When comparing open rates, <strong>exclusives have a clear advantage in opens,</strong> at 54.16% as compared to those without (37.16%).</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/exclusive.png" alt="exclusives outperform non-exclusives"/></p>
<p>This may seem obvious once you see the numbers.</p>
<p>But if it’s so obvious, why don’t more PRs use exclusives?</p>
<p>Overall, just 13.38% of pitches in our dataset mention exclusives.</p>
<p><strong>I know that some PRs feel that exclusives limit coverage, but given the ever-shrinking pool of journalists and publications, I believe that exclusives may be the key to success in this context.</strong></p>
<p>But, there are a few more angles I’d like to highlight before we draw some overall conclusions.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="Does_Title_Case_or_Sentence_Case_Matter"></span>Does Title Case or Sentence Case Matter?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>When I was with Siege Media, we conducted a study and found that sentence case performed best for the types of link building we did in 2016.</p>
<p>For those of you who are unsure, here’s the quick key:</p>
<ul>
<li>Sentence case doesn’t capitalize every word.</li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li>Title Case Capitalizes Every Word.</li>
</ul>
<p>So, let’s see if this trend still holds:</p>
<p>Based on this data, it appears that <strong>title case outperforms sentence case</strong>, with an open rate of 40.75% and a reply rate of 1.46%, compared to 35.03% and 1.01% for sentence case, respectively.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" class="graph" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/title-case.png" alt="email subject line title case vs sentence case" /></p>
<p>This is a minor detail that seems to have a significant impact.</p>
<p>Over 78.43% of our users use sentence case, while the other ~22% use title case (a very small percentage use all caps, which I don’t recommend).</p>
<p>Next, I wanted to consider something that might come into play when journalists are scanning their inboxes: reading comprehension.</p>
<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="Does_Reading_Level_Impact_Subject_Line_Open_Rates"></span>Does Reading Level Impact Subject Line Open Rates?<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>I hypothesize that, given journalists have less time, they will be more inclined to open a subject line that is easy to read and comprehend.</p>
<p>I analyzed our dataset using the <a href="https://readable.com/readability/flesch-reading-ease-flesch-kincaid-grade-level/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Flesch-Kincaid Reading Ease</a> score, which ranges from 1 to 100 (with 100 being the most complex), based on word complexity.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" width="1080" height="1080" class="alignnone size-full wp-image-10379" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1.png" alt="email subject lines the flesch reading ease score" srcset="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1.png 1080w, https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1-300x300.png 300w, https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1-1024x1024.png 1024w, https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1-150x150.png 150w, https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/reading-score-1-768x768.png 768w" sizes="(max-width: 1080px) 100vw, 1080px" /></p>
<p>As you can see, <strong>the best open rates begin at the 10th-grade reading level (40.54%) and peak at college and beyond (41.22%).</strong></p>
<p>To help illustrate the range, here are some examples:</p>
<ul>
<li>5th Grade &#8211; Bringing Back Y2K &#8211; How to get Zendaya&#8217;s Coachella look for less</li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li>8th-9th Grade &#8211; AI Tools Are Changing How Teens Study — But Are They Helping?</li>
</ul>
<ul>
<li>College+ &#8211; Urban Migration Trends: Why Gen Z Is Reshaping Housing Demand in Midwestern Cities</li>
</ul>
<p>While recommendations from the UX research site NNGroup recommended writing web content for the <a href="https://www.nngroup.com/articles/writing-for-lower-literacy-users/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">6th grade reading level</a>, it seems like that doesn’t carry over to subject lines, at least not for journalists.</p>
<p>I assume this is because educated journalists most likely have a higher literacy level than the average reader.</p>
<p>However, realistically, this may ultimately come down to how a journalist or publication writes its headlines.</p>
<p>Best practice when pitching is to try to emulate the headline writing style of your target journalist. Therefore, it is possible that more publications are being pitched by journalists who write in a more formal style.</p>
<p>(I may also do some digging into the headline writing style of major publishers to see if there is any correlation. Let me know if you think this is a good idea for a post!)</p>
<p>Upon breaking down the usage, it appears that more PR professionals prefer a reading level of 10th-12th grade.</p>
<p><strong>College+:</strong> 17.67%</p>
<p><strong>10th-12th Grade:</strong> 28.86%</p>
<p><strong>8th-9th Grade:</strong> 24.92%</p>
<p><strong>6th-7th Grade:</strong> 16.58%</p>
<p><strong>≤5th Grade:</strong> 11.97%</p>
<p>So, there is some alignment there, but PRs may be able to go even higher.</p>

<h2><span class="ez-toc-section" id="The_Perfect_Subject_Line_is_Relevant_to_the_Beat"></span>The Perfect Subject Line is Relevant to the Beat<span class="ez-toc-section-end"></span></h2>
<p>The best way to stand out is to deliver a <strong>pitch that is relevant to a journalist.</strong></p>
<p>We’ve found that a highly targeted approach to building email lists <a href="https://www.buzzstream.com/blog/spray-and-pray-study/">yields the best open rates, replies, and coverage rates.</a></p>
<p>Furthermore, both <a href="https://muckrack.com/research/the-state-of-journalism" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Muck Rack’s State of Journalism 2025</a> and <a href="https://www.cision.com/resources/guides-and-reports/2025-state-of-the-media-report/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cision’s State of the Media 2025</a> highlight that journalists ignore non-relevant pitches.</p>
<p>Journalists will sometimes go as far as to block you from subsequent pitches when they aren’t relevant, limiting your pool even further for later campaigns (not to mention giving the PR industry a bad name).</p>
<p>So, before you craft your subject line, be sure you are taking the time to understand a journalist’s beat and if they are still actively covering it.</p>
<p>For instance, say I’m looking for journalists who cover the Love Island reality TV show.</p>

<div class="screen"><img decoding="async" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/liza-esquibias.png" alt="liza esquibias writer" /></div>
<p>Unfortunately, I don’t find much there, so I’d look at her recent articles.</p>
<div class="screen"><img decoding="async" src="https://www.buzzstream.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/06/liza-esquibias-recent-articles.png" alt="liza esquibias recent articles" /></div>
<p>With recent articles, I have a better sense that she’s covered two Love Island stories recently.</p>
<p>This process helps guarantee a more targeted result.</p>
