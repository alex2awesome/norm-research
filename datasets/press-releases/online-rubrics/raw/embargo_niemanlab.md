<p><iframe width="500" height="300" src="https://www.dipity.com/edyong209/Reprogrammed-stem-cells/?mode=embed&#038;bgcolor=%23800000&#038;bgimg=/images/black_grad_up.png#tl" style="border:1px solid #CCC;"></iframe></p>
<p>The events that science journalists publish about most frequently are themselves acts of publishing: the appearance of research papers in peer-reviewed journals. Most journals embargo papers before publication, granting reporters access to unpublished work in exchange for an agreement not to report until the embargo is lifted. Embargoes give reporters time to study new research and seek out commentary from authoritative voices; they also allow journals to exercise power over reporters and to guard their control over the flow of scientific information. Reporters who break embargoes risk losing access to information about new findings, emerging technologies, and exciting discoveries — along with the chance to process and vet those findings to determine whether excitement is warranted.</p>
<p><a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Rennie_(editor)">John Rennie</a>, the former editor-in-chief of Scientific American, is hardly alone in his frustration with the fickle and ever-shifting embargo practices of scientific journals. <a href="http://www.guardian.co.uk/science/2011/jan/26/science-online-2011-journalism-blogs">In a January 26 column in the Guardian</a>, Rennie argues that embargoes encourage superficial and premature reporting on new science. &#8220;Out of fear of being scooped,&#8221; he writes, news outlets rush their coverage, &#8220;publish[ing] stories on the same research papers at the moment the embargo ends. In that stampede of coverage, opportunities for distinctive reporting are few.&#8221; As a kind of thought experiment, Rennie suggests that science journalism could answer with self-imposed embargoes, in which news outlets would agree not to report on new journal papers until six months after publication.</p>
<p>As Rennie admits, that isn&#8217;t going to happen. Instead, he encourages journalists to experiment with new ways of enriching reporting between embargoes, shooting the gaps with coverage that offers nuance and a broadened perspective from which to judge the significance of new findings.</p>
<p><span id="more-30260"></span></p>
<div class="subhead">Consciously looking for context</div>
<p>Having seen John Rennie speak about the problems of embargo-driven journalism at the <a href="http://scienceonline2011.com/">ScienceOnline 2011</a> conference last month, British science writer <a href="http://twitter.com/#!/edyong209">Ed Yong</a> cast about for a way to add context to his coverage of stem cell research, a beat he covers frequently for Wired, Discover, and New Scientist, among other venues. As Paul Raeburn reports at MIT&#8217;s <a href="http://ksjtracker.mit.edu/2011/02/08/beyond-paper-of-the-week-beyond-multimedia-taking-science-coverage-in-new-directions/">Knight Science Journalism Tracker</a>, Yong crafted a timeline to document the field&#8217;s major stories from the last few years. Using a free web-based timeline creator he found at <a href="http://www.dipity.com/edyong209/Reprogrammed-stem-cells/">Dipity.com</a>, Yong assembled articles from major journals and coverage from science news outlets into an annotated history of the discoveries that have shaped the field. Yong calls his timeline a tool for &#8220;looking at the stories that lead up to new discoveries, rather than focusing on every new paper in isolation.&#8221; <a href="http://blogs.discovermagazine.com/notrocketscience/2011/02/02/research-into-reprogrammed-stem-cells-an-interactive-timeline/">Posted</a> at Yong&#8217;s Discover-hosted blog <a href="http://blogs.discovermagazine.com/notrocketscience/">Not Exactly Rocket Science</a>, the timeline is a rich and engaging piece of analysis. It also serves Yong as a resource for further reporting, giving him a baseline from which to judge the significance of emerging science before it comes out from behind its embargoes.</p>
<p>Yong&#8217;s tool offers another example of the <a href="http://www.niemanlab.org/tag/future-of-context/">future-of-context</a> ideas we write about often here at the Lab — like <a href="http://www.niemanlab.org/2011/01/mojos-egypt-explainer-future-of-context-ideas-in-action/">explainer pages</a> and <a href="http://www.niemanlab.org/2009/06/matt-thompson-on-adding-context-and-depth-to-how-we-report-news/">building background into stories</a>, issues that apply across all beats and topics. Similarly, Yong turns a tried and true model of information visualization — the timeline — into a tool for putting any given story in stem cell research into its proper light. And rather neatly, he does it with time as the axis — for time, after all, is precisely what embargoes are all about. It&#8217;s just one example, but it&#8217;s a conscious attempt to break out of the imposed news cycle of embargo-driven reporting.</p>
<div class="subhead">The Ingelfinger Rule</div>
<p>In fact, science journalists are squeezed at both ends of the journals&#8217; publishing cycles. In addition to levying embargoes, many journals also observe the so-called <a href="http://cogprints.org/1703/1/harnad00.lancet.htm">Ingelfinger Rule</a>, refusing to publish research that has been reported or commented on elsewhere. Named for former New England Journal of Medicine editor <a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_J._Ingelfinger">Franz Ingelfinger</a>, the rule was formulated to keep untested health-science findings from making their way into public sphere before being submitted to the peer-review process — what some call &#8220;<a href="http://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_by_press_conference">science by press conference</a>.&#8221; But the rule more obviously helps journals protect their revenue sources — and it is for this reason that it has been widely adopted by most science publishers, even those who operate in fields with no public-health ramifications. (The cost of those journals — <a href="http://www.wired.com/wiredscience/2010/12/the-head-of-the-harvard-library-system-is-pissed/">$27,465 for a year&#8217;s subscription to The Journal of Comparative Neurology!</a> — has even the most resource-rich libraries up in arms.)</p>
<p>Ivan Oransky agrees that Yong&#8217;s tool is a simple and effective answer to the challenge presented by the journals&#8217; squeeze tactics, calling it &#8220;terrific&#8221; and &#8220;scalable.&#8221; Oransky, who is executive editor of Reuters Health and an MD on the faculty at the NYU School of Medicine, runs the blog <a href="http://embargowatch.wordpress.com/">Embargo Watch</a>, where he covers the uses and misuses of embargo practices in careful detail (and which John Rennie praised in his <a href="http://blogs.plos.org/retort/2011/01/26/improving-science-journalism/">remarks</a> at the ScienceOnline meeting). And he echoes Rennie&#8217;s call for finding ways to do science reporting outside the restrictions imposed by journals. &#8220;Journals serve a purpose,&#8221; Oransky told me in an email, &#8220;by applying the imperfect but valuable filter of peer review. We&#8217;d all like to get away from such heavy reliance on them.&#8221; With embargoes and the Ingelfinger Rule, he argues, journals exercise a &#8220;chilling effect on communication between scientists — many publicly funded — and journalists,&#8221; frustrating reporters who try &#8220;to move science reporting upstream to cover science <em>before</em> it&#8217;s in one of the journals.&#8221;</p>
<p>In science journalism&#8217;s crowded ecosystem, the double-barreled threat of embargo and the Ingelfinger Rule can have a deranging effect, pressuring serious news outlets to compete for scoops with online aggregators and casual bloggers. And <a href="http://www.nature.com/news/2011/110119/full/469286a.html">as scientists themselves join the fray in blogs or through the social media</a>, the veneer of decorum and collegiality imposed by embargoes is becoming increasingly illusory. On its own, an analytic tool like Ed Yong&#8217;s won&#8217;t break the deranging control that journals exercise over science coverage. But in striving to report on the practice of science as well as published results, Yong&#8217;s combination of web-based publishing tools and knowledgable reporting makes for a node on a promising timeline.</p>
