<H2>Journal Embargoes: So Often Impeached, Yet Never Convicted</H2>

<P><I>by Howard J. Lewis</I></P>

<P>You would have thought that all the pros and cons of the embargo
system used to defer public announcement of journal articles had
been thoroughly explored. There was the Ingelfinger Rule of the
<I><A HREF="www.nejm.org">New England Journal of Medicine</A></I>,
and, later,&#151;in response to continuing complaints from reporters,
the Relman Clarification, and, more recently, the Kassirer Elucidation.
In its issue of 31 October 1996, <I><A HREF="www.nature.com">Nature</A></I>
had spelled out for both scientists and journalists the hazards
of reporting major scientific discoveries before publication:</P>

<BLOCKQUOTE>
  <P><IMG SRC="EMBARGO.GIF" WIDTH="264" HEIGHT="162" ALIGN="RIGHT"
  BORDER="0" NATURALSIZEFLAG="3"><I>Nature&#146;s</I> guidelines
  for potential authors at [scientific conferences] are clear-cut
  in principle: talk to other researchers as much as you wish,
  but do not encourage or risk premature publication by discussion
  with the press, beyond your formal presentation. That advice
  may jar with those (including most researchers and all journalists)
  who see the freedom of information as a good thing. But it embodies
  a longer-term view: that publication in a peer-reviewed journal
  is the appropriate culmination of any piece of original research
  and an essential prerequisite for public discussion.</P>
</BLOCKQUOTE>

<P>And just two years later, in its 10 October 30 1998 issue,
<I>Science</I> editor Floyd Bloom, after warning prospective contributors
to forgo previous publication in print or on the Internet, said
pretty much the same thing:</P>

<BLOCKQUOTE>
  <P><A HREF="http://www.sciencemag.org/"><I>Science</I></A> holds
  the view that&#133;organized dissemination of important information
  through the public press leads to better quality coverage and
  public scrutiny of the achievements of scientific research. The
  embargo period provides sufficient time for reporters to analyze
  and report on the often complex stories behind the data&#133;
  Conclusive evidence that a reporter has knowingly broken our
  embargo will result in his or her exclusion from future embargoed
  information. Authors who fail to keep their manuscripts privileged
  risk annulment of acceptance&#133;</P>
</BLOCKQUOTE>

<P>What made that issue of <I>Science</I> extraordinary was a
10-page spread by Eliot Marshall and James Glanz on the embargo
phenomenon in all its intricacies and complexities (full text
available at <A HREF="www.sciencemag.org/cgi/content/full/282/5390/860">www.sciencemag.org/cgi/content/full/282/5390/860</A>).</P>

<P>Since most embargo systems are imposed by publications on the
media rather than through mutual agreement, Rick Borchelt of Vanderbilt
University, and Lynne Friedmann of Friedmann Communications (and
managing editor of <I>ScienceWriters</I>), organized a workshop
at the 1999 NASW meeting to (a) look at one embargo arrangement
that had been crafted between scientists and reporters by mutual
agreement and (b) invite staff from <I>Nature</I>, <I>Science</I>,
and the National Academy of Sciences to respond to issues raised
by reporters about their embargo procedures.</P>

<H3>For Their Mutual Convenience</H3>

<P>Recalling a different era, Dave Perlman of the <I>San Francisco
Chronicle</I>, and Cristine Russell of the <I>Washington Post</I>
described an extraordinary agreement fashioned by the organizers
of the 1975 Asilomar Conference on Recombinant DNA and a group
of about 16 reporters. The conference itself was extraordinary.
First, it dealt with a research advance almost mythical in its
potential impact&#151;a relatively simple procedure for rearranging
the genetic structure of living matter. Second, it brought together
a group of potential Nobelists contemplating an unprecedented
moratorium on that line of research until its relative safety
could be demonstrated.</P>

<P>The conference planners envisioned limiting press attendance
to <I>Science</I> and <I>Nature</I>, but both the subject and
venue of the meeting were already known to reporters. Responding
to the ensuing pressure, the organizers proposed that press attendance
be limited to reporters willing to accept an embargo on all publication
until the conclusion of the three-day meeting. Some 16 signed
onto that agreement, press briefings were held twice daily, and
the embargo stood firm, even despite the appearance of an Associated
Press story midway through the meeting based on an isolated report
that appeared in a local daily.</P>

<P>Both Perlman and Russell held positive recollections of the
affair, noting that benefits to the reporters included not only
extraordinarily accurate and lucid coverage but also at least
one major writing award, several successful books, and a marriage
of two participants who met in the press room. [A detailed account
of press arrangements at the Asilomar conference was published
in the February 1985 issue of ScienceWriters.]</P>

<P>A succeeding panel on the current system was less positive.
Robert Lee Hotz of the <I>Los Angeles Times</I> asked if the system
hadn&#146;t broken down. He read off a depressing record of recent
events:</P>

<BLOCKQUOTE>
  <P>Last November, <I><A HREF="http://www.sciam.com/">Scientific
  American</A></I> printed as its lead item a report on an embargoed
  research paper in <I>Nature Medicine</I> by scientists at the
  Salk Institute about the ability of human brain cells to regenerate.
  According to lead researcher Rusty Gage and the editors of <I>Nature
  Medicine</I>, <I>Scientific American</I> broke both the embargo
  with <I>Nature</I> and a personal agreement with Gage, who consented
  to an interview with the magazine on the condition that it did
  not run until after the paper was made public.</P>
  <P>Several days later, <I>U.S. News &amp; World Report</I> scooped
  the journal <I>Nature</I> on an embargoed study of Thomas Jefferson
  and the paternity of the descendants of Sally Hemings. <I>U.S.
  News</I> had the story by its own enterprise, all agree. Even
  so, Nature moved its embargo up to that Sunday, robbing <I>U.S.
  News</I> of its exclusive. The timing of the release&#151;on
  the eve of a critical US mid-term election&#151;prompted accusations
  that the <I>Nature</I> scientists were playing politics.</P>
  <P>In mid-November, <I>Nature</I> again moved an embargo, this
  time concerning the discovery of dinosaur eggs and embryos. <I>Nature
  </I>lifted its news embargo three days early to coincide with
  a press conference by <I><A HREF="http://www.nationalgeographic.com/">National
  Geographic</A></I> and The American Museum of Natural History.
  <I>Nature</I>, however, ignored the fact that <I>National Geographic</I>
  had already featured the research on its cover in an issue that
  had reached subscribers the week before.</P>
  <P>It was the second time in six months that <I>National Geographic</I>
  imposed an embargo on the general press that left its commercial
  partners free to exploit the same information without competition.
  Last summer, the magazine embargoed news of the discovery of
  the wreck of the aircraft carrier Yorktown, even though, through
  its partnership with National Public Radio, it had already started
  broadcasting a three-part series on the research and its Web
  site had been covering the discovery for weeks.</P>
  <P>Even as Nature was sliding back its release date to accommodate
  <I>National Geographic</I>, the <I>Proceedings of the National
  Academy of Sciences</I> moved the release date of a research
  paper on angiogenesis forward almost two months to coincide with
  an embargoed research paper being published in <I>Science</I>.
  All the <I>PNAS</I> editors had on hand was an uncorrected manuscript;
  even so, they released it&#151;as best as one can tell&#151;to
  avoid being upstaged by a competitor.</P>
  <P>In December, the <I>Daily Express</I> broke the embargo on
  research scheduled for publication in <I>Science</I> about the
  success of Japanese researchers in cloning cattle. The newspaper
  and the reporter involved obtained their advance copies of the
  research through the <I><A HREF="http://www.eurekalert.org/">EurekAlert!</A></I>
  Internet site, which provides advance copies of research from
  more than 30 peer-reviewed journals and embargoed press releases
  from universities to some 2,000 science writers who have registered
  with the site. Then, on the explicit instructions of a <I>Daily
  Express</I> assignment editor, the science reporter deliberately
  broke the embargo.</P>
  <P>After first confirming the details of the embargo break with
  the newspaper and the reporter, <I>EurekAlert!</I> suspended
  the <I>Daily Express</I>, five staff reporters and two freelancers
  who work there from receiving embargoed news through the Web
  site for six months. I do not know what - if anything, <I>Science</I>
  did on its own to enforce its embargo.</P>
  <P>Last January, Geron scientists and collaborators at the University
  of Texas Southwestern Medical Center published in <I>Science</I>
  their success in using the telomerase gene to extend the life
  span of human cells grown in culture. Normally the announcement
  of the results would have been withheld until the end of the
  trading day on the eve of publication, but the Alliance for Aging
  Research broke the embargo by describing the experiments in a
  press release. On the day the embargo was broken, the stock climbed
  43 percent.</P>
  <P>In at least three other documented cases in recent months,
  embargoed research scheduled for publication in the <I>New England
  Journal of Medicine</I>, the <I><A HREF="http://www.ama-assn.org/public/journals/jama/jamahome.htm">Journal
  of the American Medical Association</A></I> and <I>Science</I>
  has leaked to stock analysts well before the embargoed release
  dates. That resulted in a significant run-up in stock prices
  that allowed a privileged few to profit from the public&#146;s
  ignorance.</P>
</BLOCKQUOTE>

<P>Hotz expressed dissatisfaction with the response of the <I>New
England Journal</I> when queried: that policing stockbrokers was
not their problem. Hotz argued, &#147;If the issues are [getting
to stockbrokers] early, then the <I>New England Journal</I> embargo
is meaningless because the research is already public days before
the official release date&#133;</P>

<P>&#147;Why&#133;should any reporter honor an embargo system
if the organization setting the restriction does not&#133;hold
the embargoed information as tightly as possible?</P>

<P>&#147;&#133;In this age of instantaneous Internet communications,
on-line trading and 24-hour news cycles, we no longer may have
the luxury of waiting until material is actually published in
print prematurely or disclosed in a formal broadcast. The <I>Los
Angeles Times</I> will consider an embargo broken the instant
it is apparent that the news has traveled outside the circle of
those who have agreed to the embargo. And we want that circle
to be as small as possible&#133;.</P>

<P>&#147;The problem before us is not that embargoes get broken&#133;[T]he
system operates surprisingly well, [but] as the recent record
shows, the journals often are playing games with the embargo system.
So our problem may be that reporters and editors do not sanction
those journals that do not themselves take the embargo system
seriously enough.</P>

<P>&#147;I suspect that peer-review publications may be afraid
to enforce serious sanctions against offending networks or other
major news outlets because that would effectively cost them the
free publicity the arrangement is designed to generate for them&#133;</P>

<P>Hotz concluded, &#147;Perhaps this is something that the NASW
as a body might care to address.&#148;</P>

<P>Doug Levy, a technology reporter for <I>USA Today</I>, agreed
with Hotz and added a distinction between &#147;real&#148; embargoes
and those that were merely self-serving&#151;such as a Sunday
6 p.m. release on a Congressional report that was obviously aimed
at network news programs. In such cases, he said, embargoes were
ignored and competing media duly notified. He had similar reactions
to publisher&#146;s embargoes on books that had already been distributed
to book stores. Finally, he argued, embargoes did not work on
stories too hot to hold onto&#151;such as an attempt by the <I>Journal
of the American Medical Association</I> to embargo a story about
firing its own editor. [See pp. 9-11]</P>

<H3>The Journals Respond</H3>

<P>Monica M. Bradford, <I>Science</I> managing editor, said that
any media violating an embargo would no longer receive advance
texts unless it acknowledged its misdeed with a letter of apology.
She added, however, that embargo breakdowns are often not the
result of media malfeasance, but that of some other organization&#151;the
research site, funding organization, or scientific society. These
violations, she acknowledged, are harder to punish. As for prior
presentation at meetings, she said that <I>Science</I> allows
scientists to talk to media at the meeting, but not to hand out
copies of the paper or slides: &#147;We want the scientists to
decide where they want to publish.&#148; [Cris Russell noted later
that there was a &#147;fine line between &#145;author&#146;s choice&#146;
and intimidation.&#148;] Bradford added that <I>Science</I> is
considering publishing papers on the Internet as soon as they
are ready for publication; then, embargoes would be timed by the
paper&#146;s electronic appearance (possibly with a different
stated hour for each time zone!).</P>

<P>Laura Garwin, speaking for <I>Nature</I>, declared that the
journal takes abuse of embargoes most seriously: &#147;Let us
know of any violation; sanctions will surely follow!&#148; She
stressed the embargoed journal&#146;s benefits to reporters: It
saved reporters from having to wade through endless preprints
on the Web, and journal publication provided immediacy to an otherwise
indeterminate discovery process, enabling a reporter to declare:
&#147;It was announced today&#133;.&#148; In response to a complaint
in the audience, she said that <I>Nature</I> now plans to notify
pertinent public relations offices of research universities and
supporting agencies of embargoed releases.</P>

<P>Susan Turner-Lowe, director of the National Academy of Sciences
news office, suggested that the acceptance by over a thousand
reporters of an embargo agreement with the <I>Proceedings of the
National Academy of Sciences</I> implied &#147;a new world order,&#148;
made even more telling by Rick Weiss&#146;s report that the <I>Washington
Post</I> now saves news holes in Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday
editions for embargoed articles from the major weekly journals.</P>

<P>Routine issuance of embargoed news releases drew criticism
from Doug Levy on the grounds that they were likely to be tossed
without reading by reporters unable to keep up with the daily
flood. Turner-Lowe reported complaints from some press contacts
about announcements issued for immediate release. She invited
the media to reach agreement on the matter and let her know.</P>
