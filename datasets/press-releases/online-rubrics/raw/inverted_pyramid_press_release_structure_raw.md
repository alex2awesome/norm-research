<p>The <b>inverted pyramid</b> is a <a href="/wiki/Metaphor" title="Metaphor">metaphor</a> used by <a href="/wiki/Journalist" title="Journalist">journalists</a> and other writers to illustrate how information should be prioritised and structured in <a href="/wiki/Prose" title="Prose">prose</a> (e.g., a news report). It is a common method for writing <a href="/wiki/News_style" title="News style">news stories</a> and has wide adaptability to other kinds of texts, such as blogs, editorial columns and marketing factsheets. It is a way to communicate the basics about a topic in the initial sentences. The inverted pyramid is taught to <a href="/wiki/Mass_communication" title="Mass communication">mass communication</a> and journalism students, and is systematically used in <a href="/wiki/English-speaking_world" title="English-speaking world">English-language</a> media.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>The inverted or upside-down pyramid can be thought of as a triangle pointing down. The widest part at the top represents the most substantial, interesting, and <a href="/wiki/Importance" title="Importance">important</a> information that the writer means to convey, illustrating that this kind of material should head the article, while the tapering lower portion illustrates that other material should follow in order of diminishing importance.
</p><p>It is sometimes called a <b>summary news lead</b> style,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-errico&#95;2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-errico-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> or <b>bottom line up front</b> (<a href="/wiki/BLUF_(communication)" title="BLUF (communication)">BLUF</a>).<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>  The opposite, the failure to mention the most important, interesting or attention-grabbing elements of a story in the opening paragraphs, is called <i><a href="/wiki/Burying_the_lead" class="mw-redirect" title="Burying the lead">burying the lead</a></i>.
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Purpose">Purpose</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Inverted_pyramid_(journalism)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Purpose"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Other styles are also used in news writing, including the "anecdotal lead", which begins the story with an eye-catching tale or <a href="/wiki/Anecdote" title="Anecdote">anecdote</a> rather than the central facts; and the <a href="/wiki/FAQ" title="FAQ">Q&amp;A</a>, or question-and-answer format. The inverted pyramid may also include a "hook" as a kind of prologue, typically a provocative quote, question, or image, to entice the reader into committing to reading the full story.
</p><p>This format is valued for two reasons. First, readers can leave the story at any point and understand it, even if they do not have all the details. Second, it conducts readers through the details of the story by the end.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (April 2025)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p><p>This system also means that information less vital to the reader's understanding comes later in the story, where it is easier to edit out for space or other reasons. This is called "cutting from the bottom."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Rather than petering out, a story may end with a "<a href="/wiki/News_style#Kicker" title="News style">kicker</a>"—a conclusion, perhaps call to action—which comes <i>after</i> the pyramid. This is particularly common in <a href="/wiki/Feature_style" class="mw-redirect" title="Feature style">feature style</a> articles.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="History">History</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Inverted_pyramid_(journalism)&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: History"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Historians disagree about when the form was created. Many say the <a href="/wiki/Electrical_telegraph" title="Electrical telegraph">invention of the telegraph</a> sparked its development by encouraging reporters to condense material, to reduce costs,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> or to hedge against the unreliability of the telegraph network.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Studies of 19th-century news stories in American newspapers, however, suggest that the form spread several decades later than the telegraph, possibly because the reform era's social and educational forces encouraged factual reporting rather than more interpretive narrative styles.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-errico&#95;2-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-errico-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Chip Scanlan's essay on the form<sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> includes this frequently cited example of telegraphic reporting:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1244412712">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 32px}.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;margin-top:0}@media(min-width:500px){.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{padding-left:1.6em}}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>This evening at about 9:30 p.m. at <a href="/wiki/Ford%27s_Theatre" title="Ford&#39;s Theatre">Ford's Theatre</a>, the <a href="/wiki/Abraham_Lincoln" title="Abraham Lincoln">President</a>, while sitting in his private box with <a href="/wiki/Mary_Todd_Lincoln" title="Mary Todd Lincoln">Mrs. Lincoln</a>, <a href="/wiki/Clara_Harris" title="Clara Harris">Mrs. Harris</a> and <a href="/wiki/Henry_Rathbone" title="Henry Rathbone">Major Rathburn</a>, was shot by an assassin, who suddenly entered the box and approached behind the President.
</p><p>The assassin then leaped upon the stage, brandishing a large dagger or knife, and made his escape in the rear of the theatre.
</p><p>The pistol ball entered the back of the President's head and penetrated nearly through the head. The wound is mortal.
</p><p>The President has been insensible ever since it was inflicted, and is now dying.
</p><p>About the same hour an assassin, whether the same or not, entered <a href="/wiki/William_H._Seward" title="William H. Seward">Mr. Seward</a>'s apartment and under pretense of having a prescription was shown to the Secretary's sick chamber. The assassin immediately rushed to the bed and inflicted two or three stabs on the chest and two on the face.
It is hoped the wounds may not be mortal. My apprehension is that they will prove fatal.
</p><p>The nurse alarmed Mr. <a href="/wiki/Frederick_W._Seward" title="Frederick W. Seward">Frederick Seward</a>, who was in an adjoining rented room, and he hastened to the door of his father's room, when he met the assassin, who inflicted upon him one or more dangerous wounds. The recovery of Frederick Seward is doubtful.
</p><p>It is not probable that the President will live through the night.
</p>
<p><a href="/wiki/Ulysses_S._Grant" title="Ulysses S. Grant">General Grant</a> and his wife were advertised to be at the theatre...</p></blockquote><div class="templatequotecite"><p style="display: inline; padding-left: 2.3em;">—&#8202;<i><a href="/wiki/New_York_Herald" class="mw-redirect" title="New York Herald">New York Herald</a></i>, April 15, 1865<span style="display:none" data-plural="1"></span></p></div>
<p><a href="/wiki/Five_Ws" title="Five Ws">Who, when, where, why, what, and how</a> are addressed in the first paragraph. As the article continues, the less important details are presented. An even more pyramid-conscious reporter or editor would move two additional details to the first two sentences: That the shot was to the head, and that it was expected to prove fatal. The transitional sentence about the Grants suggests that less-important facts are being added to the rest of the story.
</p><p>Other news outlets such as the <a href="/wiki/Associated_Press" title="Associated Press">Associated Press</a> did not use this format when covering the assassination, instead adopting a chronological organization.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
