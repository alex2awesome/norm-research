<h2>P.1 Purpose</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">a. This NASA Procedural Requirements (NPR) prescribes 
    specific procedures for managing and controlling communications to and from 
    the Principals of the Office of the Administrator and guidance and standards 
    for preparing internal and external correspondence and communications in both 
    paper and electronic formats. </p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. This NPR describes various types of correspondence 
    and communications designed to improve the management and flow of communications 
    for cost-effectiveness, availability, and dissemination of NASA information. 
    The NPR provides guidance for preparing, documenting, and disseminating effective 
    communications to the widest possible audience as prescribed by NASA Policy 
    Directive (NPD) 1090.xx, NASA Communicate Knowledge Process Policy for Programs 
    and Projects, NPD 7120.4A, Program/Project Management, and NPR 7120.5A, NASA 
    Program and Project Management Processes and Requirements.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. This NPR prescribes "plain language" format 
    and style for preparing correspondence and communications as promulgated by 
    the President's National Partnership for Reinventing Government.</p>

<h2>P.2 Applicability</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">a. This NPR addresses drafting, preparing, and reviewing 
    internal and external correspondence and communications. </p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. This NPR applies to NASA Headquarters and NASA Centers, 
    including Component Facilities.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. This NPR provides guidance for scientists, technologists, 
    engineers, program and project directors and managers, and those functions 
    involving or supporting public and legislative affairs, education, history, 
    scientific and technical information, and technology transfer offices, and 
    records management for preparing correspondence and communications in communicating 
    the aspects of the knowledge gained from NASA-funded research, development, 
    or operation to various audiences.</p>

<h2>1. Communications Addressed to the Principals of the Office of the
Administrator</h2>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.1 The OCIC, Headquarters Correspondence 
Management Office manages and controls incoming and outgoing, internal and external correspondence,
communications, and mail addressed to or signed by the Principals of the
Office of the Administrator, e.g., Administrator, Deputy Administrator,
Associate Deputy Administrator, Chief Engineer, Chief Information Officer,
Chief Scientist, and Chief Technologist.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.2 The OCIC manages, controls, and tracks incoming and outgoing,
internal and external communications requiring responses, as well as those
actions initiated by the Administrator, Deputy Administrator, or Associate
Deputy Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.3 The OCIC prescribes specific guidance, 
procedures, and standards for preparing internal and external 
correspondence and communications in both paper and electronic formats.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.4 The OCIC serves as the Agency focal point for communications addressed
to or signed by the Principals of the Office of the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.5 The OCIC serves as the Agency authority and establishes Agencywide
guidelines to ensure that NASA disseminates optimal communications to the
widest possible audience.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.6 The OCIC establishes the style and format for preparing various
types of correspondence and communications designed to improve the management
and flow of communications.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.7 The OCIC ensures quality editing and proofreading that reflect
"plain language" as promulgated by the President's National Partnership
for Reinventing Government.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.8 The OCIC coordinates senior-level communications for the Executive
Office of the President, Congress, and domestic and international 
dignitaries.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.9 The OCIC provides editorial guidance to scientists, technologists,
engineers, program and project directors and managers, and to those whose
functions involve or support the public and legislative affairs, education,
history, scientific and technical information, and technology transfer
offices.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.10 The OCIC manages a tracking system of accountability for incoming
and outgoing, internal and external correspondence, communications, and
mail for the Office of the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.11 The OCIC manages a recordkeeping system of accountability for
the incoming and outgoing, internal and external correspondence, communications,
and mail for the Office of the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.1.12 The OCIC ensures that the Office of the Administrator's records
are maintained in accordance with the prescribed Federal guidelines and
applicable Agency Retention Schedules.</p>

<h2>1.2 Quality Control Liaison (QCL) and Correspondence Control
Liaison (CCL) Designations for NASA Headquarters</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.2.1. The Associate Administrator for each Headquarters organization
(hereafter also referred to as the Action Office) must designate a member
from its organization to serve as a QCL and CCL. These designations are
required to facilitate the smooth and efficient flow of communications
between the Action Office and the OCIC.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.2.2. The QCL supports the following correspondence management activities:</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. The QCL reviews and edits Action Office responses for actions
assigned by the OCIC.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. The QCL ensures that responses for signature by the Principals of
the Office of the Administrator reflect Agency standards, format, style,
and contain all appropriate information.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. The QCL ensures that signature packages are properly assembled.
<p class="h05Indent">d. The QCL initials the Action Document Summary (ADS), NHQ Form 117,
to signify that packages prepared for signature by the Principals of the
Office of the Administrator have been reviewed and are correct. The OCIC 
will return all packages the QCL fails to initial. (See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/appendixa/image4.gif" WIDTH="612" HEIGHT="792" alt="Figure A-4">Appendix
A, Figure A-4, Block 3</a>.)</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.2.3. The CCL supports the following correspondence management activities:</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. The CCL serves as the focal point for correspondence and
actions that are assigned to the Action Office by the OCIC.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. The CCL manages a tracking system of accountability for actions that
are assigned to the Action Office by the OCIC.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">1.2.4. Each Associate Administrator must provide the OCIC
 with the name(s) of the individual(s) designated to serve as the QCL or CCL.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.2.5. Each Associate Administrator must timely apprise the OCIC of
changes to QCL or CCL designation(s).</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.2.6. Each Associate Administrator must provide the OCIC
 with the name of an individual to serve as a Point of Contact (POC) when either the designated
QCL or CCL is absent or unavailable.</p>

<h2>1.3 Action Office Mailboxes</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.3.1 The OCIC establishes mailboxes for each Action 
Office.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.3.2 The OCIC uses these mailboxes to dispatch and 
transmit correspondence management matters to the Action Office.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.3.3 Each Action Office must check its mailbox at 
least once per business day.</p>

<h2>1.4 High-Profile Responses (Administration Offices)</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.4.1 Action Offices must meet due dates for high-profile (e.g., Secretary
of Transportation, Director General, Russian Space Agency) responses.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.4.2 To help identify correspondence as high profile and to call attention
to special handling requirements, pertinent information, a short due date,
or the number of staff hours expended to prepare the response, the OCIC 
notes any special information in the "Abstract" section of the Action Cover
Mask. (The OCIC summarizes pertinent information regarding the correspondence
and produces an Action Cover Mask that is sent with the correspondence to 
the Action Office.)</p>

<h2>1.5 Due Dates</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.5.1. The OCIC establishes a due date, using a 10-business-day timeframe
when no due date is stated in the incoming correspondence.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.5.2. When the incoming correspondence specifically states a due date,
the OCIC uses that date.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.5.3. The White House Correspondence Office establishes due dates for
its referrals. The due dates vary between 15 and 25 business days, depending
on the subject. The OCIC must use the White House Correspondence Office
due dates in establishing an Action Office due date.</p>

<h2>1.6 Extensions of Due Dates</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.6.1. All due dates must be met. Requests for extensions should be
rare, and the Action Office must specifically justify the reason for its
request.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.6.2. The Action Office must immediately notify its assigned OCIC
 Correspondence Analyst regarding any possible delinquency.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.6.3. The OCIC will provide guidance to the Action 
Office and, as appropriate, will coordinate the request for an extension on 
behalf of the Agency with the external organization and advise the Action 
Office accordingly.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.6.4. The OCIC will provide guidance to the Action 
Office and will coordinate the request for an extension on behalf of the 
Action Office for Office of the Administrator-initiated actions with the 
Office of the Administrator and advise the Action Office accordingly. For 
example, Capital Investment Council, Program Management Council, Senior 
Management Council, Senior Staff Meeting Minutes.</p>

<h2>1.7 Office of the Administrator Signature Packages</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.7.1. All packages for signature by the Principals of the Office of
the Administrator must first go through the OCIC. This includes NASA 
Directives.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.7.2. The OCIC reviews and processes the package for signature by the
Office of the Administrator. Incomplete packages or packages requiring
corrections are returned.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.7.3. Do not carry packages directly to the Office of the Administrator.
The Office of the Administrator will return them to the OCIC.</p>

<h2>1.8 Concurrences for Office of the Administrator Signature Packages</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.8.1 The Action Office must obtain all required concurrences on the
ADS before submitting signature packages to the OCIC. The OCIC recommends
that the Action Office simultaneously hand-carry or fax the package.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.8.2 The Action Office reviews the "Info Offices" 
listed by the OCIC on the Action Cover Mask to determine the appropriate 
concurrences.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.8.3 The Action Office must obtain concurrences on the ADS from the
cognizant Headquarters organizations when responses include data from other
Headquarters offices or Centers.</p>

<h2>1.9 Correspondence to Officials-in-Charge of Headquarters Offices
and/or Center Directors</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">1.9.1. The Action Office must include the most current distribution
list in the signature package.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.9.2. The OCIC will return the signature package to the originating
office if the distribution list is absent or incorrect.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">1.9.3. Visit the Correspondence Management Web site on the <b>HQ Home
Page</b> at <a href="http://www.hq.nasa.gov/office/codec/codeci/mailcor/cormgt.html">http://www.hq.nasa.gov/office/codec/codeci/mailcor/cormgt.html</a>
to see the most current listings.</p><br>
		<h1>CHAPTER 2: NASA Writing Standards</h1>

<hr>

<h2>2.1 Organized Writing</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Though format is important, clarity is even more
important. The following techniques and guidelines can help make your writing more organized, natural, and concise.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.1.1. <b>Follow the newspaper format</b>. Open
with the most important information and decrease to the least important.
Avoid mere chronology.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.1.2. <b>Start fast, explain as necessary, then
stop</b>. When writing a letter, think about the one key sentence that
expresses the main idea. Do not waste the opening--the strongest place
in a letter. Begin with the key sentence, if appropriate; if not, be sure
it appears by the end of the first paragraph. Put requests before justifications, answers before explanations, conclusions before discussions, and summaries before details.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.1.3. <b>Key points</b>. In a complex proposal
or a reply to various questions, there may be many key points. In these
cases, begin with a general statement of purpose, such as the policy directives.  The following examples demonstrate this technique:</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. We inspected the Engineering Department on January 24, 1997, and found its overall performance satisfactory.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. We request authorization to hire a full-time clerk typist or to reassign someone from the word processing center.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">c. This memorandum summarizes initial plans for reorganizing the Personnel Department.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.1.4. <b>Be direct</b>. Occasionally, you may delay
a main point to soften bad news or to introduce a controversial proposal.
However, do not delay routinely. Readers, like listeners, are deterred
by those who take too long to get to the point. In most cases, it is best
to be direct.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.1.5. <b>Persuade vs. Inform.</b> When writing
to persuade rather than to inform, end strongly--with a forecast, appeal,
or implication. When feelings are involved, exit gracefully--with an expression of good will. When in doubt, offer assistance and the name and telephone number of a contact.</p>

<h2>2.2 Spoken Style</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.2.1. <b>Speak and communicate on paper</b>.
Because readers hear writing, the most readable writing sounds like people
talking to people. To achieve a spoken style, imagine your reader is sitting across the desk from you. If you are writing to many readers and none in particular, talk to one typical reader. Then write with personal pronouns, everyday words, and the other techniques provided in this NPR. Once you have a draft, read it aloud. It should sound like something you might say in person. Whether writing formally or informally, use language you would use in speaking.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.2.2. Do not use contractions in formal writing.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.2.3. <b>Speak directly to your readers</b>.  Use the imperative mood when preparing administrative documents, especially
procedures, "how-to" instructions, and lists of duties. Directness also
avoids the passive voice. This style results in guidance that is shorter,
crisper, and easier to understand.</p>

<p class="h05Indent"><u>SAY</u>: Sign all copies. Provide the draft by Monday.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.2.4. <b>Focus on your reader's needs</b>. Writing
helps you find out what you think; as you compose, you discover ideas.  To keep from wandering, analyze your audience in light of your purpose.  You should be able to answer these next questions before you begin to write.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">What is my purpose?</p>

<p class="h05Indent">Who are my readers?</p>

<p class="h05Indent">What are their interests?</p>

<p class="h05Indent">How much do they know already?</p>

<p class="h05Indent">What will make it easy for them to understand or
act?</p>

<h2>2.3 Compact Writing</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.3.1. Give your ideas no more words than they
deserve. Shorten paragraphs to sentences, sentences to clauses, clauses
to phrases, phrases to words, words to pictures, or strike the idea entirely.  Keep only what contributes to meaning.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.3.2. Avoid "it is" and "there is." Unless "it"
refers to something mentioned earlier, write around "it is." "It is" constructions tangle sentences, delay meaning, encourage passive verbs, and hide responsibility.  Spare only natural expressions such as "it is time to ." and "it is your job to ."</p>

<p class="h025Indent"><b><u>It is</u></b> my understanding that your program covers medical expenses from such injuries.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">I understand that your program covers medical
expenses from such injuries.</p>

<p class="h025Indent"><u>It is</u></b> recognized that as the project
evolves, there likely will be changes in task definitions and priorities.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">We recognize that as the project evolves, task
definitions and priorities are likely tochange.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.3.3. Beware of "there is" and "there are." Most
of these delayers can be avoided by simple subtraction or slight manipulation.  This technique is illustrated in the following examples:</p>

<p class="h05Indent"><u>There are</u> some deadlines that cannot be changed.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">Some deadlines cannot be changed.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.3.4. <b>Shorten wordy expressions</b>. Instead
of adding impressive bulk to writing, wordy expressions clutter it by getting in the way of the words that do the important work. Phrases like<b> in order to (to)</b> and <b>a number of (some)</b> are minor ideas that deserve no more than a word apiece. Tap them and move on.</p>

<h2>2.4 Tone</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.4.1. A writer's attitude toward the subject or readers causes relatively few problems in routine letters. The rules are straightforward.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.4.2. Subordinates may suggest, request, or recommend, but only superiors may direct. Though pronouns are acceptable, do not get personal. Courtesy is required; warmth is not. A neutral tone is preferred.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.4.3. Because much writing is routine, tone sometimes causes problems when the matter is delicate. The more sensitive the reader or issue, the more careful we must be to promote good will. Tactlessness in writing suggests clumsiness in general. When feelings are involved, one misused word can offend the reader.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.4.4. Be mindful of rubberstamp endings. They do not improve good letters or save bad ones. To the reader whose request has been denied, an offer of further assistance promises further disappointment.  
As an example, the following closing sentence should be dropped entirely
or incorporated with the rest of the letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">This setback aside, we hope that you will take
advantage of other courses available to you.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.5.2. The positive statement is usually clearer and briefer. But a negative statement can also be clear. Use it if you are cautioning the reader.</p>

<h2>2.6 Write Short, Disciplined Sentences</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.1. Readable sentences are simple, active,
affirmative, and declarative. The more a sentence deviates from this structure, the harder the sentence is to understand. Though short sentences will not guarantee clarity, they are usually less confusing than long ones.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.2. State one thing only in each sentence.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.3. Divide long sentences into two or three short sentences. Average 20 words or less when mixing long and short sentences.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.4. <b>Use parallel structure</b>. In parallel
structures, sentences and phrases use the same parts of speech to express
different ideas. Look for opportunities to arrange two or more equally
important ideas so that they look equal. Any sentence over 30 words should
be perfectly parallel. Parallel structure is especially important when you use a list. Parallelism saves words, clarifies ideas, and provides balance. This technique is demonstrated in the following examples:</p>

<p class="h05Indent">In sentences--</p>

<p class="h05Indent">(not parallel)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">Their position is that the symposium is a forum for
the dissemination of information and is not intended to establish standards.</p>

<p class="h075Indent">(parallel, concise, ideas balanced)</p>


<p class="h075Indent">Their position is that the symposium is a forum for
sharing information and not for setting standards.</p>

<p class="h075Indent">(not parallel)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">Effective October 1, 1997, addressees will be required to utilize the cost accounts contained in Enclosure 1. Addressees will cease reporting against cost accounts A12, B34, and C45.</p>

<p class="h075Indent">(parallel, concise, ideas balanced)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">On October 1, 1977, begin using the cost accounts in Enclosure 1, and cease using cost accounts A12, B34, and C45.</p>

<p class="h075Indent">(not parallel)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">By purchasing this equipment, we would cut down on
errors, and expenses in the long run would be reduced.</p>

<p class="h075Indent">(parallel, concise, ideas balanced)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">By purchasing this equipment, we would reduce errors
and expenses<b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">In a list--</p>

<p class="h05Indent">(not parallel)</p>

<p class="h075Indent">o  The duties of the Executive Secretary of the Administrative Committee are:</p>

<p class="h075Indent">

o  To take minutes of all the meetings; <u>(phrase)</u><br>


o  The Executive Secretary answers all the correspondence;

and (<u>clause</u>)<br>


o  Writing of monthly reports. <u>(topic)</u></p>

<p class="h05Indent">(parallel, concise, ideas balanced)</p>


<p class="h075Indent">

o  The duties of the Executive Secretary of the Administrative

Committee are--</p>

<p class="h075Indent">

o  To take minutes of all the meetings;<br>

o  To answer all the correspondence; and<br>


o  To write the monthly reports.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.5. <b>Remove all unnecessary words</b>. Strive

for a simple sentence with a subject and verb. Eliminate unnecessary modifiers.

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.6. <b>Place key ideas deliberately</b>. Begin

and end a sentence with the most important point because ideas gain emphasis when they appear at either end. To mute an idea, place it in the middle.  To improve sentences that mumble, place ideas deliberately, place less emphasis on minor ideas, use more parallelism, and use concise sentences.  These techniques are demonstrated in the following examples:</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. It has been determined that moving the computer, as shown in Enclosure 1, would allow room for another cabinet to be installed.
("moving the computer" muted)</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. Moving the computer, as shown in Enclosure 1, would allow room for another cabinet.  ("moving the computer" stressed)</p>

<p class="h05Indent">c. I would like to congratulate you on your selection as our Employee of the Month for December.  ("congratulations" muted)</p>

<p class="h05Indent">d. Congratulations on your selection as our December Employee of the Month.  ("congratulations" stressed)</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.7. Place minor ideas in secondary clauses; do
not make them the main subject of a sentence.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. The revised housing allowance tables, which have been mailed to all pay offices, are effective October 1, 1997. ("date" stressed)</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. The revised housing allowance tables, which are effective October 1, 1997, have been mailed to all pay offices. ("mailing" stressed)</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.8. <b>Be concise</b>. An occasional sentence
of six words or fewer grabs the reader's attention. This is an excellent
technique to make a key point. This technique is illustrated in the following example:</p>

<p class="h025Indent">I can get more information if each of you gives me less. Here is why. In a week, around 50 staff actions appear in my In box. I could handle that if all I did was work the In box. Yet 70 percent of my time is dedicated to attending briefings. I could handle that dilemma, too - listening to briefings and thinking about staff papers at the same time. However, I do not.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.6.9. <b>Use questions in your writing</b>. A request gains emphasis when it ends with a question mark. Look for opportunities to reach out to your reader. This technique is demonstrated in the following example:</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Request this office be notified as to whether the conference has been rescheduled.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Has the conference been rescheduled?  (preferred)</p>

<h2>2.7 Use Short Paragraphs</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.7.1. Long paragraphs overwhelm ideas and impede
the reader's progress. You can improve clarity by using short, compact
paragraphs. Each paragraph should deal with a single, unified topic. Cover
one topic completely before beginning another. Present lengthy, complex,
or technical discussions in a series of related paragraphs or as an 
Appendix.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.7.2. Use short paragraphs, especially at the
beginning of letters. Long first paragraphs discourage reading.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.7.3. Call attention to lists of items or instructions by displaying them in subparagraphs or separating by bullets. However, do not use so many levels of subparagraphs that the writing becomes difficult to follow. </p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.7.4. Occasionally, use a one-sentence paragraph to highlight an important idea.</p> 


<h2>2.8 Use PersonalL Pronouns </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.8.1. Personal pronouns are immediate and easy to understand. They help clarify the "who." </p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.8.2. Use the following techniques to help retain the reader's interest:</p> 

<p class="h05Indent">a. When referring to the Agency, office, or group, 
use "we," "us," "our." Do not refer to the Agency as "it."</p> 

<p class="h05Indent">b. When speaking for yourself, use "I," "me," "my."
</p> 

<p class="h05Indent">c. Use "I," "me," and "my" less often, and then 
only to indicate special concern or warmth. </p>

<p class="h05Indent">d. When referring to the reader, stated or implied,
 use "you." </p>


<h2>2.9 Use Active Voice</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.9.1. The active voice eliminates confusion by forcing you to name the actor in a sentence. This construction makes it clear to the reader who is to perform the duty. Use a who-does-what order. This technique is demonstrated in the following example:</p>  

<p class="h05Indent">Active: The worker inspected the orbiter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.9.2 The passive voice makes sentences longer and roundabout. Who is responsible is much less obvious. Passive verbs have a form of the verb "to be" plus the past participle of a main verb:</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Passive: The orbiter was inspected by the worker.</p>
        
<p class="h025Indent">Examples of these verb forms include the following words: am, is, are, was, were, be, and been, plus, a main verb usually ending in "en" or "ed."  These include "was received," "is being considered," and "has been selected." </p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.9.3. The passive voice reverses the natural, active order of English sentences. In the following passive example, the receiver of the action comes before the actor:  Passive: The regulation [receiver] was written [verb] by the drafter [actor]. Active: The drafter [actor] wrote [verb] the regulation [receiver].</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.9.4. Passive constructions are confusing. Active sentences must have actors, but passive ones are complete without them. See the following ambiguous passive sentences.</p>
<table border=1 cellpadding=5>
<tr>
<td>The material will be delivered. </td>

<td>By whom?  </td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>The start date is to be decided.  </td>
<td>By whom?  </td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>The figures must be approved.  </td>
<td>By whom? </td>
</tr>
</table>
<p class="h025Indent">Putting the actor before the verb forces you to be clear about responsibility.</p> 
<p class="h05Indent">a. The messenger will deliver the material.  </p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. The contractor will decide the start date. </p>

<p class="h05Indent">c. The administrator must approve the figures.</p>

<p class="h025Indent"> 2.9.5. Use the passive voice only when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or obvious. This does not usually apply in administrative writing.</p> 
<p class="h05Indent">a. Small items are often stolen. </p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. The applications have been mailed.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">If only some of the rocket engines ignited, say: "The engines that ignited." </p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.12.1. Do not use acronyms more than necessary. Spell out an acronym the first time it appears, followed by the acronym in parentheses. Capitalize the first letter of each word that constitutes the acronym, as indicated below and as cited in Appendix H. Acronyms.</p> 

<p class="h025Indent">Flight Readiness Review (FRR)</p>
 <p class="h025Indent">2.12.2. If an acronym appears only twice or infrequently, spell out the term every time or avoid the acronym entirely. </p>

<h2>2.13 Computer/Internet Words: Usage and Style</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">2.13.1. Study The Gregg Reference Manual, Eight Edition, 1998, Appendix B. Glossary of Computer Terms, before preparing paper or electronic communications containing computer or Internet words.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.13.2. Use the following styles for expressing Internet words.</p>
 
<p class="h05Indent">a. Internet and Net: Initial cap the "I" and "N," even within a sentence.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. World Wide Web or WWW or Web or the Web: Initial cap the "W," even within a sentence.</p> 
<p class="h05Indent">c. Web site and Web page (two words): Initial cap the "W," even within a sentence.</p> 
<p class="h05Indent">d. Home Page (two words): Initial cap when referring to a specific home page or home page title; e.g., NASA Headquarters Home Page; you may want to develop a home page for your audience.</p> 
<p class="h05Indent">e. E-mail (electronic mail): Always hyphenated; initial cap the "e" only when used as a heading or at the beginning of a sentence.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">2.13.3. Do not use all-capital letters in composing an e-mail message.  Generally, it implies that the writer is shouting at the reader. </p>

<h2>3.1 Standard Business Letter</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Before preparing letters, study the figures in this chapter. These guidelines are standard features for correct letter 
formatting for NASA.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.1. <b>Administrator's Correspondence</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">For specific instructions on preparing correspondence
for the Office of the Administrator, see <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixA">
Appendix A</a>: Administrator's Correspondence Preparation and Control.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.2. <b>Stationery</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">Prepare all correspondence on official NASA insignia
"meatball" letterhead. Use plain bond paper for succeeding pages. See <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixA">
Appendix A</a> for guidelines on stationery to be used for officials in the Office of the Administrator.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.3. <b>Type</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">Larger, pica font is preferred over smaller, elite
font. Use script or italic only for occasional emphasis. Do not type entire
letters in script or italic.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.4. <b>Ink</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">Use black or blue-black ink to type, date, and sign correspondence.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.5. <b>Margins</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">Set the left margin two spaces to the right of the printed caption "Reply to Attn of:" (1.2 or 1 1/8 inches). Set the right and bottom margins at approximately 1 inch. Set the top margin at 1 inch on the second and succeeding pages. Do not justify the right margin.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.6. <b>File Copies</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">Always prepare an official record copy.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Official Yellow File Copy.</b> An official
"yellow" record copy is preferred. If a yellow copy is not possible, prepare a white copy visibly marked in the upper right corner with "Official File" or "Official Record Copy." If concurrences are required, use NASA Form 1267 (or 1267A for laser printers) for the first page of the official file copy of the letter.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>Other File Copies.</b> Individual office
requirements vary. Prepare only the number of copies required for distribution based on the correspondence practices of your office.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.7. <b>Reply to Attn of: (Writer's Code).</b></p>

<p class="h025Indent">The<b> </b>writer's organizational and/or mail code or mail stop serves as a basic identification symbol and must appear on all outgoing correspondence. Type only the writer's code in letters or numerals on the "Reply to Attn of:" line.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.8. <b>Dating Correspondence</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Date the letter after it is signed. Stamping the
date is preferred; however, if typed, ensure that the type style is the
same as the letter. Stamp or type the date at the right margin directly
across from the "Reply to Attn of:" line (e.g., August 20, 1998). Do not
use military style (e.g., 20 Aug 1998) or endings such as "st" or "th"
after the day (e.g., Aug 20th).</p>

full name and title. Do not abbreviate names or use nicknames. If the gender is unknown, address the individual by first and last name in the salutation line; e.g., "Dear Kris Szaniawski:" Single-space the address and arrange it in block style.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.12. <b>Salutation</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">A personalized business letter requires a salutation.

Appendix E</a> Forms of Addresses, for examples of proper salutations.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.13. <b>Body of Letter</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Use single-spaced text with double-spacing between
paragraphs. Type text paragraphs in block form. When a paragraph is separated into subparagraphs, number and letter the subparagraphs as shown in <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter3/Image2.gif" alt="Chapter 3 image 2">Figure 3-2</a> of this chapter. Hyphenate sparingly to ensure a uniform right margin. Avoid hyphenating more than two consecutive lines. Also, avoid hyphenating at the end of the first line or at the end of the last full line of a paragraph.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.14. <b>Widows and Orphans</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Do not begin a paragraph near the end of a page
unless there is room for at least two lines on that page. Do not continue
a paragraph on the following page unless two lines of that paragraph can
be carried over to that page. See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter3/Image3.gif" alt="Chapter 3 image 3">Figure 3-3</a> of this chapter for instructions on numbering pages.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.15. <b>Succeeding Pages</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">Type succeeding page(s) of a letter on plain bond
paper. Beginning with the second page, type the page number five lines from the top of the page, approximately 1 inch to the left of the right edge of the page. Continue the body of the letter two lines below the page number. Allow the same margins as for the first page. See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter3/Image3.gif" alt="Chapter 3 image 3"> Figure 3-3</a> of this chapter for instructions on numbering pages.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.16. <b>Complimentary Close</b>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">A personalized business letter requires a complimentary close. Use "Sincerely," for all NASA correspondence. Use "Respectfully," when the letter is addressed to the President of the United States.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.1.20. <b>Postscripts</b>. Although writing a

followed by the postscript. The author then signs or initials the postscript.  When the signer handwrites a postscript, type it on all copies or photocopy the original for the file.</p>

<h2>3.5 Nonpersonalized Business Letter</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.1<b>. General</b>. Study the figures in this
section before preparing nonpersonalized business letters. The nonpersonalized business letter is a format for specific types of communications with organizations outside NASA. Use this format when the addressee's name is unknown or when a letter is addressed to an organization with attention to a specific individual for special handling. This section describes the format to use when preparing a nonpersonalized business letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.2. <b>Attention: Line</b>. In the nonpersonalized
business letter, an attention line generally follows the first line of the address at the left margin.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.3.<b> Salutation</b>. There is no salutation in a nonpersonalized business letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.4. <b>Subject</b>. Type the word "Subject:" at the left margin, three typing returns below the last line of the address.
One-line subjects are preferred. If more than one line is needed for the
subject, begin succeeding lines flush with the first character of the subject. Keep the subject short; state concisely the main topic of the letter. Lengthy subjects are discouraged. If responding to a letter with a "Subject:" line, use the same subject. If acronyms or abbreviations are used in the subject, spell them out, followed by the acronym in parentheses. If an abbreviation or acronym is frequently used in a letter, establish it in the subject line so that only the shortened form is necessary in the body of the correspondence.  Capitalize the first letter of key words within the subject line.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.5. <b>Reference</b>. Avoid a separate reference
line in a business letter, except when it contributes to the understanding
of the correspondence and when the number of references is too great to
be reasonably incorporated in the first paragraph of the letter. Type "Ref:" at the left margin, blocked, two typing returns below the last line of the subject, followed by an itemized listing of the references. Abbreviate items as much as possible; e.g., type dates in numerical figures (4/15/97).  List references as (a), (b), (c), single-spaced, in chronological order with the most recent date listed first. See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter3/Image4.gif" alt="Chapter 3 image 4">Figure 3-3</a> of this chapter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.6. <b>Text.</b> Prepare the text portion in
block format, the same as the standard business letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.5.7. <b>Complimentary Close. </b>There is no
complimentary close in a nonpersonalized business letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.6.1. <b>General</b>. The Office of the General

solely for legal correspondence relating to a specific case, protest, patent, or license.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.6.2. <b>Format. </b>The legal letter format is based on the standard business letter with the exception of a caption as explained in paragraph 3.6.3 below. All other elements are the same as the standard business letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.6.3. <b>Caption. </b>Begin the caption by typing

<h2>3.7 Multiple-Addressee Letters</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">3.7.1<b>. General</b>. Study the figures in this
chapter before preparing a multiple-addressee letter. Use this format for
addressees outside NASA or when a more personalized format is needed for
addressees within NASA; e.g., award, thank you, and congratulatory letters,
or personal invitations.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.7.2. <b>Format. </b>Prepare identical letters to more than one individual in the same format as the standard business
letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.7.3. <b>Original and Copies.</b> Prepare an original letter for each recipient and only one official file copy. Document the list of addressees as follows:</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Original</b>. When the recipient of a letter
needs to know who received identical letters, type "Identical letter to:"
two typing returns below the signature block or enclosure line. On the
next line, begin the list of recipients of the letter. If there is not
enough space at the bottom of the page, type the list on a separate sheet
and attach it behind the letter.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>File Copies</b>. Document the recipients of the letter on the official file copy when the addressees do not need to know who received copies. On the file copy, type "Identical letter to:" two typing returns below the signature block, enclosure line, or the "cc:" line. On the next line, begin the list of the recipients of the letter.  If there is not enough space at the bottom of the page, type the list on a separate page and place it behind the file copy.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.7.4. <b>Exception. </b>When the list of addressees is mostly within NASA, but includes a few individuals from outside NASA, use the multiple-addressee memo as shown in this chapter.  An example would be when non-NASA individuals participate in NASA committees or advisory groups.</p>

<h2>3.8 Assembly for Signature for Multiple-Addressee Letters</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">3.8.1. <b>General</b>. Assemble a letter in the
proper order so the signer can quickly identify and review all pertinent
documents.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.8.2. <b>Instruction</b>s. Arrange the letter
and accompanying documents as described in Appendix A when the letter is
ready for final review and signature.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.8.3. <b>Administrator's Packages</b>. See <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixA">
Appendix A</a> for instructions for assembly of Administrator's correspondence packages.  Complex packages, at any signature level, may be assembled this way for easier understanding.</p>

<h2>3.9 Condolence Letters</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.1. A condolence letter will be prepared for the Administrator's signature upon notification of the death of an employee and, as appropriate, upon the death of a former employee or immediate family member of a current or former employee.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.2. The Office of the Administrator requests
that the letter be completed and mailed within 3 business days after receipt of notification of the death.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.3. Immediately advise the OCIC upon notification
of the death of an employee and, as appropriate, upon notification of the
death of a former employee or immediate family member of a current or former employee.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.4. The office to which the employee or former
employee was assigned will prepare the letter to the next of kin for the
Administrator's signature. The condolence letter should detail the employee's recent career highlights and any other pertinent information in the letter.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.5. The office to which the employee or former
employee was assigned will prepare a letter to the employee for the Administrator's signature upon the death of an immediate family member.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.6. The OCIC will initiate the letter in cases in which the applicable office is ambiguous and may request supporting 
documentation from a cognizant office.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">3.9.7. A condolence letter, signed by the Administrator, does not preclude office members and colleagues from sending other condolence communications.</p><br>
																	
<h1>CHAPTER 4: Memorandums (Memos) (Formats)</h1>
<hr>
<h2>4.1 Standard Memo</h2>
<p class="h025Indent">NASA policy is to use memos for internal use only.
Letters are used for external and formal use, e.g., congratulations, retirement,
condolence. Before preparing memos, study the figures in this chapter.
These guidelines are standard features for correct memo formatting for
correspondence within and between all NASA Centers, including Headquarters.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.1 <b>Administrator's Correspondence.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">See <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixA">Appendix
A</a>: Administrator's Correspondence Preparation and Control for specific
instructions on preparing memos for the Office of the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.2<b> Stationery.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Prepare all correspondence on official NASA letterhead.
Use plain bond paper for succeeding pages. See <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixA">Appendix
A</a> for stationery to be used for officials in the Office of the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.3<b> Type.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Larger type is preferred over smaller type. Use
script or italic only for occasional emphasis. Do not type entire memos
in script or italic. However, to prevent a memo from continuing onto a
second page, the font size of the type may be reduced.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.4. <b>Ink.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Use black or blue-black ink to type, stamp, and
sign correspondence.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.5. <b>Margins.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Set the left margin two spaces to the right of
the printed caption "Reply to Attn of:" (1.2 or 1 1/8 inches). Set the
right and bottom margins at approximately 1 inch. Set the top margin at
1 inch on the second and succeeding pages. Do not justify the right margin.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.6. <b>File Copies.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Always prepare an official record.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Official Yellow File Copies. </b>An official
"yellow" record copy is preferred. However, if a yellow copy is not possible,
prepare a white copy visibly marked in the upper right corner "Official
File" or "Official Record Copy." If concurrences are required, use NASA
Form 1267 (or 1267A for laser printers) for the first page of the official
file copy of the memo.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>Other File Copies.</b> Individual office
requirements vary. Prepare only the number of copies required for distribution,
based on the correspondence practices of the preparing office.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.7<b> Reply to Attn of: (Writer's Code).</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">The writer's organizational and/or mail code or
mail stop serves as a basic identification symbol and must appear on all
outgoing correspondence. Type only the writer's code in letters or numerals
on the "Reply to Attn of:" line.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.8. <b>Dating Correspondence.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Date the memo after it is signed. Stamping the
date is preferred; however, if typed, ensure that the type style is the
same as the memo. Stamp or type the date at the right margin directly across
from the "Reply to Attn of:" line, e.g., August 20, 1994. Do not use military
style (e.g., 20 Aug 1994) or endings such as "st" or "th" after the day
(e.g., Aug 20th).</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.9. <b>Address.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Always type the "TO:," "THRU:," and "FROM:," in
all caps.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>TO:</b> Type "TO:" at the left margin, three
typing returns below the "Reply to Attn of:" line. Seven spaces after "TO:,"
type the mail code and title of addressee. The use of titles is preferred;
however, if an individual does not use a title, it is acceptable to have
a memo from a title to a name or from a name to a name (use full name,
e.g., B/John A. Smith).</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>THRU:</b> Use the "THRU:" line when one
or more addressees are needed to endorse (i.e., approve, concur, coordinate)
the document prior to its receipt by the addressee and when the endorsement
needs to appear on the original document. The "THRU:" line addressee signs
and dates the memo next to his or her name before forwarding. Type "THRU:"
directly below the last line of the "TO:" line. Single space between each
"THRU:" line addressee.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. <b>FROM:</b> Type "FROM:" at the left margin,
two typing returns below the "TO:" or "THRU:" line. The "FROM:" line includes
the mail code and title of the signer. The use of titles is preferred;
however, if an individual does not use a title, it is acceptable to have
a memo to a title from a name or from a name to a name (use full name,
e.g., B/John A. Smith).</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.10. <b>Subject.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Type "SUBJECT:" at the left margin in all caps,
two typing returns below the last line of the "FROM:" line, followed by
the subject. If more than one line is needed for the subject, begin succeeding
lines aligned with the first character of the subject. The subject should
state concisely the main topic of the memo. If acronyms or abbreviations
are used in the subject, spell them out, followed by the acronym in parentheses.
If an abbreviation or acronym is frequently used in a memo, establish it
in the subject line so that only the shortened form is necessary in the
body of the correspondence. Capitalize the first letter of key words within
the subject line, and do not use articles, e.g., a, an, the.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.11. <b>Reference.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Avoid a reference line in a business memo, except
when it contributes to the understanding of the correspondence and when
the number of references is too great to be reasonably incorporated in
the first paragraph of the memo. Type "REF:" at the left margin, blocked,
two typing returns below the last line of the subject, followed by an itemized
listing of the references. Abbreviate items as much as possible; e.g.,
type dates in numerical figures (4/5/94). List references as (a), (b),
(c), single-spaced in chronological order with the most recent date listed
first. See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image2.gif">Figure
4-2</a> of this chapter.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.12 <b>Body of Memo.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Use single-spaced text with double spacing between
paragraphs. Type text paragraphs in block form. When a paragraph is broken
into subparagraphs, number and letter the subparagraphs as shown in <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter3/Image3.gif">Chapter
3, Figure 3-2</a>. Hyphenate sparingly to ensure a uniform right margin.
Avoid hyphenating more than two consecutive lines. Also, avoid hyphenating
at the end of the first line or the end of the last full line of a paragraph.
</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.13. <b>Widows and Orphans</b>.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Do not begin a paragraph near the end of a page
unless there is room for at least two lines on that page. Do not continue
a paragraph on the following page unless two lines can be carried over
to that page.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.14. <b>One-Paragraph Memo.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Double spacing may be used if a memo contains
only one paragraph and consists of 10 lines or less. See <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image2.gif">Figure
4-2</a> of this chapter.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.15. <b>Succeeding Pages.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Type succeeding page(s) of a memo on plain bond
paper. Beginning with the second page, type the page number five lines
from the top of the page, approximately 1 inch to the left of the right
edge of the page. Continue the body of the memo two lines below the page
number. Allow the same margins as for the first page.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.16. <b>Signature Blocks.</b></p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Format.</b> Type the signature information
at the left margin, five typing returns below the last line of text. Type
the name of the signing official only. Do not use Mr., Mrs., Ms., or Dr.,
in the signature block.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>Acting Official.</b> If it is known before
typing that an acting official will sign the memo, type that person's name
and the word "Acting" before the title in the "FROM:" line. If the memo
has been prepared for the signature of the signing official but is actually
signed by another, the person signing will write "for" in front of the
typed name. Mark courtesy copies with the actual signer's name to establish
for the record which person actually signed.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.17. <b>Enclosures.</b></p>
<p class="h025Indent">Any material included with a memo is called an
enclosure. Type the word "Enclosure" flush left, two lines below the signature
block.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Enclosures Identified in the Text.</b> When
possible, identify enclosures in the text in as few words as possible or
by title. If there is more than one enclosure, indicate the number as follows:
</p>
<p class="h075Indent">3 Enclosures</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. <b>Enclosures Not Identified in the Text.</b>
When enclosures are not identified in the text, type the list at the left
margin. List each enclosure on a separate line as in the following examples:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Example of unnumbered enclosures:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">4 Enclosures:
<br>Form Letters Handbook
<br>Plain Letters Pamphlet
<br>Organizational Chart
<br>The NASA Scientific and Technical Information System</p>
<p class="h075Indent">or</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Example of numbered enclosures:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">3 Enclosures:
<br>1. Form Letters Handbook
<br>2. Plain Letters Pamphlet
<br>3. Organizational Chart</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. <b>Marking Enclosures</b>. On the first page
of each enclosure, the word "Enclosure" must be typed in the lower right
corner; e.g., Enclosure, Enclosure 1, Enclosure 2.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.18. <b>Material Sent Under Separate Cover</b>.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">When material referred to in the text is sent
under separate cover, type "Separate Cover:" at the left margin, two typing
returns below the signer's title or the enclosure notation if there is
one. List the material, whether or not it is identified in the text. Send
a copy of the memo with the material that is mailed under separate cover.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Separate Cover:
<br>Form Letters Handbook-10 copies
<br>Plain Letters Pamphlet-10 copies
<br>Correspondence Handbook-10 copies</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.1.19. <b>Courtesy Copies.</b></p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Courtesy Copies (cc:).</b> Type the distribution
of courtesy copies on the original when the recipient needs to know who
received copies. Type "cc:" at the left margin two typing returns below
the last typed line of the signature block, the enclosure line, or separate
cover listing. Directly below "cc:," single space the list of recipients,
using office or organizational codes. Always use titles such as Dr., Mr.,
Mrs., and Ms. on correspondence to outside NASA addressees; titles are
preferred on NASA internal correspondence but may be omitted as long as
names are listed in a consistent format.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Examples (listed in order of preference):</p>
<p class="h05Indent">cc:
<br>G/Mr. Frankle
<br>G/Mr. E. Frankle
<br>G/E. Frankle</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Examples of designating officials in an "Acting"
capacity:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Y/Mr. Smith (Acting)
<br>Y/Mr. M. Smith (Acting)
<br>Y/M. Smith (Acting)</p>
<p class="h075Indent">A dual-column "cc:" distribution list is permitted
when the list is too long to fit in a single column on one page.</p>

<p class="h025Indent"><a NAME="4.1.20.a"></a><b> Official
Record Copy Preparation</b>.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">The official record copy includes all relevant
drafting information concerning the correspondence.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. <b>Blind Courtesy Copies (bcc:).</b> Use blind
courtesy copies when the recipient does not need to know who received copies
or to document the office of record. Type "bcc:" on the official file copy
two typing returns below the "cc:" listing. Never type "bcc:" on the original.
The list should include the official file copy with the Agency filing scheme
number from <A HREF="npg_img/N_PR_1441_001D_/N_PR_1441_001D_.doc">Appendix
A of NPR 1441.1C</a>, Records Retention Schedules. If the memo has enclosures,
indicate any recipients who were not sent enclosures by typing the notation
"(w/o enclosure)" or "(w/o encl.)" after their names or codes. Identifying
"bcc:" recipients and using dual-column "bcc:" lists will follow the same
guidelines as stated in <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=Chapter3">Chapter
3</a>, paragraph 3.1.23.a.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h05Indent">bcc:
<br>FM/Mr. Sullivan
<br>FPH/Official File 1330
<br>FPH/Reading File (w/o enclosure)</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. Identification of Office, Writer, and Typist.</p>
<p class="h075Indent">1) On the bottom of file copies of the signature
page, type the mail code of the preparing office, the writer's first initial
and surname, the typist's initials, date of typing, and the writer's telephone
extension.</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">F/JSmith:abd:2/20/97:1234</p>
<p class="h075Indent"><a NAME="4.1.20.b(2)"></a>2) If the memo is rewritten
or retyped, type a second identification line directly below the first.</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">F/JSmith:abd:2/20/94:1234
<br>Rewritten: F/EJones:olr:2/28/94:8135
<br>Retyped:AI/sdf:3/5/94:1024</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1440_006G_&page_name=main">NPD
1440.6G, NASA Records Management</a>, and <A HREF="npg_img/N_PR_1441_001D_/N_PR_1441_001D_.doc">NPG
1441.1D, Records Retention Schedules</a>, contain the NASA numbering system
for indexing files. Type the official file number on all file copies as
indicated in paragraph 4.1.20.a.</p>

<h2>4.2 Concurrences</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">4.2.1. Obtaining and Documenting Concurrences</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.2.1.1. If concurrences are required, use NASA
Form 1267 (or NASA Form 1267A for laser printers) for the first page of
the official file copy. In the concurrence blocks, codes and names of concurring
officials may be typed. Those officials will sign and date in the space
provided. The signature page will reflect the cc:, bcc:, official file,
reading file, and identification line.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.2.1.2. As an alternative to using NASA Forms
1267 or 1267A, type the mail codes and names of concurring officials on
the bottom of plain paper (first page) which will be used as the official
file copy of the memo. Concurring officials will sign and date in the space
provided.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.2.1.3. Each time a memo is rewritten or retyped,
an updated identification line should be added to the official record copy.
Replace and destroy all previous copies except the official copy and the
marked-up copy. Mark through the previous official copy with a diagonal
red line and staple it to the back of the rewritten one. If the marked-up
copy is other than the official one, fold it and place it on the top of
the package for the attention of the rewriter or reviewer. See <a href="#4.1.20.b(2)">paragraph
4.1.20.b.(2)</a>.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.2.1.4. Sometimes it is necessary to have concurring
or approving officials sign on an original letter or report; e.g., personnel

<h2>4.5 Multiple-Addressee Memos</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Memos are often addressed to more than one individual
and require a variety of formats. Study the figures in this chapter before
preparing a multiple-addressee memo.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.5.1. <b>Address.</b> If a memo is addressed
to more than three individuals, address it to a categorical group. If addressees
do not fall under a category, address the memo to "Distribution." Figures
4-7 through 4-13 (<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image7.gif">4-7</a>
/<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image8.gif">4-8</a> /<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image9.gif">4-9</a>
/<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image10.gif">4-10</a> /<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image11.gif">4-11</a>
/<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image12.gif">4-12</a> /<a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter4/Image13.gif">4-13</a>)
depict forms of addressees for all variations of multiple-addressee memos.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.5.2. <b>Original vs. Photocopy.</b> When addressees
are listed in the "TO:" line of a memo, send each individual an original
memo with an original signature. If addressees are part of a distribution
list, send each addressee a photocopy of the memo and retain the original
in the official file.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4.5.3. <b>Distribution List.</b> Type the name
of the specific group or "Distribution:" two typing returns below the signature
block or the enclosure or separate-cover listing. Begin the list of all
recipients directly beneath the heading. Single space the list and arrange
in alphabetical/ numerical order by Center/Code, except when listing those
names at the signer's Center first. (See <a href="#4.1.19.b">4.1.19.b</a>
for preferred order.) When the list will not fit on the same page as the
signature, type a separate page for the distribution list, or a dual-column
list may be used. After the memo is signed, staple the list in front of

<h1>CHAPTER 6: Electronic Communications</h1>
<hr>

<h2>6.1	General</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Electronic communications, where available, are 
designed to send information quickly, easily, and with the least amount of 
effort to one or more addressee(s). Follow the tips from the <B>Gregg 
Reference Manual</B>, Eighth Edition, page 484, section 1708.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.1.1.	<B>Keep messages short.</B> Hold the 
overall length to 25 lines (the number of lines that will fit on one 
screen). Limit each line to a maximum of 80 characters.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.1.2.	<B>One subject</B>. Restrict each message 
to one subject. It is better to send two separate messages than to cover 
several topics in one message.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.1.3.	<B>Subject line</B>. Provide a subject line 
for each message. A subject line helps the recipient of many messages to 
screen them quickly to determine which require the most prompt action.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.1.4. <B>Do not use all capital letters in your 
messages.</B>  Follow the standard rules of capitalization. Generally, 
capitalizing all letters implies that the writer is shouting at the reader.</P>

<h2>6.2	Electronic Mail Communications</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.1.	E-mail is an alternative communications 
tool to facilitate Government business.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.2.	EUDORA PRO is the NASA Headquarters 
electronic service (e-mail) and is one of three approved clients for the 
Agency. Each NASA Center has a postmaster who is responsible for the 
effective use of the e-mail system and for coordinating with the 
postmasters at the other NASA Centers.  In addition, some NASA Centers and 
individual organizations may have local e-mail systems.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.3.	E-mail systems are not secure. Never use 
them to transmit classified information even if it is encrypted. However, 
sensitive, but unclassified, information may be sent using e-mail if it is 
encrypted.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.4.	Follow the standards, writing styles, and 
formatting as stated in this NPG when e-mail is purposely selected as the 
alternative for sending an official NASA memo and when e-mail is used as 
the communications tool for disseminating other NASA information; e.g., 
special notices and press releases.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.5.	Personalized signature blocks should 
reflect official business information; e.g., sender's name, title, 
organization, telephone and fax numbers.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.2.6.	Follow Management Bulletin: 97:01, Guidance 
for Determining Retention of E-mail (E-mail Documents that are Federal 
Records), to properly manage the creation and retention of documents and 
messages, both record and nonrecord, that were created or transmitted using 
e-mail.</P>

<h2>6.3	Facsimile (Fax)</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">6.3.1.	Use facsimile transmissions to transmit 
official business when there is not enough time to send documents through 
other mail services. Since faxing costs much more than ordinary mail 
services, fax only when necessary. Faxes must be followed up by sending the 
original correspondence for official record purposes. Classified 
information may be faxed only from secure fax systems located at the 
Center's Security Office and some communications centers. Consideration may 
be given to using these secure systems to fax sensitive but unclassified 
information.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.3.2.	<B>Lead Sheet for Fax Transmission</B>. Use 
NASA Form 1120, Facsimile Transmission, as the first page of transmissions 
outside NASA. Show both the sending and receiving Center's name in the "TO:" 
and "FROM:" lines. In addition to the names of the person(s) addressed and 
the telephone numbers, show both the sending and receiving fax numbers, 
voice numbers, and office mail codes. The sender may provide additional 
information concerning the message in the "TO:" section of the form. The 
office of primary responsibility for the transmission keeps the official 
file copy.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.3.3.	<B>Page Size</B>. Pages to be transmitted 
may be 8 1/2 by 11 inches to 8 1/2 by 16 1/2 inches.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.3.4.	<B>Multiple Addressees</B>. Fax machines 
are located in most NASA offices and are very efficient for sending 
time-critical information to one or more addressees. Every NASA Center has 
a Communications Center that has the capability of sending documents to 
multiple addressees simultaneously at a high speed.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.3.5.	<B>Approval Required</B>. Transmissions of 
more than 50 pages require the signature of a Branch Chief or higher level 
official and full justification in the body of the form.</P>

<h2>6.4	Telex (Teleprinter Exchange)</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">6.4.1.	NASA Headquarters and every NASA Center 
have a message center and the capability to send both domestic and 
worldwide telex messages. </P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.4.1.1. A telex is more costly and slower than a fax 
transmission.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.4.1.2. Use telex only when fax or other means of 
communications are unavailable or impractical, e.g., when transmitting to 
the states of the former Soviet Union.</P>

<h2>6.5	NASA Communications Network (NASCOM) Messages</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">6.5.1.	The NASA Communications Network (NASCOM) 
provides operational communications in support of NASA projects and 
program activities.  The NASCOM interconnects such facilities as NASA 
foreign and domestic tracking, telemetry, and command control stations; 
launch areas; test sites; and mission and network control centers. The 
NASCOM network is an unclassified network; NO CLASSIFIED TRANSMISSIONS ARE 
AUTHORIZED. NASCOM should only be used for the transmission of mission and 
nonmission-message traffic that falls within the scope outlined above. 
NASCOM message traffic can be readily identified by the use of unique 
four-character routing indicators, e.g., GNHQ equates to NASA Headquarters.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.5.2.	Contact your Center's message center for 
information on formats and use of NASCOM.</P>

<h2>6.6	Other Types of Communications Services</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">6.6.1.	Many Center message centers provide 
additional types of electronic communications services. Each Center should 
make their users aware of services provided to ensure that the service 
desired is available.  Some of these services are as follows:</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.6.1.1. <B>Priority Letter</B>. Laser-printed 
priority letters are delivered within 2 business days in the United States.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.6.1.2. <B>Telegram</B>. Telegrams are delivered by 
telephone or printed copy in a few hours to any location within the United 
States (except Hawaii) and to Canada.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.6.1.3. <B>Cablegram</B>. Cablegrams are delivered 
overnight by printed copy to Hawaii and overseas locations. Send a 
cablegram to communicate with someone overseas who does not have a fax or 
telex.</P>

<p class="h025Indent">6.6.1.4. <B>Mailgram</B>. Mailgrams are overnight 
letters you can send to anyone in the United States, Puerto Rico, and 
Canada and are delivered with the next day's mail. Consult with your 
Center's Mail Manager for the availability of this type of service.</P>

<h1>CHAPTER 7: Envelopes and Mailing</h1>
<hr>
<h2>7.1 General</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">7.1.1. This chapter explains how to address 
letter-sized envelopes and other mailers for <b>unclassified correspondence</b> 
as depicted in <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter7/Image1.gif">
Figure 7-1</a>.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.1.2. Use only Government-approved envelopes and 
mailers. All official Government mail must be placed in approved mailers,
and all outgoing mail must be processed by a NASA Mail Center.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.1.3. Never place official Government mail in U.S. 
Postal Service (USPS) deposit boxes nor take to USPS Centers.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.1.4. Consult with your Center's Mail Manager to 
obtain information for bulk or mass mailings and use of the NASA mailing
permit indicia.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.1.5. Consult with your Center's Mail Manager or 
mail handbook about types, costs, and restrictions for special mail 
services, including registered, certified, and express and expedited 
mailing or shipping requirements, bulk and mass mailings, and messenger 
services.</p>

<h2>7.2 Envelopes</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.1. Use NASA-printed envelopes (see <a 
href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter7/Image1.gif">Figure 7-1</a>) to send 
official mail through the USPS and to other NASA Centers.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.2. Use preprinted NASA mailing labels on plain
business and flat-sized envelopes and other mailers.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.3. Use the smallest sized envelope necessary to 
reduce postage costs.</p>

<p class="h05Indent"><a NAME="7.2.3.a"></a>a. Letter-sized envelopes (4 
1/8 x 9 1/2 inches). Fold from one to five pages of correspondence to a 
.25-inch maximum thickness.</p>

<p class="h05Indent"><a NAME="7.2.3.b"></a>b. Flat-sized envelopes. Flat 
envelopes are rectangular in shape and exceed one or more of the maximum 
dimensions for letter size envelopes but do not not exceed 15 inches in 
length, 12 inches in height, or .75 inches in thickness. The NASA standard 
flat-sized envelopes are brown 9 1/2 x 12 inches and brown 10 x 12 inches. 
Use flat-sized envleopes for communications that cannot be folded or, if 
folded, will exceed the .25-inch maximum thickness permitted for 
letter-sized envleopes.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.4. Do not use any other envelopes or mailers
other than those described in <a href="#7.2.3.a">7.2.3.a</a>. and <a 
href="#7.2.3.b">7.2.3.b</a>.  above). Consult with your Center's Mail 
Manager before planning to use any other envelope or mailer to ensure they 
comply with NASA policy and USPS restrictions.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.5. Do not use window envelopes to mail general
correspondence. Window envelopes cannot be used to mail material that--</p>

<p class="h05Indent">a. contains national security information,</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. is highly confidential to NASA or the addressee,</p>

<p class="h05Indent">c. is being sent registered mail via the USPS,</p>

<p class="h05Indent">d. is being sent to international addresses, or</p>

<p class="h05Indent">e. is being sent to high-level officials in the
Government or private sector.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.2.6. Do not use staples to secure any size mailer,
including padded mailers.</p>

<p class="h025Indent">7.3.6. International Destinations: Place the name of

<p class="h025Indent">7.3.7. The mail stop or mail code of the originating

<h1>CHAPTER 8: Classified Correspondence</h1>
<hr>
<h2>8.1 Ssfeguards and Controls and Equipment and Supplies</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">8.1.1. <b>Markings</b>. The drafting of classified
correspondence is governed by <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1600_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1600.2A</a>, NASA Security Policy. This is the primary reference for preparing,
marking, and handling classified correspondence. Personnel with or without
a security clearance should review <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1600_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1600.2A</a>. All material requiring protection in the interest of national
security must be marked appropriately to alert people of the classification
level so that the material may be safeguarded.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.2. <b>Equipment and Supplies</b>. To draft
or reproduce classified correspondence, use only typewriters, computers,
and copiers that have been specifically designated for that purpose. NASA
Center Security Offices are the only organizations authorized to confirm
and approve certification of equipment for these purposes.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.3. <b>Safeguards</b>. When classified material
is not under the direct control and supervision of an authorized person,
it must be stored appropriately. Only Center-designated Security Offices
are authorized to approve and designate storage containers.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.3.1. Appropriate storage includes the use
of safes, vaults, or alarmed rooms. Only GSA-approved safes may be used
to store classified materials.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.3.2. Ensure that all classified material is
properly secured in an approved container prior to leaving your work area.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.3.3. Not only must the document be stored
in the safe, but so must related classified notes, drafts, and any typewriter
ribbons and/or disks used in drafting the document.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.1.3.4. There are special stickers to place on
computer disks that contain classified materials. These stickers are available
at your Center's Security Office.</p>

<h2>8.2 Format and Marking</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1. Except for marking requirements, prepare
classified correspondence in the same format as unclassified correspondence.
Executive Order 12958, Classified National Security Information, requires
four specific markings that are required on classified documents. Refer
to Figure <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter8/Image1.gif">8-1</a>.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Portion Marking</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Overall Classification</p>

<p class="h05Indent">A "Classification by" line</p>
<p class="h05Indent">A "Declassify on" line</p>

<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.1. <b>Portion Marking</b>. The first step in
preparing a classified document is for the writer to identify the classification,
or lack thereof, of each component (subject, title, and paragraph) of the
document. This ensures that the reader understands the classification of
specific information. At the beginning of each paragraph, the writer annotates
the classification as listed below and shown in <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter8/Image3.gif">Figure
8-3</a>. Titles and subject lines are marked at the end of the sentence
in a similar fashion.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">(S) SECRET paragraph or portion</p>
<p class="h05Indent">(C) CONFIDENTIAL paragraph or portion</p>
<p class="h05Indent">(U) UNCLASSIFIED Portion</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.2. <b>Overall Classification</b>. The second
step is to determine the overall classification of the document. The overall
classification of a document is determined by the highest classification
of any paragraph in the document. For example, in a five-paragraph document,
if one paragraph is classified as <b>SECRET</b>, another as <b>CONFIDENTIAL</b>,
and the remainder as <b>UNCLASSIFIED</b>, the overall classification of
the document is <b>SECRET</b>. Mark the overall classification clearly
at at both the top and bottom on each page of the document.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.3. <b>Format and Location</b>. This overall
classification must be stamped in capital letters, plainly, and conspicuously
about 1/4 inch from the top and bottom of each page of the document. These
overall classifications, CONFIDENTIAL, SECRET, and TOP SECRET, may be accompanied
by additional information, known as caveats. Caveats are always placed
below the overall classification on the top of the page and above the overall
classification at the bottom of the page.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Top of the page</p>
<p class="h025Indent">SECRET<br>NOFORN</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Bottom of the page</p>
<p class="h025Indent">NOFORN<br>SECRET</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.4. The most common caveats used in drafting
classified correspondence include the following. Refer to <a 
href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1620_001A_&page_name=main">NPR
1620.1A</a>, Security Procedures and Guidelines, and <a 
href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1600_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1600.2A</a>, NASA Security Policy, for further information.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">CNWDI - Critical Nuclear Weapons Design Information
<br>FGI - Foreign Government Information<br>
FRD - Foreign Restricted Data<br>
LIMDIS - Limited Dissemination<br>
NATO - North Atlantic Treaty Organization; Releasable to the 
representatives of the member nations<br>
NOCONTRACT - Restricts access from Contractor personnel<br>
NOFORN - Restricts access from Foreign Nationals<br>
RD - Restricted Data<br>
SAP - Special Access Program<br>
WNINTEL - Warning Intelligence Sources or Methods Involved</p>

<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.5. <b>A "Classified by" Line</b>. The "Classified
by" marking identifies the original classification authority, which is
the originating agency or office. This line is specifically used when the
originator's name does not appear in the document, either in a "FROM" line
or as the signatory of the cover letter. Place the "Classified by" marking
on the bottom lower right of the front or title page. Refer to 
<a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1600_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1600.2A</a> for information on officials authorized to make classification,
declassification, and downgrading decisions; procedures for declassification;
and derivative classification determinations required when classified information
is extracted from source documents and restated in other documents.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Classified by: Office of Space Flight</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.6. <b>A "Declassify on" Line. </b>This marking
indicates the date the document will become declassified. Whenever possible,
cite a specific date or event. If this is not possible, state "Originating
Agency's Determination Required," which means that the information must
be reviewed by the originator before any declassification action can be
taken. Place this marking on the lower right of the cover page or first
page of the document, below the "Classified by" marking.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Declassify on: January 1, 2010</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.7. Occasionally, the original classifier
of a document can predetermine a date or event upon which it will be possible
to downgrade the classification level rather than fully declassifying it.
In those cases, instead of "Declassify on," mark as follows:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">DOWNGRADE TO: SECRET
<br>ON: July 1, 2010</p>
<p class="h025Indent">or:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">DOWNGRADE TO: CONFIDENTIAL
<br>ON: Completion of the 1998 Trade Commission</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.2.1.8. <b>Page, Copy, and Series Numbering</b>.
These marks are used to assist in page counting of SECRET and TOP SECRET
documents and to ensure the ability to account for each copy of the document.
These marks are applied to the upper right corner of all SECRET and TOP
SECRET documents. This marking is not required for CONFIDENTIAL documents.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">This Document consists
<br>of ____ Pages.
<br>Copy No. ____ of _____ copies
<br>Series _________.</p>

<h2>8.3 Transmission of Classified Correspondence</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">8.3.1. Transmission of Classified Correspondence
(see <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/chapter8/Image2.gif">Figure 8-2</a>).
Classified material may be transmitted and disseminated only by approved
methods. Each classification level has its own specific, approved methods.
Refer to <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1600_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1600.2A</a> for detailed instructions. The following are the most common
transmission guidelines.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.3.1.1. <b>Mailing Classified Material</b>. The
USPS may be used to transfer Confidential and Secret material within the
United States as discussed below. However, ALL classified material to be
transferred outside of the United States or its territories must be approved
by the cognizant Center's Security Office.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.3.1.2. <b>Confidential</b>. Send by registered
or certified mail within the United States or its territories via the USPS.
</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.3.1.3. <b>Secret</b>. Send by registered mail
via the USPS.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.3.1.4. <b>Top Secret</b>. NEVER send Top Secret
material through any mail system. Top Secret material may only be transferred
by authorized and approved electronic means or carried by cleared couriers.
All Top Secret material should be logged in and out by the Top Secret Control
Officer of your Center's Security Office.</p>

<h2>8.4 Addressing Classified Correspondence</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">8.4.1. Always address classified material to an
activity, not to an individual.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.2. The outer envelope must include the complete
destination address and your activity's complete return address.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.3. Double-wrap classified material and correspondence;
use two envelopes. The outside envelope must conform to the instructions
detailed within this NPR. The inner envelope must have both addresses as
shown on the outer envelope, plus the overall classification of the document
being transferred. Mark the classification level on the front and back
top, and bottom of the envelope.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.4. The inner envelope must have both addresses
as shown on the outer envelope, plus the overall classification of the
document being transferred.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.5. This classification mark will be placed
on the front and back of the envelope and the top and bottom.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.6. Attach a classified material receipt (NASA
Form 387) to the inner envelope for the addressee to sign and return to
your activity. This confirms that the addressee received the document.
For specific instructions on sending Top Secret material, refer to <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1620_002A_&page_name=main">NPD
1620.2A</a> or contact the Center's Security Office. If receipt is not
confirmed within 20 days, initiate tracer action. If this action does not
confirm receipt, contact the Center's Security Office.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.7. Internal to NASA Centers. When you take
classified documents outside the work area, you must carry them in a sealed,
opaque envelope to prevent advertising that you are carrying classified
material. When you are using classified material within the work space,
it should have a classified cover sheet on the document if it is outside
of the container, e.g., on your desk.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8.4.8. External to NASA Centers. If you are required
to hand-carry classified documents outside of a NASA Center, you must receive
a briefing and a courier card from your Center's Security Office before
you are approved to take classified documents offsite.</p>

<h1>APPENDIX A: Administrator's Correspondence Preparation and Control</h1>

<hr>
<p class="h025Indent">1. Before preparing memos or letters for officials
in the Office of the Administrator, study the figures in this appendix.
Prepare correspondence for signature of the officials in the Office of
the Administrator in a clear, direct, and concise style and confine to
one page whenever possible. If additional information is essential, develop
it in an enclosure.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. Letters. Prepare letters in the modified
semiblock style, indenting paragraphs five spaces (see Appendix A, <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/appendixa/Image1.gif">Figure
A-1</a>). Do not right-justify margins.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. Memos. Prepare memos in block style, following
the general guidelines outlined in Chapter 4: Memorandums (see <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/appendixa/Image2.gif">Figure
A-2</a> of this appendix). Do not right-justify margins.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. Approval Memos. When requesting approval of
officials in the Office of the Administrator, prepare a memo for the official's
signature containing the proposed response. See Appendix A, <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/appendixa/Image3.gif">Figure
A-3</a>.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">d. White House and Congressional Correspondence.
See <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixB">Appendix
B</a>, White House and Congressional Correspondence.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">2. Stationery. Use Office of the Administrator's
letterhead for preparing letters and memos for the signature of officials
in the Office of the Administrator. Administrator's letterhead is available
from the OCIC or from the GSFC Supply Center through designated 
code-specific authorizing officials (usually the lead secretary of the 
code).</p>
<p class="h025Indent">3. Type. Use any font for the Administrator's
correspondence that is easy to read. Suggested fonts are courier, times
new roman, or arial, ranging in sizes from 10 to 12.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">4. Reply to Attn of: The Administrator's letterhead
does not contain a "Reply to Attn of:" line. Do not show the originating
office's code and mail stop on either original memos or letters.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">5. Date. Do not date the letter or memo. Correspondence
is date stamped in either the Administrator's office or OCIC.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">6. Salutation. <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1450_010C_&page_name=AppendixE">Appendix
E</a> lists models of addresses, including proper salutations. For addresses
not listed, or if in doubt as to proper salutation or complimentary close,
contact the OCIC.<p>
<p class="h025Indent">7. Complimentary Close/Signature Block on Letters.
Type the complimentary close and signature block on letters five spaces
to the right of the center. <a href="#17">See 17</a>. Signature Blocks
of this Appendix and also <a href="npg_img/N_PR_1450_010C_/appendixa/Image1.gif">Figure
A-1</a>, page A-6, of this Appendix. These examples indicate the proper
complimentary close and signature blocks for officials in the Office of
the Administrator.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">8. Copies.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. The OCIC requires three file copies:</p>
<p class="h075Indent">Yellow official file copy-Showing concurring
initials: (NASA Form 1267) OCIC official file copy.</p>
<p class="h075Indent">White file copy-OCIC file copy (plain bond).</p>
<p class="h075Indent">White file copy (letterhead copy)-Stamped and
returned to Action Office as its official file copy; action office uses
this copy to reproduce and distribute cc's and bcc's.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. Original. The OCIC will dispatch the original.
Absence of an envelope indicates that the original will be mailed by the
Action Office. For addressees outside NASA Headquarters, the Action Office
is to include properly addressed envelopes (see <a href="#13.c">paragraph
13.c</a>. of this appendix).</p>
<p class="h025Indent">9. Distribution of Copies. Keep reproduction and
distribution of information copies to the minimum required.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. Courtesy Copies (cc). Type cc's on
the original only when it is necessary for the recipient of the letter
to know who received copies. After signature, the OCIC will return a photocopied
original to the action office for distribution to cc's, or if no cc's are
required, this photocopy will serve as a record of the signature for the
Action Office.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. Blind Courtesy Copies (bcc). Type bcc's on
file copies only. The bcc's include all concurring officials and individuals
who received a copy of the incoming document. After obtaining signature,
the OCIC will return a file copy to the preparing office for reproduction
and internal NASA distribution.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">10. Administrator's Control Number and Suspense Date.
Show the Administrator's control number and suspense date on all file and
internal information copies (bcc's). Do not include any of this identifying
information on cc's or copies going outside NASA.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">11. Identification of Office, Writer, and Typist.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Example:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">BPM/RFJones:pld:12/12/92:4831</p>
<p class="h025Indent">If rewritten or retyped:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">BPM/RFJones:pld:12/12/92:4831
<br>Rewritten:BFB/JLSmith:ofb:1/4/93:3675
<br>Retyped:BFB/ofb:1/5/93:3675</p>
<p class="h025Indent">12. Action Document Summary (ADS).</p>
<p class="h05Indent">a. NHQ Form 117, Action Document Summary,

<p class="h05Indent">b. ADS guidelines:</p>

<p class="h075Indent">2. Type on the original form or on a reproduced

<p class="h075Indent">3. Limit to one page. If it is necessary to use

<p class="h025Indent">16. Coordination of Assigned Actions.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. Extending or Canceling Suspense Requirements.

prior to the expiration of the original suspense date.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">d. Telephone Reply. When a reply to a letter is

26) or e-mail your analyst stating the following: "Handled by telephone

a brief summary of the telephone conversation.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">e. Change in Designation of Signing Official.</p>
<p class="h075Indent">1. If a reply may be signed by an official
outside the Administrator's office (e.g., head of a Headquarters office),
request a change of signature level in writing or by telephone from the

<p class="h075Indent">2. When a reply is sent from a Headquarters office,

OCIC. The Action Office prepares and sends all required copies.</p>

<h1>Appendix C: Privacy Act Correspondence</h1>

<hr>
<h2>1. General.</h2>
<p class="h025Indent">a. Privacy Act Correspondence must be
safeguarded as prescribed by <a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PD_1382_017E_&page_name=main">NPD
1382.17E</a>, Privacy Act-Internal NASA Direction In Furtherance of NASA Regulation.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. Correspondence falls within the purview of
the Privacy Act if it contains any item, collection, or grouping of information
about an individual, including, but not limited to, the individual's education,
financial transactions, medical history, and criminal or employment history,
and if it contains the individual's name or an identifying number (such
as a social security number), symbol, or other identifying particular assigned
to the individual, such as a fingerprint, voiceprint, or photograph. "Individual"
means a citizen or permanent resident of the United States. The following
documents contain Privacy Act information:</p>
<p class="h05Indent">SF 171, Personal Qualifications Statement</p>
<p class="h05Indent">SF 52, Request for Personnel Action</p>
<p class="h05Indent">SF 50, Notification of Personnel Action</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Financial Statements</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Pay Statements</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. Correspondence containing any item of information
as described in <a href="#2">paragraph 2</a> of this appendix, which is
removed from a system of records not under the control of a system manager
or an authorized representative, is to be prominently identified as a record(s)
protected by the Privacy Act. Use NASA Form 1534, The Attached Material
is Subject to the Privacy Act of 1974, as a cover sheet for the correspondence.
If NASA Form 1534 is unavailable, identify the records as being protected
by the Act by using the wording in NASA Form 1534.</p>

<h2><a NAME="2"></a>2. Transmitting Privacy Act Correspondence.</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">a. Within NASA Center distribution channels,
seal information in a single envelope or package reflecting the addressee
by title and the return address, and mark it--</p>
<p class="h05Indent">"TO BE OPENED BY ADDRESSEE ONLY"</p>
<p class="h05Indent">Incoming correspondence so marked will be opened
as prescribed by the system manager.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">b. Send information between NASA Centers in a
single sealed envelope, and mark it as in subparagraph above.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">c. Send information between other Government agencies
and non-Government agencies via the USPS in a sealed inner and outer envelope,
reflecting the addressee by title, and mark the return address on both
envelopes. Additionally, the inner envelope must be marked--</p>
<p class="h075Indent">"TO BE OPENED BY ADDRESSEE ONLY"</p>

<h2>3. Destroying Privacy Act Correspondence.</h2> 

<p class="h025Indent">Forward Privacy Act material to your Center's mail 
center in a plain brown wrapper or burn bag for shredding or burning. Check 
<a href="displayCA.cfm?Internal_ID=N_PR_1441_001C_&page_name=main">NPR
1441.1C</a>, Records Retention Schedules, before destroying.</p>

<h1>APPENDIX D:  Foreign Correspondence</h1>
<hr>

<h2>1. General. </h2>
<p class="h025Indent">Due to political sensitivities and concerns regarding 
potential technology transfer, NASA policy requires special handling of all 
official correspondence to designated areas.</P>

<p class="h05Indent">a. The list of designated countries and guidelines for 
transmitting correspondence to the Office of External Relations for review 
is provided periodically in the form of a memorandum to NASA 
Officials-in-Charge of Headquarters Offices, Directors of NASA Centers, and 
Director, JPL.</p>

<p class="h05Indent">b. All correspondence to designated areas requires the 
concurrence of the Office of External Relations.</p>

<h2>2. Format. </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Prepare correspondence for individuals overseas in 
the standard business-letter style.</P>

<h2>3. Address. </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">The country name is the official name, e.g., Federal 
Republic of Germany, not West Germany. The Office of External Relations can 
provide official country names.</P>

<h2>4. Avoid Jargon. </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Avoid the use of U.S. jargon and acronyms, which may 
not be familiar to non-U.S. personnel.</P>

<h2>5. Coordination with Headquarters. </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Transmit all correspondence with non-NASA U.S. 
Embassy personnel through the Office of External Relations, NASA 
Headquarters.</P>

<h2>6. Capitalization. </h2>

<p class="h025Indent">Capitalize the country name in the inside address. 
However, USPS policy requires that the name of the country be in all 
capital letters and appear on the last line of the address information on 
the envelope or mailer.</P>

<h1>APPENDIX E: Forms of Addresses</h1>
<hr>
<h2>1. General. </h2>
<p class="h025Indent">The forms of address in this appendix
are the conventional forms of address as determined by social and official
custom. Since address forms may vary in certain instances, it is important
to consult the rules as they appear in the list that follows on page E-2.</p>

<h2>2. Titles of Addressees.</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">a. "The Honorable" is a title used to address current
and former officials, e.g., Presidential appointees, Federal and State
elected officials, and mayors. "Honorable" is not used by itself. "The
Honorable" is not used before a last name alone. When appearing within the 
text of a letter or other communication, "the" is not capitalized.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. "The Honorable" may be replaced by a title such 
as "Dr." or "General," but it is not used in combination with another 
title.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. After leaving a titled position, an individual
may opt to retain that distinctive title ("Judge," "General," or "The 
Honorable") throughout his or her lifetime.</p>

<h2>3. Abbreviation of Titles and Degrees.</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">a. With the exceptions of "Mr./Ms./Dr.," do not 
abbreviate titles preceding full names in an address. Long titles such as 
"Lieutenant Colonel" and "Brigadier General" may be abbreviated for the 
sake of balance and appearance in the address when necessary, e.g., Lt. 
Colonel John L. Smith. Note that the basic rank is not abbreviated.  The 
salutation should read, "Dear Colonel Smith."</p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. Abbreviate designations of degrees, religious
and fraternal orders, and military service branch titles after a name.
Type the initials of a degree or order with periods but no spaces between
them. Type military service designations in capital letters without 
periods.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. Do not use scholastic degrees in combination
with complimentary titles of address or with military service ranks, except
in the case of "The Reverend." When a name is followed by abbreviations, their sequence is as follows: religious
orders first, theological degrees, academic degrees earned in course, and
honorary degrees in order of the bestowal. Using more than two degrees
is unnecessary.</p>
<p class="h05Indent">John Doe, D.D., D.Lit.
<br>Patricia Smith, M.D.
<br>Henry Brown, LL.D., Ph.D.
<br>Lt. Colonel Mary Hill, USAF
<br>The Reverend George Green, D.D.</p>

<h2>4. Spouse of Distinguished Individual.</h2>

<p class="h025Indent">va. An individual does not share a spouse's
official title and, therefore, should be addressed individually by his
or her own appropriate title and last name.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">The American Ambassador and Mrs. Doe</p>
<p class="h025Indent">b. In cases of the titled spouse being a female,
the following rule applies:</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Justice Sandra Day O'Connor and Mr. O'Connor</p>
<p class="h025Indent">c. Not all married women use their husband's name,
especially if they use another name professionally.</p>
<p class="h025Indent">Senator Dianne Feinstein and Mr. Richard Blum</p>
<p class="h025Indent">The Honorable Strobe Talbott and Ms. Brooke Shearer</p>
<p class="h025Indent">The forms of address listed in this appendix are
the conventional forms of address as determined by social and official
custom. The style of salutation and complimentary close is informal.</p>

<h1>APPENDIX F. Compound Words</h1>
