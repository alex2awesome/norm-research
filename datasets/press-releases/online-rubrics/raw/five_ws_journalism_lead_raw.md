<p><b>The Five Ws</b> is a checklist used in <a href="/wiki/Journalism" title="Journalism">journalism</a> to ensure that the <a href="/wiki/News_style#Lead" title="News style">lead</a> contains all the essential points of a story. As far back as 1913, reporters were taught that the lead should answer these questions about the situation being reported:<sup id="cite&#95;ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li><i>Who?</i></li>
<li><i>What?</i></li>
<li><i>Where? </i></li>
<li><i>Why?</i></li>
<li><i>When? </i></li></ul>
<p>Journalism students are taught that these are the fundamental five questions of newswriting.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:3&#95;2-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:3-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Reporters also use the "5 Ws" to guide research and interviews and to raise important ethical questions, such as "How do you know that?".<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Use_outside_of_journalism">Use outside of journalism</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Five_Ws&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Use outside of journalism"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>In England and Wales, the Five Ws are used in <a href="/wiki/Key_Stage_2" title="Key Stage 2">Key Stage 2</a> and <a href="/wiki/Key_Stage_3" title="Key Stage 3">Key Stage 3</a> lessons (ages 7–14).<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5ws-in-ks3&#95;4-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5ws-in-ks3-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> In data analytics, the Five Ws are used in the first stage of the <a href="/wiki/BADIR" title="BADIR">BADIR</a> to identify the business problem and its context in an analytics request.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>Militaries use the five Ws to convey the details of tactical tasks within a broader mission as part of the <a href="/wiki/Five_paragraph_order" title="Five paragraph order">five paragraph order</a>.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Origins_in_antiquity">Origins in antiquity</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Five_Ws&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Origins in antiquity"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>The Five Ws are rooted in the seven questions used in ancient Greece to communicate stories clearly:<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> 
</p><p>Although long attributed to <a href="/wiki/Hermagoras_of_Temnos" title="Hermagoras of Temnos">Hermagoras of Temnos</a>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:2&#95;7-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> in 2010, it was established that <a href="/wiki/Aristotle" title="Aristotle">Aristotle</a>'s <i><a href="/wiki/Nicomachean_Ethics" title="Nicomachean Ethics">Nicomachean Ethics</a></i> is in fact the source of the elements of circumstance or <i>Septem Circumstantiae</i>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;8-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas" title="Thomas Aquinas">Thomas Aquinas</a> had much earlier acknowledged Aristotle as the originator of the elements of circumstances, providing a detailed commentary on Aristotle's system in his "Treatise on human acts" and specifically in part one of two Q7 "Of the Circumstances of Human Acts". Aquinas examines the concept of Aristotle's voluntary and involuntary action in his <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la"><a href="/wiki/Summa_Theologiae" class="mw-redirect" title="Summa Theologiae">Summa Theologiae</a></i></span></i> as well as a further set of questions about the elements of circumstance.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:1&#95;9-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> Primarily, he asks "Whether a circumstance is an accident of a human act" (Article 1), "Whether Theologians should take note of the circumstances of human acts?" (Article 2), "Whether the circumstances are properly set forth (in Aristotle's) third book of Ethics" (Article 3) and "Whether the most important circumstances are 'Why' and 'In What the act consists'?" (Article 4).
</p>
<blockquote><p>For in acts we must take note of <i>who</i> did it, by what aids or instruments he did it (<i>with</i>), <i>what</i> he did, <i>where</i> he did it, <i>why</i> he did it, <i>how</i> and <i>when</i> he did it.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:1&#95;9-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>For Aristotle, the elements are used to distinguish voluntary or involuntary action, a crucial distinction for him.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> These elements of circumstances are used by Aristotle as a framework to describe and evaluate moral action in terms of What was or should be done, Who did it, How it was done, Where it happened, and most importantly for what reason (Why), and so on for all the other elements:
</p>
<blockquote><p>Therefore it is not a pointless endeavor to divide these circumstances by kind and number; (1) the <i>Who</i>, (2) the <i>What</i>, (3) around what place (<i>Where</i>) or (4) in which time something happens (<i>When</i>), and sometimes (5) with what, such as an instrument (<i>With</i>), (6) for the sake of what (<i>Why</i>), such as saving a life, and (7) the (<i>How</i>), such as gently or violently…And it seems that the most important circumstances are those just listed, including the <i>Why</i>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;8-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>For Aristotle, ignorance of any of these elements can imply involuntary action:
</p>
<blockquote><p>Thus, with ignorance as a possibility concerning all these things, that is, <i>the circumstances of the act</i>, the one who acts in ignorance of any of them seems to act involuntarily, and especially regarding the most important ones. And it seems that the most important circumstances are those just listed, including the <i>Why</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;8-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In the <a href="/wiki/Politics_(Aristotle)" title="Politics (Aristotle)"><i>Politics</i></a>, Aristotle illustrates why the elements are important in terms of human (moral) action:
</p>
<blockquote><p>I mean, for instance (a particular circumstance or movement or action), How could we advise the Athenians whether they should go to war or not, if we did not know their strength (<i>How much</i>), whether it was naval or military or both (<i>What kind</i>), and how great it is (<i>How many</i>), what their revenues amount to (<i>With</i>), Who their friends and enemies are (<i>Who</i>), what wars, too they have waged (<i>What</i>), and with what success; and so on.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-11" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Essentially, these elements of circumstances provide a theoretical framework that can be used to particularize, explain or predict <i>any</i> given set of circumstances of action. Hermagoras went so far as to claim that <i>all</i> <a href="/wiki/Hypothesis" title="Hypothesis">hypotheses</a> are derived from these seven circumstances:
</p>
<blockquote><p>In other words, no hypothetical question, or question involving particular persons and actions, can arise without reference to these circumstances, and no demonstration of such a question can be made without using them.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:2&#95;7-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:2-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>In any particular act or situation, one needs to interrogate these questions in order to determine the actual circumstances of the action.
</p>
<blockquote><p>It is necessary for students of virtue to differentiate between the Voluntary and Involuntary; such a distinction should even prove useful to the lawmaker for assigning honors and punishments.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:0&#95;8-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:0-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>This aspect is encapsulated by Aristotle in <a href="/wiki/Rhetoric_(Aristotle)" title="Rhetoric (Aristotle)"><i>Rhetoric</i></a> as <i>forensic speech</i> and is used to determine <i>"The characters and circumstances which lead men to commit wrong, or make them the victims of wrong"</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> to accuse or defend. It is this application of the elements of circumstances that was emphasised by latter rhetoricians.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="Usage_in_rhetoric">Usage in rhetoric</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Five_Ws&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Usage in rhetoric"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Even though the classical origin of these questions as situated in ethics had long been lost, they have been a standard way of formulating or analyzing <a href="/wiki/Rhetorical_question" title="Rhetorical question">rhetorical questions</a> since antiquity.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-13" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-13"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>13<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> The rhetor Hermagoras of Temnos, as quoted in <a href="/wiki/Pseudo-Augustine" title="Pseudo-Augustine">pseudo-Augustine</a>'s <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">De Rhetorica</i></span></i>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-14" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-14"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>14<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> applied Aristotle's "elements of circumstances" (<span title="Ancient Greek (to 1453)-language text"><span lang="grc" style="font-style: normal;">μόρια περιστάσεως</span></span>)<sup id="cite&#95;ref-15" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-15"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>15<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> as the <a href="/wiki/Locus_(rhetoric)" class="mw-redirect" title="Locus (rhetoric)">loci</a> of an issue:
</p>
<dl><dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, quando, ubi, cur, quem ad modum, quibus adminiculis</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-17" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-17"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>17<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd>(Who, what, when, where, why, in what way, by what means)</dd></dl><p>
Aquinas<sup id="cite&#95;ref-:1&#95;9-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> also refers to the elements as used by <a href="/wiki/Cicero" title="Cicero">Cicero</a> in <span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la"><a href="/wiki/De_Inventione" title="De Inventione">De Inventione</a></i></span> (Chap. 24 DD1, 104) as:</p><blockquote><p><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, ubi, quibus auxiliis, cur, quomodo, quando</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-:1&#95;9-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-:1-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote><p>Similarly, <a href="/wiki/Quintilian" title="Quintilian">Quintilian</a> discussed <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">loci argumentorum</i></span></i>, but did not put them in the form of questions.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Gaius_Marius_Victorinus" title="Gaius Marius Victorinus">Victorinus</a> explained Cicero's application of the elements of circumstances by putting them into correspondence with Hermagoras questions 5 W's and an H&#160;:<sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<figure class="mw-default-size mw-halign-center" typeof="mw:File"><a href="/wiki/File:Victorinus.gif" class="mw-file-description" title="quis=persona; quid=factum; cur=causa; ubi=locus; quando=tempus; quemadmodum = modus; quib/adminiculis=facultas"><img alt="quis=persona; quid=factum; cur=causa; ubi=locus; quando=tempus; quemadmodum = modus; quib/adminiculis=facultas" src="//upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/41/Victorinus.gif" decoding="async" width="501" height="137" class="mw-file-element" data-file-width="501" data-file-height="137" /></a><figcaption>quis=persona; quid=factum; cur=causa; ubi=locus; quando=tempus; quemadmodum = modus; quib/adminiculis=facultas</figcaption></figure>
<p><a href="/wiki/Julius_Victor" class="mw-redirect" title="Julius Victor">Julius Victor</a> also lists circumstances as questions.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p><a href="/wiki/Boethius" title="Boethius">Boethius</a> "made the seven circumstances fundamental to the arts of prosecution and defense":
</p>
<dl><dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, cur, quomodo, ubi, quando, quibus auxiliis</i></span></i>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd>(Who, what, why, how, where, when, with what)</dd></dl>
<p>The question form was taken up again in the 12th century by <a href="/wiki/Thierry_of_Chartres" title="Thierry of Chartres">Thierry of Chartres</a> and <a href="/wiki/John_of_Salisbury" title="John of Salisbury">John of Salisbury</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-circ&#95;16-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-circ-16"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>16<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>To administer suitable <a href="/wiki/Penance" title="Penance">penance</a> to <a href="/wiki/Sin" title="Sin">sinners</a>, the 21st canon of the <a href="/wiki/Fourth_Lateran_Council" class="mw-redirect" title="Fourth Lateran Council">Fourth Lateran Council</a> (1215) enjoined confessors to investigate both sins and the circumstances of the sins. The question form was popular for guiding confessors, and it appeared in several different forms:<sup id="cite&#95;ref-18" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-18"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>18<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<dl><dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, ubi, per quos, quoties, cur, quomodo, quando</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-19" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-19"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>19<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, ubi, quibus auxiliis, cur, quomodo, quando</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-20" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-20"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>20<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quis, quid, ubi, cum quo, quotiens, cur, quomodo, quando</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-21" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-21"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>21<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quid, quis, ubi, quibus auxiliis, cur, quomodo, quando</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-22" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-22"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>22<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd>
<dd><i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">Quid, ubi, quare, quantum, conditio, quomodo, quando: adiuncto quoties</i></span>.</i><sup id="cite&#95;ref-23" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-23"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>23<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></dd></dl>
<p>The method of questions was also used for the systematic <a href="/wiki/Exegesis" title="Exegesis">exegesis</a> of a text.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-24" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-24"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>24<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p><p>In the 16th century, <a href="/wiki/Thomas_Wilson_(rhetorician)" title="Thomas Wilson (rhetorician)">Thomas Wilson</a> wrote in English verse:
</p>
<style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1244412712">.mw-parser-output .templatequote{overflow:hidden;margin:1em 0;padding:0 32px}.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{line-height:1.5em;text-align:left;margin-top:0}@media(min-width:500px){.mw-parser-output .templatequotecite{padding-left:1.6em}}</style><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>Who, what, and where, by what helpe, and by whose:<br />
Why, how, and when, doe many things disclose.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Thomas&#95;Wilson&#95;25-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Thomas_Wilson-25"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>25<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>In the United States in the 19th century, <a href="/wiki/William_Cleaver_Wilkinson" title="William Cleaver Wilkinson">William Cleaver Wilkinson</a> popularized the "Three Ws" – What? Why? What of it? – as a method of Bible study in the 1880s, although he did not claim originality. This eventually became the "Five W's", but the application was rather different from that in journalism:
</p>
<blockquote>
<p>"What? Why? What of it?" is a plan of study of alliterative methods for the teacher emphasized by Professor W.C. Wilkinson not as original with himself but as of venerable authority. "It is, in fact," he says, "an almost immemorial orator's analysis. First the facts, next the proof of the facts, then the consequences of the facts. This analysis has often been expanded into one known as "The Five W's": "When? Where? Who? What? Why?" Hereby attention is called, in the study of any lesson: to the date of its incidents; to their place or locality; to the person speaking or spoken to, or to the persons introduced, in the narrative; to the incidents or statements of the text; and, finally, to the applications and uses of the lesson teachings.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-26" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-26"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>26<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
</blockquote>

<div class="side-box-text plainlist">English <a href="/wiki/Wikisource" title="Wikisource">Wikisource</a> has original text related to this article:
<div style="margin-left: 10px;"><b><a href="https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/en:The_Elephant%27s_Child" class="extiw" title="s:en:The Elephant&#39;s Child">The Elephant's Child</a></b></div></div></div>
</div>
<p>The "Five W's" (and one H) were memorialized by <a href="/wiki/Rudyard_Kipling" title="Rudyard Kipling">Rudyard Kipling</a> in his <i><a href="/wiki/Just_So_Stories" title="Just So Stories">Just So Stories</a></i> (1902), in which a poem, accompanying the tale of <i>The Elephant's Child</i>,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-27" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-27"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>27<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> opens with:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1244412712" /><blockquote class="templatequote"><div class="poem">
<p>I keep six honest serving-men<br />
(They taught me all I knew);<br />
Their names are What and Why and When<br />
And How and Where and Who.
</p>
</div></blockquote>
<p>By 1917, the "Five Ws" were being taught in high-school journalism classes,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-28" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-28"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>28<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> and by 1940, the tendency of journalists to address all of the "Five Ws" within the <a href="/wiki/Lead_paragraph" title="Lead paragraph">lead paragraph</a> of an article was being characterized as old-fashioned and fallacious:
</p>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1244412712" /><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>The old-fashioned lead of the five Ws and the H, crystallized largely by Pulitzer's "<a href="/wiki/New_Journalism" title="New Journalism">new journalism</a>" and sanctified by the schools, is widely giving way to the much more supple and interesting feature lead, even on straight news stories.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-29" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-29"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>29<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<link rel="mw-deduplicated-inline-style" href="mw-data:TemplateStyles:r1244412712" /><blockquote class="templatequote"><p>All of you know about – and I hope all of you admit the fallacy of – the doctrine of the five Ws in the first sentence of the newspaper story.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-30" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-30"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>30<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></p></blockquote>
<p>Starting in the 2000s, the Five Ws were sometimes misattributed to Rudyard Kipling (referred to as "The Kipling Method"), especially in the management and quality literature,<sup id="cite&#95;ref-31" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-31"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>31<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup><sup id="cite&#95;ref-32" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-32"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>32<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup> and contrasted with the <a href="/wiki/Five_whys" title="Five whys">five whys</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-33" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-33"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>33<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>

<p>In English, most of the <a href="/wiki/Interrogative_words" class="mw-redirect" title="Interrogative words">interrogative words</a> begin with the same letters, <i>wh-</i>; in <a href="/wiki/Latin" title="Latin">Latin</a>, most also begin with the same letters, <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">qu</i></span>-</i>. This is not coincidental as they are <a href="/wiki/Cognates" class="mw-redirect" title="Cognates">cognates</a> derived from the <a href="/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_language" title="Proto-Indo-European language">Proto-Indo-European</a> interrogative pronoun root <span title="Proto-Indo-European-language text">&#42;<i lang="ine"><a href="/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_pronouns#Interrogative/indefinite_pronoun" title="Proto-Indo-European pronouns">k<sup>w</sup>o-</a></i></span>, reflected in <a href="/wiki/Proto-Germanic" class="mw-redirect" title="Proto-Germanic">Proto-Germanic</a> as <span title="Proto-Germanic-language text">&#42;<i lang="gem">χ<sup>w</sup>a-</i></span> or <span title="Proto-Germanic-language text">&#42;<i lang="gem">kh<sup>w</sup>a-</i></span> and in Latin as <i><span title="Latin-language text"><i lang="la">qu</i></span>-</i>. <sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources.
