9 of the Biggest Super Bowl Ad Fumbles

These commercials had a huge impact, but not necessarily for the right reasons

 
By Cydney Lee

The future of brands gets decided here. Join the industry’s top marketers at Brandweek for the ideas, insights, and connections shaping what’s next. Get your ticket.

With over 120 million eyeballs watching, the Super Bowl is an easy way for advertisers to generate national buzz. For the same reason, it’s also the quickest way to crash and burn if an ad falls flat.

At $8 million pop for a slot in this year’s Big Game, hedging bets on a spot designed to create controversy is also a big financial risk.

However, according to Patti Williams, an associate professor of marketing at the Wharton School of the University of Pennsylvania, sometimes the juice is worth the squeeze.

“One of those risks might be the financial risk [and] another is a creative risk,” she told ADWEEK. “I think those risks can absolutely pay off. People don’t want to see boring ads at the Super Bowl. The people and brands making these ads want to be memorable, they want to be talked about afterward, ideally, they want to be remembered for their positive impact. But sometimes the KPIs they’re looking for are just impact.”

In years gone by, brands like GoDaddy and Carl’s Jr. have become synonymous with sexually provocative Super Bowl ads. A slew of others including Snickers and 84 Lumber have also come under fire for controversial Big Game spots touching on social issues including homophobia and illegal immigration.

Williams said while Super Bowl ad fumbles may hurt brands in the moment, controversy ultimately has the power to boost awareness, drive engagement and spark conversation.

Ahead of Super Bowl Sunday, ADWEEK has rounded up some of the biggest Super Bowl ad snafus of all time. We also explore their impact on each brand.

1. Snickers: “Mechanics” (2007)

Agency: TBWA\Chiat\Day New York

Why it got people talking? The ad showed two mechanics accidentally kissing while eating the same candy bar before ripping out some chest hair to do something “manly.” Masterfoods USA, a former subsidiary of Mars Inc. and former maker of Snickers, received complaints from the Human Rights Campaign and the Gay and Lesbian Alliance Against Defamation, calling the company and the ad homophobic.

What happened next?  “Mechanics” was pulled from TV and Snickers’ website. Former Masterfoods spokesperson Alice Nathanson released a statement, saying that the brand’s goal was “to capture the attention of our core Snickers consumer” and that “feedback from our target consumers has been positive.”

“We know that humor is highly subjective and understand that some people may have found the ad offensive. Clearly that was not our intent,” she wrote. “Consequently, we do not plan to continue to air the ad on television or on our Snickers website.”

2. General Motors: “Robot” (2007)

Agency: Deutsch

Why it got people talking? This ad showed a quality-obsessed yellow robot jumping off a bridge in a dream sequence after screwing up on the job. After it aired to 93 million people during Super Bowl 41, General Motors (GM) received backlash from groups like the American Foundation for Suicide Prevention who claimed the ad was insensitive to people who were affected by suicide. 

What happened next?  At first, GM doubled down on the ad, saying in a statement that it “conveyed how GM employees, together with our unions, [were] building the best cars, trucks, SUVs and crossovers in our history.” After continued backlash, the brand then removed the ad from TV and online and said it would rework it to have an alternate ending. “We are editing the ad as we speak and it will run again in the near future,” a company spokesperson said. 

3. Nationwide: “Make Safe Happen” (2015)

Agency: Ogilvy

Why it got people talking? This spot showed a young boy explaining how he won’t be able to grow up because of an accident that happened in his youth, but Super Bowl 49 viewers took to social media to criticize Nationwide’s ad for being morbid and an overall buzzkill. “We’re Nationwide Insurance! EVERYONE DIES. Enjoy the game! Nationwide,” one post read.

What happened next? Nationwide released a statement saying it “knew the ad would spur a variety of reactions,” but “the sole purpose of this message was to start a conversation, not sell insurance.”

4.GoDaddy: “Journey Home” (2015)

Agency: Barton F. Graf 9000

Why it got people talking? Animal rights advocates aired their grievances about “Journey Home” on social media, saying that puppies purchased online often come from puppy mills. One advocate Helena Yurcho launched a petition online to have the ad removed. “Essentially, GoDaddy is encouraging private breeding/puppy mills while shelter animals wait patiently for their forever homes or worse—to be euthanized,” she wrote.

What happened next? After receiving backlash, former GoDaddy chief executive officer (CEO) Blake Irving said it would not air its ad during the game. It was also removed from YouTube. PETA weighed in, too, telling ADWEEK: “GoDaddy’s now-yanked commercial showed that anyone who sells a dog online is a callous jerk.”

5. Carl’s Jr.: “All Natural” (2015)

Agency: 72andSunny

Why it got people talking? Adding to its list of racy, naked women-fronted ads, Carl Jr.’s ad received criticism for objectifying women, yet again, for its Big Game ad starring model Charlotte McKinney in 2015. Former ADWEEK editor Lisa Granatstein told Good Morning America: “I think everyone knows what Carl’s Jr. is all about. Those ads aren’t going after women, they’re going after men. They are not afraid of the controversy. The more controversy the better.”

What happened next? Upset viewers started a #WomenAreMoreThanMeat hashtag on X. A spokesperson at the time told ABC News that the ads “[didn’t] cross the line.”

6. 84 Lumber: “The Entire Journey” (2017)

Agency: Brunner

Why it got people talking? Just weeks after President Donald Trump’s first-term inauguration in 2017, 84 Lumber released “The Entire Journey,” challenging his views on immigration. The spot received mixed reviews with some praising its storytelling and message, while others believed it condoned illegal immigration. 

What happened next? Fox rejected the original ad “because they determined that some of the imagery, including ‘the wall’ would be too controversial,” Michael Brunner, CEO of Brunner, said in a statement. “So we went back and revised the spot to make it acceptable to them.” The agency and brand landed on a 90-second version of the ad which prompted viewers to visit the brand’s site to see its ending. 

7. He Gets Us: “Foot Washing” and “Who is My Neighbor?” (2023)

Agency: Lerma

Why it got people talking? He Gets Us’ Super Bowl 58 ads were intended to “imagine a world where people who might not see eye to eye serve one another, love one another and wash each other’s feet,” Jon Lee, brand leadership principal at Lerma, told ADWEEK last year. However, many publications claimed that the ads were hypocritical, pointing out the religious foundation’s previous ties to the Alliance Defending Freedom, a conservative Christian advocacy group that works to outlaw abortion and opposes LGBTQ+ rights.

What happened next? He Gets Us released two spots during the Big Game and plans to advertise in this year’s game.

8. American Values 2024: “Robert F. Kennedy Jr.” (2024)

Agency: n/a

Why it got people talking? Robert F. Kennedy Jr.’s family criticized him for a Super Bowl ad created by the political action committee American Values 2024. “My cousin’s Super Bowl ad used our uncle’s faces – and my Mother’s. She would be appalled by his deadly health care views,” Bobby Shriver, late President John F. Kennedy’s nephew wrote in a post on X.

What happened next? Despite being grateful the ad was created, RFK Jr. apologized to his family, saying: “I’m so sorry if the Super Bowl advertisement caused anyone in my family pain.”

If the Super Bowl Goes to Overtime, Prepare for a Wild Ad Bidding War

9. NYX, “Lips Only” (2024)

Agency: McCann New York

Why it got people talking? The NFL nixed the Cardi B-fronted ad, forcing the brand and agency to make last minute changes to the commercial. “As a female-led brand with female creators behind the work, we’re proud of our creative idea, which flips the script on male stereotypes with lighthearted humor,” said Yasmin Dastmalchi, general manager USA at NYX Professional Makeup.

What happened next? NYX shortened the ad to 15 seconds and added a QR code that sent viewers to the full, unedited ad.
