What CEOs can learn from Gerald Ratner’s meltdown

  • May 23, 2025

In 1991, Gerald Ratner was on top of the world. His jewellery chain dominated the UK high street, boasting record profits and an international presence. But one speech, just 25 minutes long, would turn his career, reputation, and company upside down. What followed wasn’t just a media frenzy. It was the perfect case study in how not to lead, how not to communicate, and how not to survive public scrutiny.

 

More than three decades later, “doing a Ratner” remains a cautionary phrase in boardrooms and business schools alike. But the real lessons go beyond one ill-advised joke. Ratner’s downfall reveals fundamental truths about leadership, governance, and the power, and peril, of public messaging.

 

Ratner famously joked that a sherry decanter set his company sold for £4.95 was “total crap.” He also quipped that his £1 earrings wouldn’t last as long as a Marks & Spencer prawn sandwich. The audience laughed. The tabloids did not. Nor did his customers.

 

His remarks weren’t just tone-deaf, they were self-destructive. In a single moment, he publicly devalued the very people who fueled his success. In today’s brand-conscious world, trust is everything. Undermining it, even as a joke, can be catastrophic.

 

The Royal Albert Hall may have roared with laughter, but the reaction from the market and media was swift and brutal. Public humour from a CEO is risky. The larger the stage, the more unforgiving the fallout.

 

Ratner mistook amusement for endorsement. But leadership isn’t stand-up comedy. Every word spoken in public, especially to influential audiences, must be weighed against its long-term impact.

 

Abercrombie & Fitch’s Mike Jeffries learned this the hard way. In 2006, he dismissed those outside his brand’s image as undesirables. His words were ignored, until 2013, when the internet rediscovered them, and social media called for boycotts. He stepped down the next year.

 

Cultural context evolves. Comments that once passed unnoticed can become liabilities. CEOs need not only PR filters; they need cultural intelligence.

 

Perhaps the most overlooked failure in Ratner’s case was internal: no one stopped him. He shared his speech with a board colleague who offered only this: “It needs a joke.”

 

This wasn’t a bad punchline; it was a governance failure. Ratner didn’t have strong, independent voices around him to challenge or refine his decisions. A lack of critical feedback can be as dangerous as a flawed strategy.

 

To this day, companies fail; think Carillion or the 2008 banking crisis, because boards are too cozy and too quiet. Strong leadership encourages challenge, not just consensus.

 

Here’s the irony: Ratner’s core message was spot on. He advocated for customer-focused innovation, supply chain collaboration, and affordable pricing during economic downturns; ideas that remain relevant in any cost-of-living crisis.

 

But that message was lost beneath arrogance and ill-conceived jokes. Strategy only works when it’s communicated with clarity, humility, and empathy.

 

Ratner’s greatest mistake may have been believing his own hype. Success insulated him from challenge. He mistook market dominance for invincibility.

 

True leadership isn’t about being the smartest person in the room, rather it’s about listening, adapting, and remembering that trust is earned daily. Humility isn’t weakness; it’s a survival trait.

 

Ratner’s story continues to resonate not because it’s dramatic, but because it’s avoidable. CEOs today operate in a far more transparent, fast-moving world. Every word, every decision and every moment can be amplified across social platforms and headlines.

 

The real lesson is be bold, but be mindful. Share your vision, but speak with care. Surround yourself with people who can say “no,” and never lose sight of the people who buy what you sell.

 

Sometimes, the smartest thing a leader can do is pause and ask, “How will this land with the people who matter most?”
