<p>A <b>video news release</b> (<b>VNR</b>) is a <a href="/wiki/Video" title="Video">video</a> segment made to look like a news report, but is instead created by a <a href="/wiki/Public_relations" title="Public relations">PR</a> firm, advertising agency, marketing firm, <a href="/wiki/Corporation" title="Corporation">corporation</a>, <a href="/wiki/Government_agency" title="Government agency">government agency</a>, or <a href="/wiki/Non-profit_organization" class="mw-redirect" title="Non-profit organization">non-profit organization</a>. They are provided to television newsrooms to shape <a href="/wiki/Public_opinion" title="Public opinion">public opinion</a>, promote commercial products and services, <a href="/wiki/Publicity" title="Publicity">publicize</a> individuals, or support other interests. <a href="/wiki/News_producer" title="News producer">News producers</a> may air VNRs, in whole or in part, at their discretion or incorporate them into news reports if they contain information appropriate to a story or of interest to viewers.
</p><p>Critics of VNRs have called the practice deceptive or a <a href="/wiki/Propaganda" title="Propaganda">propaganda</a> technique, particularly when the segment is not identified to the viewers as a VNR. Firms producing VNRs disagree and equate their use to a <a href="/wiki/Press_release" title="Press release">press release</a> in video form and point to the fact that editorial judgement in the worthiness, part or whole, of a VNR's content is still left in the hands of journalists, program producers or the like. The United States <a href="/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission" title="Federal Communications Commission">Federal Communications Commission</a> is currently<sup class="noprint Inline-Template" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Manual_of_Style/Dates_and_numbers#Chronological_items" title="Wikipedia:Manual of Style/Dates and numbers"><span title="The time period mentioned near this tag is ambiguous. (February 2022)">when?</span></a></i>&#93;</sup> investigating the practice of VNRs.<sup class="noprint Inline-Template Template-Fact" style="white-space:nowrap;">&#91;<i><a href="/wiki/Wikipedia:Citation_needed" title="Wikipedia:Citation needed"><span title="This claim needs references to reliable sources. (February 2022)">citation needed</span></a></i>&#93;</sup>
</p>
<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" />
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Details">Details</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Video_news_release&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Details"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Most VNRs feature a professional news reporter, someone with on-air news experience, or an <a href="/wiki/Actor" title="Actor">actor</a>.  VNRs also often include interviews with experts (who often have legitimate, if biased, expertise); so called "man on the street" interviews with "average" people; and pictures of celebrities, products, service demonstrations, corporate <a href="/wiki/Logos" title="Logos">logos</a> and the like, where applicable.  In some cases the "man on the street" segments feature persons randomly selected and interviewed spontaneously, and in other cases actors are hired and directed by VNR producers to deliver carefully scripted comments. In addition, regardless of whether real people or professional actors appear, VNR <a href="/wiki/Film_producer" title="Film producer">producers</a> and <a href="/wiki/Film_director" title="Film director">directors</a>, just like <a href="/wiki/Journalist" title="Journalist">journalists</a>, have complete discretion to excerpt and edit these "interviews" into '<a href="/wiki/Sound_bites" class="mw-redirect" title="Sound bites">sound bites</a>' that help make the point they are trying to make.
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Media_broadcasting">Media broadcasting</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Video_news_release&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Media broadcasting"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>Commercial television stations and other media outlets often broadcast only portions of a VNR. Sometimes they use the script provided by the VNR producer but frequently they write their own script.
</p><p>In a report released on April 6, 2006, the <a href="/wiki/Center_for_Media_and_Democracy" title="Center for Media and Democracy">Center for Media and Democracy</a> listed detailed information on 77 television stations that it said had broadcast VNRs in the prior 10 months, and which VNRs had been broadcast. Most of these VNR uses were of partial feeds.  However, CMD said that in each case the television station actively disguised the VNR content to make it appear to be its own reporting, and that more than one-third of the time, stations aired the pre-packaged VNR in its entirety.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-1"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>1<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Business_production_in_the_United_States">Business production in the United States</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Video_news_release&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Business production in the United States"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p>VNRs have been used extensively in business since at least the early 1980s.  Corporations such as <a href="/wiki/Microsoft" title="Microsoft">Microsoft</a> and <a href="/wiki/Altria_Group" class="mw-redirect" title="Altria Group">Philip Morris</a>, and the pharmaceutical industry generally, have all made use of the technique.
</p><p>According to the trade-group <a href="/wiki/Public_Relations_Society_of_America" title="Public Relations Society of America">Public Relations Society of America</a>, a VNR is the video equivalent of a press release.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-2" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-2"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>2<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>  and presents a client's case in an attractive, informative format. The VNR placement agency seeks to garner media attention for the client's products, services, brands or other marketing goals. The VNR affords local TV stations free broadcast quality materials for use in reports offered by such stations.
</p><p>Public Relations agencies have their video tapes encoded allowing very accurate tracking of where such video is used. (see: <a href="/wiki/SIGMA_(verification_service)" title="SIGMA (verification service)">SIGMA (verification service)</a> for additional information).
</p><p>One critic of the VNR technique, John Stauber, an observer and critic of the Public Relations business says, "These fellows are whistling past the graveyard, assuring themselves that this all is no big deal. There was no hint of shame, certainly no apologizing, just apparent disdain for having their business practices dissected on the front page of the New York Times. They are proud of their work."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-3" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-3"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>3<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup>
</p>
<ul><li>The <i><a href="/wiki/New_York_Times" class="mw-redirect" title="New York Times">New York Times</a></i> reported in March 2005 that "In all, at least 20 federal agencies, including the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense" title="United States Department of Defense">Defense Department</a> and the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau" title="United States Census Bureau">Census Bureau</a>, have made and distributed hundreds of television news segments in the past four years, records and interviews show. Many were subsequently broadcast on local stations across the country without any acknowledgement of the government's role in their production."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-4" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-4"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>4<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>A VNR financed by the <a href="/wiki/Department_of_Health_and_Human_Services" class="mw-redirect" title="Department of Health and Human Services">Department of Health and Human Services</a> was aired on a number of local news programs around the country, as conventional journalism when in fact, it was produced to promote the new <a href="/wiki/Medicare_(United_States)" title="Medicare (United States)">Medicare</a> plan.  The creation of the <a href="/w/index.php?title=Karen_Ryan&amp;action=edit&amp;redlink=1" class="new" title="Karen Ryan (page does not exist)">Karen Ryan</a> video, named so because of the on-screen "reporter," was ruled in May 2004 to be in violation of federal law by the <a href="/wiki/General_Accounting_Office" class="mw-redirect" title="General Accounting Office">General Accounting Office</a> (GAO), the investigative arm of the U.S. government.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-5" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-5"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>5<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In September, 2005, the GAO concluded that the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Education" title="United States Department of Education">Department of Education</a> had violated the law when it distributed a similar video news release using Karen Ryan as a "reporter" touting the <a href="/wiki/No_Child_Left_Behind" class="mw-redirect" title="No Child Left Behind">No Child Left Behind</a> program of the Bush administration.  In May 2003 the Department had hired the <a href="/wiki/Ketchum_Inc." title="Ketchum Inc.">Ketchum</a> public relations firm; the contract specified, among other things, that the firm create "audio products, videos and some print materials that present clear, coherent, targeted messages regarding ED’s programs and that relate to the Department’s legislative initiatives".<sup id="cite&#95;ref-6" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-6"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>6<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>A website of the <a href="/wiki/United_States_Census_Bureau" title="United States Census Bureau">Census Bureau</a> informs visitors: "U.S. Census Bureau Video News Feeds are available for creation of state-specific news reports. Targeted comments are provided by Census Bureau Redistricting Data Office Chief, Marshall Turner. Companion notification material includes references to websites for the newly released information. Please contact...Homefront Communications for hardcopies on Betacam SP and faxed/email notification copy."<sup id="cite&#95;ref-7" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-7"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>7<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In May 2020, multiple television stations aired a PR piece produced by <a href="/wiki/Amazon.com" class="mw-redirect" title="Amazon.com">Amazon.com</a> that defended its response to the <a href="/wiki/COVID-19_pandemic" title="COVID-19 pandemic">COVID-19 pandemic</a>, with anchors reading off an identical script vertabatim.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-8" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-8"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>8<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li></ul>
<div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="VNRs_and_U.S._law">VNRs and U.S. law</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Video_news_release&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: VNRs and U.S. law"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<ul><li>In January 1948, the U.S. Information and Educational Exchange Act of 1948, also known as the <a href="/wiki/Smith-Mundt_Act" class="mw-redirect" title="Smith-Mundt Act">Smith-Mundt Act</a>, was passed by Congress and signed into law by U.S. President <a href="/wiki/Harry_Truman" class="mw-redirect" title="Harry Truman">Harry Truman</a>, placing international overseas information activities, including VOA, under an Office of International Information at the <a href="/wiki/Department_of_State" class="mw-redirect" title="Department of State">Department of State</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-9" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-9"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>9<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In February 2005, the "Stop Government Propaganda Act" was introduced in the U.S. Senate.  It was referred to the Judiciary Committee and no further action occurred.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-10" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-10"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>10<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In April 2005, the <a href="/wiki/Federal_Communications_Commission" title="Federal Communications Commission">Federal Communications Commission</a> warned television stations that they could be fined for airing news stories provided by the government and by companies without disclosing who made them.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Roland&#95;11-0" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Roland-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In May 2006, FCC chairman <a href="/wiki/Kevin_Martin_(FCC)" class="mw-redirect" title="Kevin Martin (FCC)">Kevin Martin</a> ordered a review of airing of VNRs by television stations, following the April 2006 report by the <a href="/wiki/Center_for_Media_and_Democracy" title="Center for Media and Democracy">Center for Media and Democracy</a>.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-Roland&#95;11-1" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-Roland-11"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>11<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li>
<li>In August 2006, the FCC mailed letters to the owners of 77 television stations, asking for information regarding agreements between the stations and the creators of VNRs.  The letters also asked whether there was any "<a href="/wiki/Consideration" title="Consideration">consideration</a>" given to the stations in return for airing the material. Stations were given 60 days to respond.<sup id="cite&#95;ref-12" class="reference"><a href="#cite_note-12"><span class="cite-bracket">&#91;</span>12<span class="cite-bracket">&#93;</span></a></sup></li></ul>
