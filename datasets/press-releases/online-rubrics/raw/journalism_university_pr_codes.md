    </script><h2 id="why-do-professional-codes-of-ethics-matter-in-pr">Why do professional codes of ethics matter in PR? <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#why-do-professional-codes-of-ethics-matter-in-pr">🔗</button></h2>
<p>PR professionals hold significant influence over public opinion. They craft narratives, manage reputations, and mediate between organisations and the public. Without a shared ethical framework, this influence can easily slide into manipulation, misinformation, or deception.</p>
<p>Professional codes of ethics exist to prevent that. They provide a clear set of principles and standards that help practitioners make sound decisions &#8211; especially when facing pressure from clients, employers, or competitive environments. These codes also serve as a benchmark for the public, signalling that the profession takes its responsibilities seriously.</p>
<p>Three organisations have been especially influential in defining ethical standards for PR globally: the <strong>Public Relations Society of America (PRSA)</strong>, the <strong>International Association of Business Communicators (IABC)</strong>, and the <strong>International Public Relations Association (IPRA)</strong>. Each has developed its own code, and together they cover a wide spectrum of ethical expectations for practitioners.</p>
<h2 id="the-prsa-code-of-ethics">The PRSA Code of Ethics <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#the-prsa-code-of-ethics">🔗</button></h2>
<p>The <a href="https://www.prsa.org/professional-development/prsa-resources/ethics" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">PRSA Code of Ethics</a> is widely considered the cornerstone of ethical PR practice in the United States and beyond. First developed in 1950, it has been updated over the decades to reflect new challenges in communication. The current version, adopted in 2000, is built on a set of core values and <span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="Specific rules outlining proper and improper behavior for PR professionals, related to areas like information disclosure and conflicts of interest.">provisions of conduct</span> that guide day-to-day professional behaviour.</p>
<h3 id="core-values-of-the-prsa-code">Core values of the PRSA Code <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#core-values-of-the-prsa-code">🔗</button></h3>
<p>The PRSA Code is grounded in <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The fundamental beliefs of the PRSA which include advocacy, honesty, expertise, independence, loyalty and fairness.">six core values</span></strong> that represent the fundamental beliefs of the profession:</p>
<p><strong>Advocacy:</strong> PR professionals serve the public interest by acting as responsible advocates for those they represent. The goal is to provide a voice in the marketplace of ideas and contribute to informed public debate &#8211; not to push propaganda.</p>
<p><strong>Honesty:</strong> Practitioners must uphold the highest standards of accuracy and truth. Whether advancing a client&#8217;s interests or communicating with the public, honesty is non-negotiable.</p>
<p><strong>Expertise:</strong> This value calls for the responsible use of specialised knowledge. PR professionals are expected to continually develop their skills through research, education, and professional growth.</p>
<p><strong>Independence:</strong> Practitioners should provide objective counsel and remain accountable for their actions. This means avoiding situations where personal interests compromise professional judgment.</p>
<p><strong>Loyalty:</strong> Being faithful to clients and employers is important, but not at the expense of the public interest. Loyalty, in the PRSA framework, is always balanced against broader social responsibility.</p>
<p><strong>Fairness:</strong> PR professionals must deal fairly with all stakeholders &#8211; clients, competitors, peers, vendors, media, and the general public. Respecting diverse opinions and supporting free expression are central to this value.</p>
<h3 id="provisions-of-conduct-and-enforcement">Provisions of conduct and enforcement <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#provisions-of-conduct-and-enforcement">🔗</button></h3>
<p>Beyond values, the PRSA Code includes six specific <strong>provisions of conduct</strong>, each paired with a core principle, stated intent, and examples of improper behaviour. These provisions cover areas such as free flow of information, competition, disclosure of information, safeguarding confidences, and avoiding conflicts of interest.</p>
<p>For example, under the disclosure provision, a PR practitioner who gives expensive gifts to a journalist to influence coverage would be in violation of the code. Similarly, representing competing clients without disclosing the conflict would breach the safeguarding of confidences provision.</p>
<p>One important detail: the PRSA Code is <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The PRSA code is more about encouraging ethical behavior than having legal penalties for violations.">aspirational rather than enforceable through legal means</span></strong>. The <a href="https://archive.pagecentertraining.psu.edu/public-relations-ethics/professional-codes-of-ethics/lesson-2-tbd-waiting-on-edits/features-of-the-prsa-code-of-ethics/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Arthur W. Page Center notes</a> that while no formal enforcement system exists in the traditional sense, PRSA reserves the right to bar or expel members who violate the code. Members also sign a pledge committing to uphold ethical standards, with the understanding that misconduct can lead to membership revocation.</p>
<p>The PRSA Board of Ethics and Professional Standards (BEPS) plays a key role in keeping the code relevant. It regularly publishes <strong>Professional Standards Advisories (PSAs)</strong> that address emerging issues like social media ethics, native advertising, and the use of AI in communications.</p><h2 id="the-iabc-code-of-ethics">The IABC Code of Ethics <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#the-iabc-code-of-ethics">🔗</button></h2>
<p>The <a href="https://www.iabc.com/about/what-we-do/standards/code-of-ethics" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">International Association of Business Communicators (IABC)</a> takes a slightly different approach. While PRSA focuses specifically on public relations, IABC addresses the broader community of business communicators &#8211; including internal communications, marketing communications, and public affairs professionals.</p>
<h3 id="three-foundational-principles">Three foundational principles <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#three-foundational-principles">🔗</button></h3>
<p>The IABC Code is built on <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The foundational beliefs of the IABC that ethical societies are governed by human rights and the rule of law, that members of an organisation can agree on right and wrong, and that sensitivity to cultural norms is essential.">three interrelated principles</span></strong> that apply to professional communication worldwide. These principles assume that ethical societies are governed by respect for human rights and the rule of law, that members of an organisation can agree on criteria for right and wrong, and that sensitivity to cultural norms is essential when dealing with matters of taste.</p>
<p>From these principles, IABC derives three core standards: professional communication must be <strong>legal</strong>, it must be <strong>ethical</strong>, and it must be <strong>in good taste</strong>.</p>
<h3 id="the-11-articles-of-the-iabc-code">The 11 articles of the IABC Code <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#the-11-articles-of-the-iabc-code">🔗</button></h3>
<p>The IABC Code outlines 11 specific commitments that members must embrace. These include pledges to act honestly, communicate accurate information, promptly correct errors, protect confidential information, support free speech, respect cultural values, give proper credit to others, and avoid using confidential information for personal gain.</p>
<p>Notably, the code also requires that members <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="PR and communication professionals are advised to not represent conflicting parties without the full knowledge and agreement of all involved.">not represent conflicting or competing interests</span></strong> without full written disclosure and consent. And unlike some codes that remain vague on deliverables, IABC explicitly states that communicators must not guarantee results beyond their power to deliver &#8211; a practical provision that guards against overpromising to clients.</p>
<p>All IABC members must sign a statement affirming they have reviewed and understand the code. The association also maintains an <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="A team within IABC that offers advice on ethical dilemmas and supports professional development related to ethics.">Ethics Committee</span></strong> composed of accredited professionals who provide advice on specific ethical situations and support ethics-related professional development.</p>
<h2 id="the-ipra-code-of-conduct">The IPRA Code of Conduct <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#the-ipra-code-of-conduct">🔗</button></h2>
<p>The <a href="https://www.ipra.org/member-services/code-of-conduct/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">International Public Relations Association (IPRA)</a> offers the most globally oriented ethical framework for PR. Established in 1955, IPRA has been developing ethical codes since the early 1960s. Its current Code of Conduct, launched in 2011, consolidates three historic documents: the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="One of the historical documents consolidated into the current IPRA Code of Conduct, setting ethical standards for the profession.">1961 Code of Venice</span></strong>, the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="A historical document consolidated into the current IPRA Code of Conduct, emphasizing human dignity as the basis for ethical PR.">1965 Code of Athens</span></strong>, and the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="One of the historical documents consolidated into the current IPRA Code of Conduct, further refining ethical guidelines.">2007 Code of Brussels</span></strong>.</p>
<h3 id="a-code-rooted-in-human-rights">A code rooted in human rights <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#a-code-rooted-in-human-rights">🔗</button></h3>
<p>What sets the IPRA Code apart is its explicit grounding in international human rights instruments. The code opens by recalling the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The foundational treaty of the United Nations, outlining the organization&#039;s purposes and principles.">UN Charter</span></strong> and the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="A landmark document adopted by the UN, outlining fundamental human rights and freedoms.">1948 Universal Declaration of Human Rights</span></strong>, particularly Article 19, which protects freedom of opinion and expression. This gives the IPRA Code a distinctly global and rights-based character.</p>
<p>The Code of Athens, authored by French PR pioneer <a href="https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0363811113001951" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">Lucien Matrat in 1965</a>, is particularly notable for its moral core. It recognised that PR practitioners possess significant power to influence public opinion and insisted that this power must be guided by strict ethical standards tied to fundamental human dignity.</p>
<h3 id="18-principles-of-the-ipra-code">18 principles of the IPRA Code <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#18-principles-of-the-ipra-code">🔗</button></h3>
<p>The consolidated IPRA Code outlines <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="A comprehensive set of guidelines in the IPRA Code covering integrity, accuracy, confidentiality, fair dealing, and financial ethics.">18 specific principles</span></strong> that members must observe. These cover a wide range of professional conduct:</p>
<p><strong>Integrity and transparency</strong> require practitioners to act honestly, openly declare their identity and the interests they represent, and avoid conflicts of interest.</p>
<p><strong>Accuracy and truthfulness</strong> demand that practitioners take all reasonable steps to ensure information is correct and promptly correct any errors or misleading content.</p>
<p><strong>Confidentiality</strong> obliges practitioners to honour confidential information shared with them by clients or employers.</p>
<p><strong>Fair dealing</strong> prohibits deceptive practices such as creating front organisations, poaching competitors&#8217; clients through dishonest means, or intentionally damaging another practitioner&#8217;s reputation.</p>
<p><strong>Financial ethics</strong> prevent practitioners from accepting undisclosed payments, offering inducements to public officials or media, or selling for profit documents obtained from public authorities.</p>
<p>The IPRA Code also includes provisions relevant to the <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The current era defined by the prevalence of digital technology and the internet.">digital age</span></strong>, acknowledging that channels like the internet and social media can spread erroneous or misleading information rapidly. This makes it especially relevant for modern PR practice where misinformation can go viral in minutes.</p>
<p>The code was reviewed in 2020 and no changes were deemed necessary, indicating that its principles remain well-suited to current professional challenges.</p>
<h2 id="comparing-the-three-codes">Comparing the three codes <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#comparing-the-three-codes">🔗</button></h2>
<p>While all three codes share common themes &#8211; honesty, accuracy, confidentiality, fairness, and accountability &#8211; they differ in scope and emphasis.</p>
<p>The <strong>PRSA Code</strong> is the most detailed in terms of practical application. Its provisions of conduct with real-world examples of improper behaviour make it a highly actionable guide for day-to-day practice. It is also the most U.S.-centric, though its influence extends globally.</p>
<p>The <strong>IABC Code</strong> is broader in its audience, speaking to all business communicators rather than just PR professionals. Its straightforward, first-person format (&#8220;I am honest,&#8221; &#8220;I communicate accurate information&#8221;) makes it accessible and easy to internalise. Its emphasis on legal compliance and cultural sensitivity reflects the global diversity of its membership.</p>
<p>The <strong>IPRA Code</strong> is the most globally aspirational. Its grounding in the UN Charter and Universal Declaration of Human Rights gives it a philosophical depth that the other codes don&#8217;t explicitly match. With 18 principles, it also covers the broadest range of ethical scenarios.</p>
<p>One key similarity across all three: none of them rely on heavy-handed enforcement. All are primarily <strong>aspirational and voluntary</strong>, relying on peer accountability, education, and the professional reputation of members to ensure compliance. Each organisation reserves the right to revoke membership in cases of serious ethical violation, but formal disciplinary actions remain relatively rare.</p>
<h2 id="how-these-codes-help-navigate-ethical-dilemmas">How these codes help navigate ethical dilemmas <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#how-these-codes-help-navigate-ethical-dilemmas">🔗</button></h2>
<p>Codes of ethics are not just documents sitting on a shelf. They are practical tools for navigating the grey areas that PR professionals face regularly.</p>
<p>Consider a scenario where a client asks a PR professional to omit negative product safety data from a press release. The PRSA Code&#8217;s emphasis on honest communication and public interest makes it clear that omitting critical safety information would be unethical. The IABC Code&#8217;s requirement for accurate communication and legal compliance reinforces this. The IPRA Code&#8217;s principle on accuracy and its prohibition against disseminating false or misleading information further backs the decision to push back on the client.</p>
<p>Or take the increasingly common challenge of <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="Content created by artificial intelligence, which raises ethical questions about transparency and accuracy.">AI-generated content</span></strong> in PR. As AI tools become more prevalent in communication, the question of transparency &#8211; who created the content, whether it&#8217;s human or machine-generated, and whether it&#8217;s accurate &#8211; becomes critical. PRSA has been proactive in addressing such emerging issues through its Professional Standards Advisories, and IPRA has established a dedicated <a href="https://www.ipra.org/member-services/ai-chapter/" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank">AI Chapter</a> to provide guidance on responsible use of AI in public relations.</p>
<p>Another common dilemma involves <strong>representing competing clients</strong>. Both the PRSA and IABC codes address this directly, requiring full disclosure and consent. Without these guidelines, practitioners might easily find themselves in situations where their loyalty to one client compromises their obligations to another.</p>
<h2 id="the-broader-impact-on-trust-and-credibility">The broader impact on trust and credibility <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#the-broader-impact-on-trust-and-credibility">🔗</button></h2>
<p>Ethical codes do more than guide individual behaviour &#8211; they shape the reputation of the entire profession. When practitioners consistently uphold ethical standards, public trust in PR as a field grows. When they don&#8217;t, the fallout can be severe &#8211; not just for the individual but for the industry&#8217;s credibility overall.</p>
<p>This is why organisations like PRSA invest heavily in <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="Training and resources designed to help PR professionals understand and apply ethical principles in their work.">ethics education</span></strong>. PRSA designates September as &#8220;Ethics Month,&#8221; offering a range of educational programs, and requires accredited members (APR) to complete continuing education units focused on ethics. IABC similarly encourages ongoing engagement through its Ethics Committee and educational resources. IPRA contributes through its Thought Leadership essays and regular webinars on ethical challenges in global PR.</p>
<p>The codes also provide a <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="A benefit or edge gained over rivals by adhering to high ethical standards, which enhances reputation and attracts clients.">competitive advantage</span></strong>. Clients and employers increasingly look for practitioners who can demonstrate adherence to recognised ethical standards. In a media environment rife with misinformation, a commitment to ethical practice is a differentiator that builds long-term value.</p>
<h2 id="limitations-of-professional-codes-of-ethics">Limitations of professional codes of ethics <button class="copy-link" data-link="https://journalism.university/media-ethics-and-laws/impact-professional-ethics-pr/#limitations-of-professional-codes-of-ethics">🔗</button></h2>
<p>No ethical code is perfect. The biggest criticism of PR codes of ethics is that they are largely <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The codes of ethics are based on internal oversight and commitment rather than legal enforcement.">self-regulatory and voluntary</span></strong>. There&#8217;s no legal consequence for violating the PRSA, IABC, or IPRA codes &#8211; only the risk of losing membership or professional standing. This means enforcement depends on the willingness of practitioners and organisations to hold themselves and their peers accountable.</p>
<p>Another limitation is that these codes apply only to <strong><span class="text-hover" data-tooltip="The codes of ethics only formally apply to individuals who are part of PRSA, IABC, or IPRA.">members of the respective organisations</span></strong>. A large number of PR practitioners worldwide are not members of any professional body and therefore are not bound by any formal code. This gap means that unethical behaviour in the industry can persist without any institutional mechanism for correction.</p>
<p>Additionally, the rapid evolution of digital communication, data privacy concerns, and AI-driven content creation means that codes must be <strong>continually updated</strong> to remain relevant. While PRSA and IPRA have made strides in this area, the pace of technological change often outstrips the speed of guideline development.</p>
<p>Despite these limitations, professional codes of ethics remain the most structured and widely recognised framework for ethical PR practice. They provide a common language, a set of shared expectations, and a foundation for professional development that benefits practitioners, clients, and the public alike.</p>
<p><strong>What do you think?</strong> Can voluntary, self-regulatory codes truly keep pace with the ethical challenges of modern PR, especially in the age of AI and misinformation? And should membership in a professional PR body &#8211; with its ethical obligations &#8211; be a requirement for practising public relations?</p>
