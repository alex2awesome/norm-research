            <div class="main-content lit-style" itemscope="itemscope" itemtype="http://schema.org/CreativeWork"><div class="meta-content fm-sec"><h1 id="_NBK540733_"><span class="label"> 7</span><span class="title" itemprop="name">Recommendations</span></h1></div><div class="jig-ncbiinpagenav body-content whole_rhythm" data-jigconfig="allHeadingLevels: ['h2'],smoothScroll: false" itemprop="text"><div id="ch7.s1"><h2 id="_ch7_s1_">A. Building trust and engaging with affected populations</h2><p>No matter how well planned or applied, risk communication interventions will fail to enable people at risk to make informed decisions to act to protect their lives, their health, their families and communities against threats if they do not trust the information source. Therefore, establishing and maintaining trust is arguably the first and most important step in effective <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a>. The evidence search confirmed that building trust is inextricably linked to community engagement and open acknowledgement of uncertainty. Hence, the recommendations that were based on the evidence retrieved for the three questions on building trust, communicating uncertainty and community engagement are grouped in this section.</p><div id="ch7.s1.1"><h3>A.1. Trust</h3><div id="ch7.s1.1.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4><p><b>To build trust, risk communication interventions should link to functioning and accessible services, be transparent, timely, easy-to-understand, acknowledge uncertainty, address and engage affected populations, link to self-efficacy, and be disseminated using multiple platforms, methods and channels.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> noted that systematic review and grey literature findings emphasized the benefits of clarity, timeliness, effective dissemination and transparency in communicating what is known during emergencies, as well as interventions, and past positive experience when acting on communication from authorities for building trust. Harms occur when actions promoted by authorities cannot be performed because services are not available, unresponsive or difficult to access.</p><p><b>Remarks:</b> &#x02018;Authorities&#x02019; refers to people in charge at local, national, regional and international levels.</p></div><div id="ch7.s1.1.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s1.1.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r12" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r12/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">12</a>)</h5><p>The systematic review found that most studies analysed events in high- and middle-income countries in Asia, Europe, North America and Oceania. The event most often covered was infectious disease; other relatively common events included floods, earthquake, volcanic eruption, bioterrorism, food-borne illnesses, and radiological/radiation emergencies. All four event phases were covered, although there was strong emphasis on the preparation phase, followed by the onset and containment phases, with much less coverage of the recovery phase.</p><p><b>The review found that several factors including organizational messages and actions could predict a higher level of trust, especially:</b>
<ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>acknowledging uncertainty in messages, including forecasts and warnings;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>being transparent and not concealing negative information, such as rates of casualties;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>speedily disseminating information and intervening;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>creating scientific communication in an easy-to-understand manner;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>seeking input from the public and encouraging a dialogue;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>ensuring coordination between different health authorities and the media along with a uniform message;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>avoiding rapid changes in information and preventing conflicting information dissemination from different agencies; and</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>disseminating information through multiple platforms.</div></li></ul></p><p>The reviewers emphasized that it is important to understand that these actions occur in a larger context encompassing different components of trust, history with authorities and life circumstances of the public/person. All of these can strengthen or weaken the message-trust relationship.</p><p>Findings were also extracted from seven existing literature reviews, assessed as being of high and moderate quality. These reviews overlapped with those from the current systematic review showing that trust is influenced by: organizational reputation; quality of stakeholder relationships; understanding and managing media relations; risk information provision strategies; accuracy, timeliness and comprehensive information; transparency about available information; fairness in the treatment of populations; building trust and trustworthiness through participatory dialogue and involvement in pre-event planning, exercises, and in the design and testing of communication plans. Higher trust in the ability of public officials and governments to respond to a public health emergency links to a greater likelihood of the recommended actions being adopted.</p></div><div id="ch7.s1.1.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>Grey literature reports indicated that community engagement and building trust work together. Although local involvement is essential for successful community engagement, not all &#x02018;locals&#x02019; have an equal trust-building effect. During the West African Ebola virus disease outbreak, people did not trust some of the leaders or the individuals being paid to do the work involved with the outbreak (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r13" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r13/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">13</a>). Volunteers were used to help address this latter issue.</p><p>Reports found that trust in health systems is based on people&#x02019;s perceptions of these systems rather than objective measures. If people think a system is unlikely to help them, they will not use it. Engaging community members by involving them in service delivery meetings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r14" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r14/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">14</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r15" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r15/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">15</a>) can overcome this. Disrespectful treatment by health-care workers eroded trust.</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s1.2"><h3>A.2. Communicating uncertainty</h3><div id="ch7.s1.2.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4><p><b>Communication by authorities to the public should include explicit information about uncertainties associated with risks, events and interventions, and indicate what is known and not known at a given time.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> considered evidence (rated moderate to high quality by the reviewers) of harms caused by failure to clearly explain what is known and what is not known during emergencies. Some of the issues raised in the evidence review overlapped with the recommendation on building trust, so the recommendation was confined to the best way to effectively communicate when not all of the information is clear or certain.</p></div><div id="ch7.s1.2.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s1.2.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings(<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r16" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r16/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">16</a>)</h5><p>There is general agreement among experts and researchers that communication by authorities to the public should include explicit information about uncertainties associated with events. However, it is important that the information provided is consistent and not contradictory, and is presented clearly and in an easy-to-understand manner.</p><p>Uncertainty of data and knowledge influences interactions within and among groups of experts, and between experts and policy/decision-makers and thus decision-making, in complex ways. Absent or contradictory and inconsistent information from the authorities leads to uncertainty in medical/health-care workers and policy-makers, and in decision-making regarding communication to the public. Authorities should carefully consider these and other aspects of uncertainty when developing the best ways to communicate uncertainties to the general public, at-risk/vulnerable populations, and stakeholders.</p><p>When authorities provide information about uncertainties, it is generally associated with desirable outcomes but the possibility of some undesirable outcomes needs to be kept in mind. The public&#x02019;s understanding of information describing the likelihood of an event is prone to error. This is true of experts as well.</p><p>Mass media coverage of an event that emphasizes rapidly changing, contradictory, and conflicting information, especially that which differs from official information from authorities, increases uncertainty in the public, which can in turn lead to several undesirable outcomes, such as lack of trust in authorities and in the actions that they recommend.</p></div><div id="ch7.s1.2.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>Grey literature studies found that the manner in which a message is communicated is as important as the message content itself. Panic sparked by Ebola virus disease transmission in the United States of America (<a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI15/">USA</a>) was augmented by initial failure to communicate all the facts, for example, no mention was made of breaches in infection prevention and control protocols. Scientists also failed to communicate clearly the true risk levels for different sectors, leading the general public to overestimate their risk (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r17" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r17/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">17</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s1.3"><h3>A.3. Community engagement</h3><div id="ch7.s1.3.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4></div><div id="ch7.s1.3.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s1.3.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings(<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r18" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r18/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">18</a>)</h5><p><b>Identify people whom the community trusts and build relationships with them and involve them in decision-making to ensure that interventions are collaborative and contextually appropriate, and that the community owns the process of communication.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> found there was limited evidence considered evidence comparing the effectiveness of different community engagement strategies. However, there was evidence, assessed as moderate quality, indicating that inclusion of community members in planning activities was beneficial in improving preparedness for and response to an event. A large body of descriptive grey literature about experiences with different approaches indicated the benefit of identifying trusted people within communities and iinvolving them in decision-making. The harms identified were failure to understand the context and culture when developing interventions.</p><p><b>Implementation consideration</b>: Providing feedback is an essential part of good community engagement.</p><p><b>Remarks</b>: &#x02018;Community&#x02019; encompasses a wide range of groupings.</p><p>Investigators rarely directly studied which strategies or tactics were most effective for engaging community participation. Studies of community participation in preparedness activities found that including community members as planners, as well as attendees in pre-event activities led to increases in preparedness and response activities. Specific activities investigated were:
<ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>planning design and development</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>information dissemination</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>training on roles, responsibilities and tasks</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>conducting preparedness actions</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>relationship building/bonding.</div></li></ul></p><p>Training on roles and responsibilities is beneficial if it includes community-level considerations, local context and past experience, which not only aid in cooperation and collaboration but also may address the issue of risk paradox tied to perception and experience.</p><p>Relationship building and bonding not only serves to create trust and confidence in leaders, but also to create social connectedness and networks.</p><p>There is a clear indication that activities prior to an event will more successfully engage communities than those that are only attempted during an evolving event.</p></div><div id="ch7.s1.3.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>The majority of grey literature studies and reports found that engaging communities should play a central role in emergency responses. Local action was crucial to controlling the 2014&#x02013;2015 West African Ebola virus disease outbreak because the turning point was reached before the full response was operational (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r19" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r19/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">19</a>). This review found reports of the different methods used for improving community engagement, but no formal assessment of their effectiveness. The most common are listed in <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figch7tab1" rid-ob="figobch7tab1">Table 3</a>.</p><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col table-wrap" id="figch7tab1"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab1/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Table 3" class="img_link icnblk_img figpopup" rid-figpopup="figch7tab1" rid-ob="figobch7tab1"><img class="small-thumb" src="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab1/?report=thumb" src-large="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab1/?report=previmg" alt="Table 3. Methods used to improve community engagement." /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt"><h4 id="ch7.tab1"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab1/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobch7tab1">Table 3</a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Methods used to improve community engagement. </p></div></div></div></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s2"><h2 id="_ch7_s2_">B. Integrating Emergency risk communication into health and emergency response systems</h2><p><a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI7/">Risk communication</a> for health protection needs to work within health systems. For this reason, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> took a health systems focus when selecting some of the priority questions. The health systems &#x02018;pillars&#x02019; of governance, information systems, capacity building, and finance underpinned the questions that provided evidence for the recommendations on integrating <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> into health systems.</p><div id="ch7.s2.1"><h3>B.1. Governance and leadership</h3><div id="ch7.s2.1.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4><p><b><a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> should have a designated strategic role in global and national emergency preparedness, and response leadership teams with well-defined roles and responsibilities for communication personnel.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> found there was evidence of benefit from designating <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> as a strategic role because this facilitated incorporation of risk communication as a functioning &#x02018;pillar&#x02019; of preparedness for and response to emergencies. This recommendation has resource considerations, as designating that role entails training and paying for risk communication professionals. However, the GDG concluded that the benefit strongly outweighed the resource implications.</p><p><b>Remarks:</b> This applies to preparedness and response leadership teams at local, national, regional and international levels.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.1.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s2.1.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r20" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r20/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">20</a>)</h5><p>Six articles presenting examples of mechanisms that might lead to the integration of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> functions into the leadership structure were identified. Such mechanisms can be summarized under three themes/outcomes:
<ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>placing <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> functions into the national leadership structure;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>creating organizational proximity of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> practitioners to national response leadership;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>development of laws, regulations, policies and frameworks in support of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a>.</div></li></ul></p></div><div id="ch7.s2.1.2.2"><h5>Evidence from Chinese-language reviews</h5><p>The systematic reviewers examined the Chinese language literature and reports describing the need for more training, the establishment of professional roles (e.g. media monitoring personnel and spokespersons), and for enabling the timely release of relevant information on disease outbreaks and prevention.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.1.2.3"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>There were very few reports in the grey literature dealing with this issue. The United Nations General Assembly stated that compliance with <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI9/">IHR (2005)</a> includes the establishment of preparedness and response mechanisms. Community engagement and culturally appropriate communication form an integral part of such mechanisms (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r21" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r21/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">21</a>). This also implies the integration of risk communication into national health plans. Additionally, in its Ebola Response Improvement Plan, the United States Department of Health and Human Services noted the importance of prioritizing risk communication. A department-wide <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> strategy was to be developed, including training staff to serve as spokespersons and planning to ensure an adequate supply of trained risk communication personnel (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r22" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r22/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">22</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s2.2"><h3>B.2. Information systems and coordination</h3><div id="ch7.s2.2.1"><h4>Recommendations</h4><p><b>B2.1. Develop and build on relevant stakeholder and organizational networks across geographical, disciplinary and, where appropriate, national boundaries.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed there was moderate quality evidence indicating that a benefit- improved coordination and information flow could be achieved by the creation of task forces and committees involving all key stakeholders, regardless of geographical location, discipline or other boundaries. This recommendation has resource implications &#x02013; time, human and financial. However, the GDG agreed that the benefit of improved coordination and information flow strongly outweighed the harms and resource considerations.</p><p><b>B2.2. Tailor information and communication systems to users&#x02019; needs and involve local stakeholders to guarantee the flow of information across sectors.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> considered the evidence of the benefits derived from the involvement of local stakeholders in information flows. A potential harm was concern about the release of information across sectors, especially in contexts where policies and laws forbid such information flows. This was reflected in the implementation consideration below.</p><p><b>Implementation consideration</b>: <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> professionals need legislative and policy support. Release of information may be forbidden and information flows may be very limited by levels of authority in some settings.</p><p><b>Remarks</b>: Examples of &#x02018;users&#x02019; include government staff, civil society, local communities, emergency response teams, relief agencies, etc.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.2.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s2.2.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r20" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r20/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">20</a>)</h5><p>Three themes emerged from the literature as mechanisms to enhance information sharing and coordination.</p><ol><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The creation of task forces and committees with key stakeholders has been described as a mechanism for improving or facilitating information sharing between national and subnational authorities, and between agencies. A number of components for the effective operation of networks, task forces, and committees were identified. These include: the importance of existing relationships between responders prior to an incident; the role of network teams, as opposed to hierarchical teams for improved emergency response performance and decision-making; and the importance of information exchange and distribution between decision-making units. They also include: the use of information managers for coordinating information between agencies to reduce information demand and improve the supply of key information; and the importance of a public information officer to improve information dissemination.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Existing platforms and information systems can be leveraged to enhance <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a>. Gresham et al. (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r23" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r23/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">23</a>) presented the Middle East Consortium on Infectious Disease Surveillance (MECIDS), a regional disease surveillance network of public health experts and ministry of health officials from Israel, Jordan and the Palestinian Authority, as a case example of use of regional disease surveillance networks as a useful mechanism for information sharing. MECIDS unites public health officials of differing Middle Eastern nationalities and contributes to regional health and stability by engaging in regular cross-border information exchanges, conducting regular executive board meetings, performing laboratory and risk communication training, and implementing innovative communication technology. Initially focusing on food- and waterborne diseases, the partnership developed a network of laboratories, protocols for specimen collection and diagnosis of diarrhoeal diseases, and data sharing and notification capabilities, to analyse and share information on disease threats.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Engagement of local stakeholders is important for an effective <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> strategy. The following mechanisms for engagement of local stakeholders in communication efforts were identified:
<ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>use of existing social networks in small municipalities for disaster risk reduction activities (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r24" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r24/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">24</a>);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>the need to centralize resources that can be used to facilitate communication with culturally diverse communities (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r25" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r25/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">25</a>); and</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>the identification and designation of an agency (e.g. in Taiwan, China, the police) with the best geographical and community reach to lead the process of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> information sharing (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r26" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r26/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">26</a>).</div></li></ul></div></li></ol><p>It was also important to address technology gaps in resource-poor areas, integrate nongovernmental organizations (NGOs) into the emergency management information system, and develop capacity to monitor and use relevant social media channels.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.2.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>The grey literature offered little evidence of the best ways to ensure coordination and information sharing. A total of 33 documents were identified as potentially relevant to coordination: 18 from sources of moderate credibility and 15 of high credibility. However, most of the reports addressed general collaboration and coordination, rather than risk communication, describing collaborative activities but providing no discussion or measure of effectiveness. One paper noted poor understanding between risk assessors and risk communicators to the extent that it was as if they spoke different languages, and suggested that simulation exercises and the training of risk communication personnel on the principles of risk assessment might overcome this (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r27" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r27/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">27</a>). Two documents discussed the importance of coordinating with the press before and during an incident, meeting their needs and remaining available to them. This type of coordination was considered especially important as many people depend on the media for their health information (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r28" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r28/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">28</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r29" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r29/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">29</a>). Three papers highlighted local collaboration efforts. In Montserrado County, Liberia, the International Rescue Committee (IRC) led a consortium of four NGOs. This proved to be more effective than working separately, as it allowed organizations to focus and capitalize on their areas of strength and advocate more effectively with the government. Sharing information between members helped build trust, as did getting technical input from <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a> (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r19" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r19/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">19</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s2.3"><h3>B.3. Capacity building</h3><div id="ch7.s2.3.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4><p><b>Preparation and training of personnel for <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> should be organized regularly and focus on coordination across involved stakeholders.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> found there was moderate quality evidence indicating that training focusing on coordination across agencies led to the improvement of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> capacity. Although this entailed altering the focus of training exercises, it was not considered to have significant feasibility and resource implications or harms. The evidence review also identified that incorporating media training and involving media professionals in the training was also beneficial. However, the GDG considered evidence of potential harms, such as concerns about feasibility and resource use, and this led the group to agree that this should be an implementation consideration.</p><p><b>Implementation consideration</b>: When planning training, consider incorporating, training and include media representatives in training exercises, as appropriate.</p><p><b>Remarks</b>: &#x02018;Focus on coordination&#x02019; means ensuring training exercises test the ability to share information and the development of coordinated risk communication interventions.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.3.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s2.3.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r30" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r30/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">30</a>)</h5><p>The majority of studies identified were based on exercises involving tabletop exercises, simulations, and, in a few cases, coursework or workshops. Findings synthesized across methodological streams indicate these exercises should focus on coordinating across agencies, building skills in dealing with media, and designing messages sensitive to audience needs and comprehension. The general consensus in the literature is that tabletop and simulation exercises can, and often do, improve awareness and understanding. These findings, however, come with major caveats. Limitations in methodological rigour, relevance and adequacy of data for most findings were moderate to severe. Among the prescriptive findings identified in the review, only the suggestions that exercises should focus on coordination across agencies and incorporate training in media can be held with even a moderate level of confidence.</p><p>Some confidence can be placed in descriptive findings about current norms in <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> training. Syntheses across all methodological streams indicate that current training exercises: do not employ post-action reports in a way that can promote generalized learning; contain little instruction on using social media; are generally disastrous; almost never employ blended online and face-to-face formats; and rarely include training in evaluation. However, because these findings are descriptive, they can only suggest directions for future research. Separate investigations will have to be undertaken to determine what influence each of these factors has on ERC skills.</p><p>Scholars have identified a general lack of rigour in studies of emergency preparedness training. They have observed that few standardized assessment tools exist. This makes it difficult to determine whether anecdotal success of drills, workshops, courses, and other types of training translate into actual additional skills and knowledge (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r31" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r31/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">31</a>). Also worth noting is that published evaluations of disaster preparedness training and staff development are mostly confined to the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI15/">USA</a>, meaning that even if research designs were more robust, findings might not necessarily be applicable to other national contexts.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.3.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>Reports on this subject agree that building risk communication capacity is needed at national, provincial and local levels. Presence or absence of a risk communication plan does not necessarily indicate capacity, nor do elements of infrastructure indicate operational capabilities. Failure to develop local risk communication capacities has led to reliance on outside experts being engaged in times of crisis. Rather than relying on outside assistance, risk communication capacity should be based at each geographical level &#x02013; local, national, regional and global &#x02013; with clearly defined roles, responsibilities and infrastructure, with particular focus on developing national capacities. A cadre of personnel should be trained in health crises and risk communication, and refresher training should be provided at least annually. Media personnel should be trained in public crisis communication (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r32" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r32/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">32</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r33" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r33/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">33</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r34" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r34/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">34</a>).</p><p>Reports focus on capacities that need to be strengthened, namely:
<ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>softer communication and interpersonal skills along with consensus-building (including document preparation);</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>analysis, documentation;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>monitoring and evaluation;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>recognition of variables known to provoke outrage, such as perceived unfairness, moral indiffe-rence, and impacts on vulnerable populations;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>development of communication strategies, plans and standards of practice;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>stakeholder and partner communications;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>community engagement;</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>sociopolitical, economic and cultural analysis for risk communication; and</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>translation of technical communications into understandable, contextualized material.</div></li></ul></p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s2.4"><h3>B.4. Finance</h3><div id="ch7.s2.4.1"><h4>Recommendation</h4><p><b><a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> requires a defined and sustained budget that should be a component of core budgeting for emergency preparedness, response and recovery.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Very low quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: An extensive evidence search found minimal discussion of policies, mechanisms or considerations of the importance of a defined and sustained budget for risk communication. The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> decided that grey literature evidence showing that lack of funding hampered <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> during recent crises was indicative of the importance of recommending the development of such policies and mechanisms.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.4.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s2.4.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r35" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r35/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">35</a>)</h5><p>There is little evidence that usefully addresses the question. Although 341 potentially relevant documents were identified, most do not directly discuss risk communication funding. Some mention financing mechanisms, such as microcredit, insurance and pool funds to be used for disaster risk management (DRM). Many mention risk communication or public awareness raising/education as part of disaster risk reduction (DRR). Some mention that even where DRR awareness/education materials exist, the prevention messages do not reach the local level. Others mention the need for governments to dedicate funds (for example 1% of the national budget) to DRM/DRR. Nearly all the documents list funding as a major challenge for DRM/DRR, with some noting that funding falls into a gap between development and relief programmes, and is therefore funded by neither. Another frequent message was the need to combine DRM/DRR and climate change adaptation (CCA) funding and efforts.</p></div><div id="ch7.s2.4.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>The grey literature contained minimal relevant material. However, one consistent theme was that lack of funding hampered initial risk communication efforts at the beginning of the 2014&#x02013;2015 West African Ebola virus disease outbreak (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r36" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r36/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">36</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r37" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r37/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">37</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r38" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r38/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">38</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r39" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r39/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">39</a>).</p></div></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3"><h2 id="_ch7_s3_">C. Emergency risk communication practice</h2><p>During the initial guideline-scoping meeting, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> set priority questions seeking evidence of the best means of practising risk communication, considering the evolution of new media, and major societal and demographic change. The following best practice statements and recommendations are based on the evidence reviewed for those questions.</p><div id="ch7.s3.1"><h3>C.1. Strategic communication planning</h3><p>Strategic planning primarily involves the assessment and evaluation of intervention activities in order to improve public awareness and influence behaviour before, during and after a public health emergency event. The evidence review indicated there is no singular strategy for ensuring successful communication in such situations. The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed that planning is an overarching best practice that should be presented prior to the specific recommendations on <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> practice.</p><div id="ch7.s3.1.1"><h4>Best practice statement</h4><p><b><a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI4/">ERC</a> planning must occur well in advance, and be a continuous process with a focus on preparedness as well as response. Planning should be sensitive to stakeholders&#x02019; needs, participatory, responsive to the context and incorporate feedback from affected groups.</b></p><div id="ch7.s3.1.1.1"><h5>Remarks</h5><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Planning functions best through collaboration among constituent groups with clearly defined roles and responsibilities. Health agencies, emergency systems, and other public services need to collaborate and establish communication networks in preparation for events.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Communication planning must consider the communities, cultures and lifestyles of different segments of the public; and further, design disaster education and preparation around these social structures.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Multiple channels and means of communicating disaster messages should be identified and used.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Planning should include training/information updates for communications personnel and mechanisms for obtaining initial rapid situation assessments.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Planning should include pre-positioning of communication resources/materials, including core messages, information (e.g. factsheets) and discussion content.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Planning should include establishment of mechanisms for monitoring and assessing the effectiveness of messages and adjusting them as necessary.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Planning must be an inclusive process where all affected groups are sought, incorporated, listened to and involved in order that they take ownership. Potentially affected communities must be involved at the planning stage.</div></li></ul></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.1.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s3.1.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r40" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r40/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">40</a>)</h5><p>The reviewed studies demonstrate the importance of merging scientific expertise with the local knowledge (first-hand experience) of the communities affected by a public health emergency event. Strategic planning efforts must take into account the role of culture in preparation and response in order to contextualize efforts to meet the needs of diverse populations. Efforts across event phases must involve local stakeholders, who play an important role in communicating key messages and moving populations from awareness to action.</p><p>Research has focused on disaster response, helping to provide an understanding of the steps necessary to mitigate disaster situations. However, the studies in the present review encourage more proactive efforts devoted to disaster preparedness. This is particularly so in regions and communities that may be desensitized to the threat and harm caused by disaster because their previous history with disaster situations may have lowered their risk perception. Thus, increased information does not automatically lead to behavioural change, and it is important to use multiple channels and means of communication to reach these populations. This involves moving beyond traditional forms of mass communication to more localized forms including, but not limited to the use of community and family members to spread key messages, intervention programmes, long-term awareness campaigns, and making information available through community centres, religious centres, and other channels used to access information in a disaster.</p><p>The findings of the present review largely align with the conclusions of existing reviews set out below.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>The need for various health agencies, emergency systems, and other public services to collaborate and establish communication networks in preparation for events.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Communication strategies must consider the communities, cultures and lifestyles of different segments of the public, and design disaster education and preparation around these social structures.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Risk perception is the primary predictor for disaster prevention and mitigation behaviours.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Risk perception is itself determined by a variety of factors, including knowledge of disasters, trust in officials and demographic characteristics.</div></li></ul></div><div id="ch7.s3.1.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>According to the grey literature, effective strategic communication planning begins long before a crisis and involves a number of steps: creating a strategy and framework for communicating with all stakeholders; developing and vetting basic messages for issues likely to arise during a crisis; training communications personnel; and developing a system for rapid message review and approval (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r22" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r22/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">22</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r28" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r28/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">28</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r41" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r41/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">41</a>). Roles and responsibilities should be outlined and clearly defined (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r28" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r28/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">28</a>), and a network of communication partnerships should be created and maintained. A plan for working with the media should be developed (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r42" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r42/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">42</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r43" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r43/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">43</a>), as should a media toolkit (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r29" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r29/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">29</a>). Adequate and timely funding should be secured (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r44" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r44/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">44</a>).</p><p>A second finding was that the communication process needs to start early (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r38" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r38/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">38</a>) and an essential first step is to conduct an assessment with community leaders and members to identify favoured communication channels, barriers or potential problems, as well as potential solutions (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r32" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r32/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">32</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r45" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r45/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">45</a>). One study noted that medical anthropological assessment should be used at the beginning of a response, so that messages and interventions can be shaped accordingly (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r46" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r46/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">46</a>). This assessment should include listening to complaints and taking into account the customs and cultures of all involved groups (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r28" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r28/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">28</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r47" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r47/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">47</a>).</p><p>An example of these steps was provided by the Community Led Ebola Management and Eradication (CLEME) approach (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r48" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r48/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">48</a>). This started with an assessment of the situation, community mapping, and information gathering on caring for the sick and the dead, and a walk-through of the community. The community then decided upon interventions together, tailored these for specific groups and implemented them. Contextual analysis, feedback and follow-up visits were used to adjust the interventions.</p><p>Additional elements of good communications planning included: distinguishing evidence-based messages from uncertain ones (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r36" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r36/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">36</a>); ensuring that lessons learned were captured and applied (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r44" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r44/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">44</a>); and building local communication capacities (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r42" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r42/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">42</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.2"><h3>C.2. Monitoring and evaluation tools</h3><div id="ch7.s3.2.1"><h4>Research recommendation</h4><p><b>Research is required to establish best mechanisms and methods for rapidly evaluating <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> interventions, and incorporating evaluation findings and feedback from stakeholders and communities to inform and improve ongoing and future responses.</b></p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed that establishing an effective &#x02018;feedback loop&#x02019; enabling incorporation of evaluation findings into <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/gl/def-item/abb_DL2_DI4/">ERC</a> is essential but the systematic review found no evidence supporting the effectiveness of any of the current evaluation tools. The GDG, therefore, decided to recommend that tools used to establish feedback loops, which are all currently being used (see list in findings below), but are not being systematically studied for evidence of effectiveness, should be a priority area of risk communication research. Although all areas of risk communication practice require further research (see &#x02018;research priorities and gaps&#x02019; <a href="#ch7.s3.5">section 7.1</a>., <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab3/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figch7tab3" rid-ob="figobch7tab3">Table 5</a>), this was seen as such a crucial gap that the GDG made this a standalone recommendation.</p></div><div id="ch7.s3.2.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><p>Although there was no evidence indicating which tools worked best, <a class="figpopup" href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-figpopup="figch7tab2" rid-ob="figobch7tab2">Table 4</a> lists tools used to gather and categorize data from the literature by phase of response, which may be considered for research to establish the best mechanisms for developing a feedback loop.</p><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col table-wrap" id="figch7tab2"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab2/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Table 4" class="img_link icnblk_img figpopup" rid-figpopup="figch7tab2" rid-ob="figobch7tab2"><img class="small-thumb" src="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab2/?report=thumb" src-large="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab2/?report=previmg" alt="Table 4. Data gathering tools." /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt"><h4 id="ch7.tab2"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab2/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobch7tab2">Table 4</a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Data gathering tools. </p></div></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.3"><h3>C.3. Social media</h3><div id="ch7.s3.3.1"><h4>Recommendations</h4><p><b>C3.1. Social media may be used to engage the public, facilitate peer-to-peer communication, create situational awareness, monitor and respond to rumours, public reactions and concerns during an emergency, and to facilitate local-level responses.</b></p><p>Conditional recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: There was moderate quality evidence indicating that social media engages the public and is a means of: creating situational awareness; monitoring and responding to rumours; addressing public concerns and reactions; and facilitating peer-to-peer communication and local-level responses. However, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a>, having assessed resource requirements, feasibility and harms such as misuse, cultural concerns and limitations, decided that this recommendation should be conditional due to the contextual nature of social media use and application. Social media encompasses a wide range of activities and tools, such as social networking, photo/video sharing, live streaming and micro-blogging, each of which has different limitations, different appeal in different circumstances and different levels of affordability.</p><p><b>C3.2. Social media and traditional media should be part of an integrated strategy with other forms of communication to achieve convergence of verified, accurate information.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: There was moderate quality evidence indicating that integrating social media with traditional media interventions achieves the benefit of information convergence and trust in that information. Resource implications were minimal, and neither significant harms nor feasibility concerns were identified. Thus, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed this should be a strong recommendation.</p><p><b>Implementation consideration</b>: (applies to implementation of both recommendations above): Government agencies and implementing partners need to train, employ and pay dedicated social media officers in order to build relationships with stakeholders, and to use social media consistently to build trust and credibility before, during and after emergencies.</p></div><div id="ch7.s3.3.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s3.3.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r49" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r49/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">49</a>)</h5><p>Most studies were undertaken in Australia, China, Japan and the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI15/">USA</a>, as well as Europe. Earthquakes, typhoons, infectious diseases and floods were studied most often. Most studies of risk communication in social media focus on public and government agency posts on Twitter, Sina Weibo (the Twitter equivalent in China) and Facebook. A minority of studies focus on other social media sites such as Flickr, SMS, blogs, YouTube and mapping apps. The phases most commonly analysed are the containment phase or the containment phase in combination with preparation, onset and/or recovery phases. Most studies focus on the general population or on health agency officials; only a small minority revealed sample demographics focusing on vulnerable groups.</p><p>The review found that social media, especially Twitter and Facebook, could be used to spread truthful information and to verify information to dispel rumours and misinformation during public health crises. This is especially important for alternative social media not immediately controlled by the government in countries where people are distrustful of official government messages and campaigns, and turn to peers online to find more information.</p><p>Studies demonstrate that users on social media platforms, especially Twitter and Facebook, verify the vast majority of messages via self-regulation. Agencies actively use myth-busting messages to address rumours and spread truthful information. While peer monitoring and correcting largely maintain the accuracy of social media messages, it was found that government agencies and implementing partners needed to train, employ and pay dedicated social media officers to build relationships with at-risk communities and stakeholders, to use social media consistently to build trust and credibility, and address rumours and misinformation as soon as they arise.</p><p>The use of hashtags was found to be helpful when busting myths. This was shown in a series of studies based in Australia during a flood, where the social media accounts by local police were celebrated for their prompt myth busting and reliability to provide consistent, timely updates with accurate information. It was found that government agencies should use hashtags that have &#x02018;organically&#x02019; developed on social media, are already used by the majority of the public, and are in wide circulation, rather than creating and insisting on others using their own newly branded hashtags for an event.</p></div><div id="ch7.s3.3.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>One study found that 87% of doctors in Brazil use WhatsApp to communicate with patients and that the use of mobile phones had overtaken television as the main form of media consumption (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r50" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r50/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">50</a>). In West Africa, chat apps, especially WhatsApp, were considered better than short message services or SMS because they were cheaper. WhatsApp also proved useful for tracking rumours (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r36" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r36/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">36</a>). Other new media tools included RapidPro and SMS systems (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r51" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r51/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">51</a>). It was also found that social media is being used increasingly to monitor what the public is saying about public health issues (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r27" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r27/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">27</a>).</p><p>SMS or text messaging was used successfully to track and combat rumours and to communicate with people in quarantined areas during the West African Ebola virus outbreak (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r38" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r38/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">38</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r39" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r39/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">39</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r43" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r43/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">43</a>). A collaborative effort between the British Broadcasting Corporation (BBC) and WhatsApp enabled messages from <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a>, the United Nations Children&#x02019;s Fund (UNICEF) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) in the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI15/">USA</a>, to be channelled directly to 20 000 subscribers, most of whom were in West Africa. In Sierra Leone, the local version of this channel had 15 000 subscribers by the end of the outbreak (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r44" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r44/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">44</a>). Text messaging was also used for real-time monitoring (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r34" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r34/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">34</a>). Nigeria used mobile phones to disseminate Ebola messages (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r52" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r52/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">52</a>), and the government of Sierra Leone chose WhatsApp as one of its official response channels. Social media enabled the Sierra Leonean diaspora to play a role in social mobilization, despite living abroad, by using Skype, Facebook, WhatsApp and smartphones to share information about the outbreak. Members of the diaspora who were in the health professions used social media to mobilize professional, business and political connections, and their families (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r53" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r53/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">53</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.4"><h3>C.4. Messaging</h3><div id="ch7.s3.4.1"><h4>Recommendations</h4><p><b>C4.1. Risk should not be explained in technical terms, as this is not helpful for promoting risk mitigation behaviours.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed that there was evidence of harm when technical language confused or prevented people from taking protective actions and behaviours. There was strong agreement that avoiding the use of technical language would increase risk mitigation, and that this was feasible and did not have major resource implications.</p><p><b>C4.2. Consistent messages should come from different information sources and emerge early on in the emergency.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: This review and other reviews on trust and uncertainty all provide evidence that the use of consistent messaging via different information sources in an emergency increases the likelihood that the messages will be believed and acted upon. The <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> agreed that this was an important messaging practice. They also emphasized the potential harms of late messaging enabling rumours to take hold and form the basis of established beliefs.</p><p><b>C4.3. Messages should promote specific actions people can realistically take to protect their health.</b></p><p>Strong recommendation</p><p>Moderate quality evidence</p><p><b>Rationale</b>: The benefit of specific, culturally and contextually appropriate actions that people can take to protect themselves during an emergency was emphasised in this evidence review and other reviews on trust. The harm caused by providing vague or general messages that mean little and confuse people was noted by the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a>. Equity &#x02013; providing realistic actions to vulnerable groups, such as disabled people &#x02013; was an important element of the decision to make this a strong recommendation.</p><div id="ch7.s3.4.1.1"><h5>Remarks</h5><p>Implementation considerations for all messaging recommendations: (i) messages need to arise from and be adapted to cultural contexts by using pretesting with intended audiences; (ii) messages need to be reviewed and reshaped periodically as the emergency evolves.</p></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.4.2"><h4>Summary of evidence and considerations</h4><div id="ch7.s3.4.2.1"><h5>Systematic review findings (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r54" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r54/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">54</a>)</h5><p>The findings of the review of 34 English language and 33 other UN language studies are listed below.</p><ul><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Public health officials appear to be the most credible source for risk information. Depending on the culture and, in some cases, government officials and non-local information sources are generally found to be less credible.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Although traditional mass media, particularly radio and television, have been found to have the most impact, interpersonal communication networks become important when events include interruption to or loss of power supply.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Messages should come from different information sources and emerge early in the outbreak; the lack of early messages allows rumours to take hold.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Messages should be conveyed in non-technical language.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Messages from one set of sources will get integrated into messages from other sources, whether they emanate from mass media or family and friends. Because of this message integration at the individual level, messages should be developed &#x02018;bottom up&#x02019; so that culture and politics may be considered.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>Consideration of local cultural understanding will allow messages to recommend specific health protection behaviours appropriate to the culture.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>It is important to note that individuals and sometimes communities will dismiss many sorts of messages for a variety of reasons, which are sometimes shared but are not easy to generalize.</div></li><li class="half_rhythm"><div>For example, getting people to evacuate or obey quarantine restrictions is difficult across cultures and political systems, but the reasons that underlie that difficulty vary widely.</div></li></ul></div><div id="ch7.s3.4.2.2"><h5>Grey literature review findings</h5><p>The grey literature review found that it is essential to know how people obtain their health information (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r55" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r55/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">55</a>). Without this, even the best-crafted messages may be wasted if they are placed where they will not be noticed. The target group should assess this since media consumption varies greatly.</p><p>Source credibility and trustworthiness affects the adoption of behaviour changes. Several studies found that invoking credible sources (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r28" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r28/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">28</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r43" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r43/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">43</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r56" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r56/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">56</a>), or the opinions of trusted community leaders, family members or friends could influence behaviour change (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r13" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r13/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">13</a>). The use of messages sharing survivor numbers and stories also encouraged behaviour uptake (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r50" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r50/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">50</a>). One report notes that if experts are used, reference must also be made to their trustworthiness (<a href="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/#r43" data-bk-pop-rid="/books/n/who259807/rl.r1/def-item/r43/" data-bk-pop-others="" class="bk_pop">43</a>).</p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.5"><h3>7.1. Research priorities and gaps</h3><div class="iconblock whole_rhythm clearfix ten_col table-wrap" id="figch7tab3"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab3/?report=objectonly" target="object" title="Table 5" class="img_link icnblk_img figpopup" rid-figpopup="figch7tab3" rid-ob="figobch7tab3"><img class="small-thumb" src="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab3/?report=thumb" src-large="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab3/?report=previmg" alt="Table 5. Research priorities and gaps." /></a><div class="icnblk_cntnt"><h4 id="ch7.tab3"><a href="/books/NBK540733/table/ch7.tab3/?report=objectonly" target="object" rid-ob="figobch7tab3">Table 5</a></h4><p class="float-caption no_bottom_margin">Research priorities and gaps. </p></div></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.6"><h3>7.2. Composition of guideline groups</h3><div id="ch7.s3.6.1"><h4>Internal Steering Group (ISG)</h4><p>Set up in 2014, the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI10/">ISG</a> comprises <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a> staff members involved in emergency preparedness and response, and communications. An initial informal discussion was held in October 2014, followed by a formal meeting in March 2015. The ISG has overseen the scoping of the guidelines, the selection of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> members, the management of the systematic reviews, and the final production of the guidelines. The full list of ISG members is provided in <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/">Annex 1</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/table/annex1.tab1/?report=objectonly" target="object">Table 6</a>.</p></div></div><div id="ch7.s3.7"><h3>7.3. Guideline Development Group</h3><p>The secretariat consulted with key partners, stakeholders and the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI10/">ISG</a> to identify individuals with the appropriate expertise in areas of work such as emergencies, pandemics and epidemics, disaster risk management, communications, and other relevant disciplines, such as anthropology, sociology and health systems. Selections were also governed by the need for gender and regional balance. <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/">Annex 1</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/table/annex1.tab2/?report=objectonly" target="object">Table 7</a> gives the full list of <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> members, their affiliations, expertise and declared interests.</p><div id="ch7.s3.7.1"><h4>GDG chairpersons</h4><p>Marsha Vanderford, who was then Associate Director for Communications at the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, Center for Global Health, Atlanta, Georgia, <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI15/">USA</a>, was the initial <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> chair-person, chairing the GDG during the first scoping meeting, question formulation and evidence search phase. However, in January 2016, Dr Vanderford became a <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a> staff member and was no longer eligible to serve on the GDG. Two chairpersons replaced her, Amrita Gill Bailey from the Strategic Communication Programs, Johns Hopkins University, Baltimore, Maryland, USA, and Peter Banga from the Polytechnic School of Journalism and Media Studies, Bulawayo, Zimbabwe. Ms Bailey and Mr Banga co-chaired the guideline development meeting held in Geneva, 16&#x02013;17 February 2017, during which the GDG used the evidence retrieved to make recommendations.</p></div><div id="ch7.s3.7.2"><h4>External Review Group (ERG)</h4><p><a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI5/">ERG</a> members comprised risk communication experts and practitioners with experience in emergencies, academic interests in the analysis of risk communication strategies, and experience working in regions of the world prone to outbreaks and emergencies. Their role was to review the draft guidelines for clarity, comment on how actionable or useful the recommendations were, and identify any contextual issues, implications for implementation, issues for end-users and any other areas or issues they considered important [<a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/">Annex 1</a>, <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/table/annex1.tab3/?report=objectonly" target="object">Table 8</a> lists ERG members, their affiliations, and Conflict of Interests (<a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI2/">COI</a>), if any].</p></div><div id="ch7.s3.7.3"><h4>Management of conflicts of interest</h4><p>All experts participating in the development of these guidelines &#x02013; <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> members, <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI5/">ERG</a> members, systematic reviewers, methodologists and other consultants &#x02013; were asked to complete a Declaration of Interest (<a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI3/">DOI</a>) form detailing any interest relevant to the subject matter before their participation.</p><p>The secretariat also performed an online search of activities and publications by potential <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> members and <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI5/">ERG</a> members to determine whether they had competing interests precluding participation in the development of the guidelines. GDG members were also asked to submit short biographies, which were posted publicly for comments prior to their membership being confirmed.</p><p>The secretariat reviewed and assessed all declared interests prior to each meeting to determine whether any participants had competing interests that may preclude or limit participation in the process. Any declared interests that might be considered competing interests were referred to the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a> Compliance, Risk Management and Ethics Office for review. Before each <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> meeting, participants were asked to update their declaration of interest forms. These were summarized and presented to the entire group, so that the group was aware of any existing potentially competing interests.</p><p>During every <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> meeting, a clear explanation of what was considered or defined as a conflict of interest (<a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI2/">COI</a>) was provided. These included any interest (e.g. academic) that could reasonably be perceived to affect an individual&#x02019;s objectivity and independence while working with <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI16/">WHO</a>. All GDG members present were then asked whether they had any competing interests to declare to the group beyond what had been provided in their <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI3/">DOI</a> forms.</p><p>As described above, all individuals invited to participate in a substantive way in developing the guidelines (including preparation of systematic reviews and evidence profiles, or contributing to formulating recommendations and writing the guidelines) also completed <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI3/">DOI</a> forms, which were reviewed by the secretariat prior to confirmation of their role. None of the members of the <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI6/">GDG</a> or <a class="def" href="/books/n/who259807/abb/def-item/abb_DL1_DI5/">ERG</a>, systematic reviewers, methodologists and consultants supporting the guideline panel were deemed to have competing interests precluding or limiting their participation. See <a href="/books/n/who259807/annex1/">Annex 1</a> for declared interests.</p></div></div></div><div id="bk_toc_contnr"></div></div></div>
