# Logical Outgrowth Doctrine

## Overview

The logical outgrowth doctrine is a judicially created interpretation ensuring a balance between flexibility for agencies to respond to public comments and adequate notice to interested parties of the subjects that could be addressed in a final rule.

## Origin and Definition

The term "logical outgrowth" first appeared in a 1974 circuit court case, *South Terminal Corp. v. EPA*. The test determines whether the provisions of the final rule **logically grow out of the terms of the proposed rule**, and if so, then the commenters are deemed to have received adequate notice.

## Purpose and Application

The APA notice-and-comment process recognizes that changes may be made to the proposed rule based on the public comments received, but courts have required that any changes made in the final rule be of a type that could have been reasonably anticipated by the public – a logical outgrowth of the proposal.

## Consequences

If a change is not a logical outgrowth of the proposal in the NPRM, an agency is required to provide the public with a further opportunity for comment. If the "logical outgrowth test" is not met, an agency would need to provide a second notice with an opportunity for public comment on the changes.

## Implications for Comment Strategy

Commenters can use logical outgrowth strategically:
- **Suggesting alternatives** within the proposal's scope creates a stronger record - the agency can adopt them without re-noticing
- **Identifying gaps** between the proposal and ideal regulation can prompt re-notice
- **Comments must be specific** about which alternatives they support so the agency can claim their version is a "logical outgrowth"
- **Mass campaigns that don't suggest specific alternatives** may inadvertently constrain agencies (no logical outgrowth coverage)
