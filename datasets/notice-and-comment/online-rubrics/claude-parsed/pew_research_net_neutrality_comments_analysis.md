# Pew Research: Net Neutrality Public Comments Analysis

## Overview of the 2017 Comment Campaign

From April 27 to August 30, 2017, the FCC allowed members of the public to formally submit comments on net neutrality, and the FCC was inundated with nearly 22 million public comments.

## Evidence of Organized Campaigns and Duplicates

- Of the 21.7 million comments posted, only **6% were unique**. The other 94% were submitted multiple times – in some cases, hundreds of thousands of times.
- The seven most-submitted comments (six of which argued against net neutrality regulations) comprise 38% of all the submissions over the four-month comment period.

## Coordination and Tracing to Organizations

The text of many of the top comments can be traced back to a small number of organizations. The single most-popular comment was a pro-net-neutrality statement that appeared as a submission form on the website battleforthenet.com. The wording for three popular comments opposing net neutrality (representing the second-, sixth- and ninth-most submitted overall) appeared on the website for an organization known as the Taxpayers Protection Alliance. Combined, the text from these three suggested comments appeared in almost 2.4 million submissions, making up 11% of the total.

## Fraud and Fake Comments

More than 8.5 million fake comments that impersonated real people were submitted to the FCC. This practice — disguising an orchestrated, paid campaign as a grassroots effort, to create a false appearance of genuine, unpaid public support — is often referred to as astroturfing.

Three lead generators were responsible for millions of the fake comments: Fluent, Inc. (~4.8 million fraudulent comments); Opt-Intelligence, Inc. (>250,000); and React2Media, Inc. (~329,000).

## Implications for Comment Effectiveness Rubrics

This analysis demonstrates how agencies and analysts identify low-quality or fraudulent comments:
- Identical or near-identical text across many submissions
- Submission patterns inconsistent with stated identities (timing, geography)
- Email addresses tied to lead-generation services
- Lack of unique substantive engagement with the proposed rule
