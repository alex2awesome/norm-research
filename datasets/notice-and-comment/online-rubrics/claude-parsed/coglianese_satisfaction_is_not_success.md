# Satisfaction Is Not the Same as Policy Success (Coglianese, The Regulatory Review)

## Core Thesis
Participant contentment with regulatory processes does not indicate actual policy quality. Participants may feel satisfied with decisions that ultimately fail.

## Key Arguments
**The Satisfaction Problem**: "Satisfaction is not an appropriate basis for evaluating the overall value of public policies" because consensus-based decisions often reflect the lowest common denominator rather than effective solutions.

**Real-World Failures:**
1. **EPA Reformulated Gasoline Rule (1990s)**: Negotiators reached consensus on MTBE fuel additives, yet the chemical later contaminated groundwater nationwide.
2. **California Electricity Restructuring (1990s)**: A market compromise satisfied stakeholders when wholesale rates were low but proved disastrous when conditions changed.

## Implications for Commenters
True evaluation requires focusing on "outcomes of policy decisions" rather than participant surveys. Regulated entities — well-organized business firms — often dominate participation processes. When regulated entities leave "satisfied with a policy-making process," the policy may be weaker than necessary.

**Bottom line**: Researchers and policymakers should examine whether regulations achieve their social welfare goals, not whether participants feel good about decisions. Comments that argue from outcomes (not from process satisfaction) are stronger.
