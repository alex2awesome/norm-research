# Farina & Newhart - Rulemaking 2.0: Understanding and Getting Better Public Participation

Cornell Law School / Cornell e-Rulemaking Initiative.

## Three Barriers to Effective Public Participation

Broader, better public participation is hampered by:
1. **Ignorance of the rulemaking process** - Most citizens don't know how rulemaking works
2. **Unawareness that rulemakings of interest are going on** - Notice doesn't reach affected parties
3. **Information overload** from the length and complexity of rulemaking materials

## Quality vs Quantity

At least with respect to rulemaking and similar complex policymaking processes, more public participation is good only if it is the kind of participation that has value in the process.

Successful "Rulemaking 2.0" civic engagement systems must involve a purposeful and continuous effort to balance "more" and "better" participation.

## Design Principles for Quality Comments

The Regulation Room project identified several specific design principles for striking this balance, perhaps the most important of which is that **a democratic government should not actively facilitate public participation that it does not value**.

Key elements of valuable participation include:
- Comments that engage with the specific provisions of the rule
- Information on real-world impacts the agency cannot easily access
- Substantive responses to the agency's stated questions
- Use of accessible, non-technical language while remaining substantively grounded

## Knowledge in the People

Farina and colleagues argue that the most valuable contribution citizens make is "knowledge in the people" - situated, experiential knowledge that agencies cannot otherwise access. This is distinct from technical/legal expertise (which agencies can hire) and from raw expressions of preference (which have low marginal value).
