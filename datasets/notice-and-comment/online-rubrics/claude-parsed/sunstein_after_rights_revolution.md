# Sunstein, "After the Rights Revolution: Reconceiving the Regulatory State" (1990)

Sunstein articulates **26 interpretive norms** courts should use when reviewing regulatory
statutes and agency action, organized around four general goals: (1) promote deliberation,
(2) furnish surrogates for it when absent, (3) limit factionalism and self-interested
representation, and (4) help bring about political equality.

## Sunstein's Four Goals (Adapted as Comment Quality Frame)

### Goal 1: Promote Deliberation
Comments are valuable when they **promote agency deliberation** rather than substitute
preference-aggregation for reasoning. A high-quality comment:
- Provides reasons connected to public values
- Engages the agency's reasoning rather than asserting raw preferences
- Surfaces tradeoffs the agency must reason through

### Goal 2: Furnish Surrogates Where Deliberation Is Absent
When deliberation is captured or thin, comments can serve as a deliberative supplement.
Effective comments:
- Articulate the public interest beyond narrow stakeholder positions
- Bring perspectives systematically excluded from agency deliberation
- Apply public-regarding reasoning to private interest claims

### Goal 3: Limit Factionalism and Self-Interested Representation
Sunstein worries that agencies serve narrow factions. Effective comments:
- Identify when industry submissions reflect captured analysis
- Trace stakeholder positions to their underlying interests
- Argue against rules that disproportionately benefit the most concentrated interests

### Goal 4: Promote Political Equality
Sunstein gives high priority to protection of disadvantaged groups. Effective comments:
- Identify distributional impacts on disadvantaged populations
- Argue against rules that systematically harm vulnerable groups
- Bring affected disadvantaged voices to the docket

## Rubric Items Distilled From Sunstein's Framework

- [ ] Does the comment promote agency deliberation by surfacing reasons and tradeoffs?
- [ ] Does the comment serve as a deliberative supplement when stakeholder voices are
      one-sided?
- [ ] Does the comment trace stakeholder positions to underlying interests to expose
      factionalism?
- [ ] Does the comment identify distributional consequences on disadvantaged populations?
- [ ] Does the comment ground its reasoning in public values rather than private interests
      alone?
- [ ] Does the comment connect to a public-regarding statutory purpose?

## Caution

Sunstein's framework is interpretively rich and contested. Comments grounded in this
framework should engage with the statutory language and avoid appearing to import
extra-statutory values without textual hooks.
