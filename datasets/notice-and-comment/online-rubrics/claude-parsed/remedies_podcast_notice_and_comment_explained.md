# Notice-and-Comment Rulemaking Explained (Administrative Remedies Podcast)

## Five-Step Process
1. **Publication of Proposed Rule** — Notice of Proposed Rulemaking in the Federal Register.
2. **Public Comment Period** — "Usually 30 to 60 days. It can be longer in cases of big rules." Anyone globally can submit; no formal invitation required.
3. **Agency Review** — agencies must read and consider all comments (potentially millions for major rules).
4. **Final Rule Publication** — agency publishes final rule with preamble explaining legal authority, responding to significant comments, and justifying decisions.
5. **Effective Date** — typically 30 days after publication.

## What Makes Comments Effective
- **Specific and evidence-based** rather than vague opinions
- **Focused on measurable impacts** and practical concerns
- **Addressing overlooked issues** the agency may not have considered

"Just saying that they like it or don't like it" is much weaker than presenting specific problems with reasoning.

## Critical Misconception: It's Not a Vote
"This is absolutely not a vote. The agency isn't doing a poll." The agency is not obligated to adopt majority-supported positions. It must engage substantively with comments and explain its reasoning — a requirement that strengthens legal defensibility on judicial review.

## The Miniature Horse Case Study
DOT's 2020 service animal rulemaking — despite 400+ commenters supporting miniature horses as service animals, DOT excluded them. But the comment process forced the agency to:
- Address ADA consistency arguments
- Explain why aircraft cabin safety differs from public accommodations
- Document specific concerns ("hooves could puncture an aircraft evacuation slide")

## The Ossification Problem
The process has become so elaborate that agencies sometimes bypass it entirely by issuing non-binding guidance — accountability protections paradoxically reduce accountability.

## Why It Matters
"Notice and comment rulemaking is how democracy works in an administrative state." It combines agency expertise with public knowledge of practical problems and local conditions.
