# Libgober & Rashin, "What Public Comments During Rulemaking Do (and Why)" (2023)

Manual coding of comments in high-stakes financial rulemakings. Finding: **the vast majority
of effective comments have informational content** — describing policy consequences — rather
than threatening political or judicial sanctions.

## Two Persuasion Tactics in Comments

| Tactic | What It Looks Like | Frequency |
|--------|-------------------|-----------|
| Informational | Description of policy consequences (costs, benefits, operational impact, downstream effects) | Vast majority |
| Threat | Implicit or explicit warning of judicial review, congressional pressure, or political consequences | Limited |

## Why This Matters for the Rubric

If informational content predominates among effective comments, then:

### 1. Substantive Information Is the Main Currency
Effective comments **describe what will happen** if the rule is adopted as proposed:
- Cost figures grounded in specific operational analysis
- Quantified compliance burden by firm size, sector, or geography
- Predicted behavioral responses by affected parties
- Distributional consequences by income, region, or sector

### 2. Threats Are Generally Counter-Productive
Comments that threaten litigation or congressional pressure without substantive informational
content rarely move agencies. **The implicit threat works better when it follows substantive
content** that establishes the commenter's credibility.

### 3. Strategic Actors Behave Strategically
Even sophisticated commenters in high-stakes rulemakings emphasize information over threats —
suggesting agencies do respond to information-based persuasion.

## Rubric Items

- [ ] Does the comment describe specific policy consequences (not just preferences)?
- [ ] Does the comment quantify impacts (costs, benefits, affected populations)?
- [ ] Does the comment predict behavioral responses by regulated parties?
- [ ] Does the comment include credible factual basis before any reference to judicial or
      political consequences?
- [ ] If the comment raises legal/political risk, does it do so as supporting context rather
      than as the primary persuasion mechanism?
