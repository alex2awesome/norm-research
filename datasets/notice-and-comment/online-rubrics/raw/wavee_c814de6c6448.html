Title IX Updates 2026 & Beyond | What You Need to Know?

Title IX enforcement has changed in significant ways over the past year, and those changes matter for students, families, and universities alike. If your child is facing an investigation or disciplinary action at their college or university, the rules governing how that process unfolds may have changed dramatically in the past several months.

What has changed since August 2024?

We understand how much is at stake when a student’s academic future hangs in the balance. Staying current on these regulatory changes is critical to mounting an effective defense. This guide explains the current Title IX regulatory framework, the fate of the short-lived 2024 rules, and what families should expect as enforcement continues under the reinstated 2020 regulations.

U.S. Department of Education’s Title IX Regulations: Authority and Publication

Before examining what has changed, it helps to understand how Title IX regulations come into being. We must also look at where the Department of Education derives its authority to enforce them.

Department Authority to Regulate Title IX

The U.S. Department of Education possesses statutory authority to issue implementing regulations for Title IX. This authority allows the Department to enforce the law’s prohibition on sex discrimination in educational programs that receive federal funding.

That authority, however, has limits. Regulations must still align with Title IX’s underlying statutory purpose. Courts have repeatedly stepped in when regulations exceed that authority or collide with constitutional protections. In short, the Department’s regulatory power extends only as far as the statute allows.

When agencies attempt to redefine core statutory terms or push beyond what Congress authorized, courts are often quick to step in. This is precisely what happened with the 2024 regulations.

Where Title IX Rules and Notices Are Published

All Title IX regulations, proposed rules, and public notices appear in the Federal Register, the official daily publication of federal agency actions. This publication requirement exists to ensure transparency and give schools and individuals clear notice of what is required.

The Department’s Office for Civil Rights (OCR) maintains a dedicated page for Federal Register notices and regulations. This makes these documents accessible to schools and the public.

This matters because schools are required to follow the exact regulatory language as published, not informal summaries or prior guidance. When regulatory frameworks shift as dramatically as they did between 2020 and 2025, knowing which version applies is critical. The difference may determine whether an investigation is compliant or procedurally flawed. A violation here could undermine the entire process against a student.

Regulatory History Timeline: Pre-2020 Framework

Title IX has been the law since 1972. However, the specific regulations governing how schools respond to sexual harassment complaints have evolved considerably over the decades. For a deeper dive into this evolution, you can review the legislative history of Title IX.

Original Promulgation of Title IX Regulations (1975)

The predecessor agency to the Department of Education was the U.S. Department of Health, Education, and Welfare. This agency first issued Title IX implementing regulations in 1975. These original regulations established the foundation for compliance. They dictated how educational institutions receiving federal funds must comply with the statute’s prohibition on sex discrimination.

Recodification Without Substantive Change (1980)

Congress created the standalone Department of Education in 1980. At that time, Title IX regulations were recodified under the new agency’s authority. This was a technical transfer. No substantive changes occurred to the regulations themselves during this transition. The rules simply moved from one agency’s jurisdiction to another.

This history helps explain just how unusual the recent regulatory swings have been. The dramatic regulatory shifts of recent years represent a departure from this longstanding approach.

2020 Amendments to the Title IX Regulations

The 2020 regulations fundamentally changed how colleges and universities investigate and adjudicate sexual harassment complaints. As of January 2025, those rules are once again in effect. They establish procedural protections that many students facing allegations view as essential to a fair process.

2020 Final Rule Issuance and Effective Date

In May 2020, the Department issued a comprehensive Final Rule amending its Title IX implementing regulations. The Department reviewed more than 124,000 public comments submitted during the notice-and-comment period. Following this review, the Department published new regulations that took effect on August 14, 2020.

The 2020 regulations were the first Title IX guidance to undergo formal notice-and-comment rulemaking since 1997. Unlike earlier guidance documents that lacked the force of law, these regulations became binding legal requirements for all covered institutions.

Applicable Regulatory Citation and Time Coverage

The 2020 regulations appear at 34 CFR Part 106. They governed Title IX enforcement from August 14, 2020, through July 31, 2024. The Biden administration’s 2024 regulations briefly took effect after that date. Following the January 9, 2025, court decision vacating the 2024 rules, the 2020 framework once again was the law that governs how institutions must respond to sexual harassment allegations.

2020 Amendments: Reference Materials and Supporting Documents

The Department also released extensive supporting materials aimed at helping institutions implement the 2020 regulations.

Federal Register Publication for the 2020 Final Rule

The official Final Rule appeared in the Federal Register on May 19, 2020. It was published in both HTML and PDF formats.

Core Overview and Summary Documents (2020)

Supporting materials included fact sheets and comprehensive overviews. The Department also provided comparison documents showing differences from the proposed rule.

Technical Assistance and Guidance (2020–2025)

Over the years, OCR issued multiple rounds of technical assistance. These included the Questions and Answers documents released in 2020 and 2021. They updated guidance on sexual harassment in 2021 and revised it in 2022. Additionally, a 2021 letter clarified enforcement regarding cross-examination requirements.

Most recently, OCR released a January 2025 resource document regarding online or digital sexual harassment under the 2020 regulations. The timing matters. It was issued just days after the court vacated the 2024 regulations. This signaled that OCR intended to resume active enforcement under the 2020 framework immediately.

2024 Amendments to the Title IX Regulations

The Biden administration attempted to significantly revise Title IX enforcement in 2024. These regulations never achieved stable, nationwide implementation. They were ultimately struck down by a federal court.

2024 Final Rule Issuance and Effective Date

In April 2024, the Department issued a new Final Rule. This rule substantially amended the 2020 regulations. The 2024 rules took effect on August 1, 2024. However, they faced immediate legal challenges in multiple jurisdictions.

These regulations would have substantially changed how schools addressed not only sexual harassment, but sex discrimination more broadly. They expanded definitions and altered procedural requirements. They also attempted to incorporate gender identity and sexual orientation into the protections of Title IX.

Regulatory Citation for the 2024 Regulations

The 2024 amendments also appeared at 34 CFR Part 106. However, these regulations had an extraordinarily short and tumultuous lifespan. Preliminary injunctions blocked their enforcement in 26 states before they could be fully implemented. Then, just five months after taking effect, a federal court invalidated them entirely.

January 9, 2025, Vacatur and Post-Decision Status

On January 9, 2025, a federal district court issued a decisive ruling that vacated the 2024 Final Rule in its entirety. The Department has confirmed that the 2024 Title IX regulations and related resources are not effective in any jurisdiction.

As a result, the 2024 rules have no legal force anywhere in the United States.

For students and institutions involved in disciplinary proceedings between August 1, 2024, and January 9, 2025, the situation is far less straightforward. It is often unclear which regulatory framework should have applied. Schools in the 26 states where preliminary injunctions blocked the 2024 rules continued operating under the 2020 framework. Elsewhere, institutions may have partially or fully implemented the 2024 requirements, only to see those rules invalidated mid-academic year.

Court Decision Summary: Vacatur of the 2024 Title IX Final Rule (January 9, 2025)

The January 2025 decision marked one of the most consequential Title IX rulings in years. Understanding what the court determined helps explain what comes next.

Background of the Tennessee v. Cardona Ruling

The decision stems from legal challenges against the U.S. Department of Education’s 2024 Title IX Final Rule. These challenges addressed sex-based discrimination and sexual harassment. The decisive opinion was issued on January 9, 2025. It involved multiple authors and legal teams representing various states.

The Nationwide Reach of the 2025 Vacatur

The case, State of Tennessee v. Cardona (Civil Action No. 2:24-cv-072-DCR), was decided in the U.S. District Court for the Eastern District of Kentucky. Six states challenged the rule: Kentucky, Virginia, Indiana, Ohio, Tennessee, and West Virginia. Two intervenors also joined the challenge against the Final Rule issued on April 29, 2024.

Although the case began in a single district court, the judge’s decision to vacate the regulations applies nationwide. That ruling requires schools across the country to return to the 2020 framework.

Key Takeaways: Understanding the Court’s Vacatur of the 2024 Rule

The 2024 regulations attempted several major changes to Title IX enforcement.

Expansion of Sex-Based Harassment

The 2024 Final Rule significantly broadened the definition of sex-based harassment. It sought to include discrimination based on sexual orientation, gender identity, sex stereotypes, sex characteristics, and pregnancy or related conditions. This expansion would have brought many more types of conduct under Title IX jurisdiction compared to the 2020 regulations. You can read more about what typically constitutes sexual harassment and its types here.

Change to Institutional Response Trigger

The 2024 Rule altered when schools were required to respond. The changes to the “actual knowledge” and “deliberate indifference” standards would have required institutional action in circumstances where the 2020 regulations may not have triggered the same obligations.

Litigation Landscape Prior to Vacatur

Even before the January 2025 decision, the 2024 Rule faced substantial legal opposition. Preliminary injunctions had already halted enforcement in 26 states. This created a patchwork of regulatory compliance where geography determined which rules applied. Numerous additional lawsuits challenged various aspects of the regulations.

Constitutional and Statutory Findings Stated

The court’s opinion found multiple fundamental flaws with the 2024 regulations.

Constitutional Violations Found

The court determined that the 2024 Final Rule violated both the First Amendment and the Spending Clause of the Constitution. These are serious defects that go beyond mere disagreement about policy choices.

Statutory Authority Finding

The court held that the Department exceeded its authority under Title IX when it expanded the meaning of “on the basis of sex” to include gender identity. According to the court, Title IX has traditionally prohibited discrimination based on sex as male or female. It has not historically covered gender identity. The attempt by the Department to redefine this core term represented regulatory overreach that Congress had not authorized.

Administrative Law Findings Explained

Under the Administrative Procedure Act, agency rules must not be arbitrary, capricious, or contrary to law. The court found that the 2024 Rule was both arbitrary and capricious. It was also deemed vague and overbroad in its scope.

Free Speech and Pronoun/Name Requirement Issue

One particularly contentious aspect of the 2024 regulations involved speech. The court held that requiring teachers and others to use names and pronouns associated with a student’s asserted gender identity infringed on free speech rights. This was especially problematic because noncompliance could result in harassment claims. This represented a direct collision between the regulations and First Amendment protections.

“Clear Notice” and Funding Conditions

The Spending Clause requires that Congress provide clear notice to states of the conditions attached to federal funding. The court found that the 2024 regulations failed this test. States were not given adequate notice that receiving federal education funds would require them to comply with this dramatic expansion of Title IX’s scope.

Reasoned Explanation and Consistency Concerns

Agencies must provide a reasoned explanation when they depart from longstanding interpretations of the laws they enforce. Here, the court concluded that the Department failed to explain why it was abandoning decades of Title IX interpretation. Moreover, the court noted that the regulations created “glaring inconsistencies” within Title IX itself. Title IX allows sex-based separation in various circumstances like athletics, bathrooms, and housing.

Summary Judgment Outcomes

Both sides moved for summary judgment. The court granted summary judgment to the six plaintiff states and two intervenors. It denied the motions filed by Secretary of Education Miguel Cardona and the Department of Education. This meant the plaintiffs won on the merits without the need for a trial.

Treatment of Bostock v. Clayton County

The Department had relied heavily on the Supreme Court’s 2020 decision in Bostock v. Clayton County. That decision held that Title VII workplace discrimination protections extend to sexual orientation and gender identity. The district court rejected this analogy. It found that Title VII has different language, goals, and defenses than Title IX. Title IX explicitly allows sex-based separation in various contexts, while Title VII does not. The court also noted that the 2024 regulations conspicuously avoided addressing transgender participation in sports. This highlighted internal tensions within the Department’s approach.

Scope of Vacatur and Declaratory Relief

The court vacated the entire 2024 Final Rule and all corresponding regulations nationwide. Rather than attempting to sever problematic provisions, the judge found that the challenged elements so thoroughly permeated the regulations that the entire framework must be struck down.

The court also granted declaratory relief. It stated that the plaintiff states, their subdivisions, and recipient schools need not comply with the 2024 Rule to receive federal funding. However, this does not mean states are automatically entitled to funding regardless of their actions. They could still violate Title IX in other ways unrelated to the 2024 Rule.

Returning to the 2020 Enforcement Standards and Enforcement Baseline

In the immediate aftermath of the January 9 decision, the Department had not issued detailed guidance on implementation. However, it quickly became clear that the 2020 Title IX Rule would serve as the enforcement baseline going forward. OCR began issuing new guidance documents specifically referencing the 2020 regulations and their requirements.

The Current Landscape of Federal Civil Rights Enforcement

Civil rights compliance extends beyond Title IX. Recent developments show that multiple areas of education law are now under heightened scrutiny, with policies shifting quickly.

DOE Withdraws Proposed Title IX Regulation Change (Sports Tryouts)

In September 2025, the Department withdrew a proposed change to its sports tryout requirements. The proposal would have eliminated the obligation for DOE‑funded institutions to offer noncontact sports tryouts to students of an excluded gender when no same‑gender team exists. Under the proposal, for example, a school would not have been required to permit a female student to try out for a boys’ baseball team if the school did not offer a girls softball team, a circumstance that commonly arises in rural districts.

The proposal drew substantial public comment and raised legal concerns. This prompted the Department to withdraw it. The Department noted it might revisit similar proposals in the future. For now, existing requirements remain in place. This topic illustrates how even seemingly straightforward regulatory adjustments can trigger controversy and force agencies to reconsider.

ED MSI Grant Funding Restructuring (Race-Based Criteria)

In 2026, the U.S. Department of Education announced it will end funding for discretionary grant programs at Minority-Serving Institutions that use race-based eligibility criteria. The decision follows Supreme Court precedent and a Department of Justice determination that such programs are unconstitutional. Institutions may continue to receive funding only if programs are redesigned using race-neutral criteria, such as socioeconomic or first-generation status, marking a significant shift in federal higher education funding policy.

OCR Resolution Agreements Through September 12

OCR continued actively resolving civil rights cases across multiple categories through September 2025. New resolution agreements addressed several key areas:

  • Age discrimination
  • FAPE (Free Appropriate Public Education) issues involving IEPs or Section 504 plans
  • Title IX procedural compliance failures
  • Single-sex scholarships that may violate nondiscrimination requirements
  • Disability discrimination involving procedural, facilities, accessibility, and accommodation failures

These resolutions serve as warning examples for educational institutions. Single-sex scholarships, Title IX procedure gaps, and special education compliance issues are receiving particular OCR scrutiny.

Implications for Institutions

Taken together, these developments point in a consistent direction. The Department and OCR are addressing both new regulatory changes and ongoing enforcement across multiple civil rights areas simultaneously. Age, sex, race, and disability discrimination are all under active investigation.

Areas under heightened scrutiny include:

  • Procedural obligations in complaint investigation and resolution
  • Accommodations for students with disabilities
  • Access to programs and facilities
  • General nondiscrimination compliance

 “Things to Watch” List

Institutions would be well advised to keep an eye on several developing areas:

  1. Legislative responses: States may pass laws in response to federal funding restructuring efforts, particularly around MSI programs.
  2. OCR public reporting: Pay attention to the timeliness and detail of released resolutions. Watch which cases receive press releases or result in new guidance documents.
  3. Replacement metrics: As race and ethnicity thresholds disappear, watch what alternative metrics emerge.
  4. Institutional policy reviews: Schools should proactively review policies around disability accommodation, Title IX procedural compliance, and single-sex programs. These must align with current OCR enforcement trends.

The message for this year and beyond is that civil rights enforcement involves not just changing funding or regulatory schemes. It also involves interpreting and enforcing existing laws in ways that may shift rapidly.

New York Legislative Development: Title VI Coordinator Requirement

While Title IX receives most of the attention, recent state-level action signals that Title VI enforcement around race, color, and national origin discrimination may be intensifying. It is important to understand which forms of harassment and discrimination Title IX does not protect against, which often fall under Title VI.

New Requirement

In August 2025, New York became the first state to require every college and university to appoint a dedicated Title VI Coordinator. This groundbreaking legislation goes beyond current federal requirements, which does not mandate such a position.

The law applies to all institutions covered by New York Education Law Article 129-A. This encompasses more than 200 campuses including SUNY, CUNY, and other regents-chartered colleges and universities.

Potential Broader Adoption

New York’s approach may serve as a model for other states. There is an increased federal focus on race, color, and national origin discrimination in education. This is particularly true following campus incidents related to antisemitism and Islamophobia. More jurisdictions may follow New York’s lead.

Growing Emphasis Area

Schools, districts, and institutions should prepare for heightened compliance expectations around Title VI. This includes:

  • Appointing a knowledgeable coordinator (whether required by law or not)
  • Training leadership and staff on Title VI obligations
  • Reviewing nondiscrimination policies
  • Clarifying complaint processes

Outsourcing Consideration

Many institutions outsource Title IX responsibilities to specialized firms. If your school does this, ensure that compliance partners can also support Title VI obligations. The role parallels Title IX coordination in many ways. Integrated support may provide efficiency and consistency.

Which Rules Apply?

The answer depends on when the alleged conduct occurred and when the institution initiated its investigation.

For alleged misconduct occurring and investigations beginning after January 9, 2025, the 2020 regulations clearly apply.

For cases initiated between August 1, 2024, and January 9, 2025, the situation is more complex. Schools in the 26 states where the 2024 regulations were enjoined never implemented those rules. They continued using the 2020 framework. If your child attends a school in one of those states, the regulatory framework likely remains consistent.

However, institutions in other states may have begun following the 2024 requirements. Those requirements are now invalid. This creates potential procedural issues that may affect pending cases.

Procedural Protections Under the 2020 Regulations

The 2020 regulations provide significant procedural protections for accused students:

  • Live hearings: Institutions must provide live hearings where both parties can participate.
  • Cross-examination: Advisors (who may be attorneys) can cross-examine the other party and witnesses.
  • Standard of evidence: Schools may use either a preponderance of the evidence or clear and convincing evidence standard. However, they must apply the same standard for student and employee cases.
  • Access to evidence: Both parties must receive equal access to all evidence directly related to the allegations.

These safeguards reflect the reality that Title IX cases can carry lasting consequences for students. These consequences include suspension, expulsion, and permanent notations on transcripts.

The Path Forward: What to Expect in 2026 and Beyond

Title IX enforcement will continue to evolve. Certain trends appear likely.

The 2020 regulations are expected to remain in effect for the foreseeable future. The current administration has confirmed that it will enforce these rules. Any future regulatory changes would require a lengthy notice-and-comment rulemaking process.

Litigation will most likely continue in areas involving transgender student rights, athletic participation, and facility access. Court decisions in these specific contexts may shape how the Department interprets and enforces the 2020 regulations.

States are also passing their own laws related to sex discrimination and civil rights in education. This creates additional compliance obligations beyond federal requirements. New York’s Title VI coordinator requirement exemplifies this trend.

OCR has demonstrated active enforcement across multiple civil rights statutes simultaneously. Schools face investigations for Title IX violations, disability discrimination, Title VI issues, age discrimination, and other areas. This multi-front enforcement approach means institutions must maintain robust compliance programs across their entire civil rights portfolio.
