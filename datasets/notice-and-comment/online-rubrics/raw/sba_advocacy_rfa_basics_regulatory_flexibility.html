A Condensed Guide to the Regulatory Flexibility Act

RFA Basics

Learn how the RFA gives small entities a voice in federal rulemaking in our guide.

RFA in a Nutshell »

Why It Matters to You

Small businesses are the backbone of the U.S. economy, but federal regulations can hit them harder than large firms. The Regulatory Flexibility Act (RFA), strengthened by Executive Order 13272, ensures that federal agencies recognize that difference. This primer equips business owners, policymakers, and agency staff with the essential tools to understand how the RFA works, and how it helps prevent “one-size-fits-all” rules from overwhelming small enterprises.

How the RFA Works

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the RFA?

The Regulatory Flexibility Act (RFA) is a U.S. federal law enacted in 1980 that aims to minimize the regulatory burden placed on small businesses by federal agencies. For a more in-depth look into the RFA, visit our Regulatory Flexibility Act page to learn more.

How can a small business challenge a rule?

You can challenge regulations through the Office of Advocacy by providing feedback, participating in roundtables, and utilizing the Red Tape Hotline. The Office of Advocacy also assists with regulatory flexibility analysis and can help small businesses challenge rules through judicial review if they are significantly impacted.

Terms & Definitions

Initial Regulatory Flexibility Analysis (IRFA) — Identifies small-entity impact, considers alternatives.

Public Comment — Agency solicits public comment.

Final Regulatory Flexibility Analysis (FRFA) — Weighs feedback, justifies final choices.

Final Rule — The agency, after considering public comments on a proposed rule (NPRM), establishes the final regulatory language, including any necessary revisions and their effective date. It also includes a final regulatory flexibility analysis (FRFA) if an initial analysis was required.

Judicial Review — Small businesses can sue under SBREFA for noncompliance.

Stay Connected            

The Office of Advocacy supports businesses with fewer than 500 employees, non-profit organizations, and independent contractors. Our mission is to help small entities as regulations are developed in Washington.

We are the independent voice for small businesses in the executive branch. We do not take part in SBA’s work on loans, disaster relief, or procurement.
