Dear [Name],

I hope this finds you well. I'm writing from Stanford, where I'm a postdoctoral researcher working on a computational study of how institutions make evaluative decisions, and I wanted to reach out about a possible research collaboration with CSR.

The broader project I'm working on investigates what I've been calling the "articulability gap": the degree to which the quality signals underlying institutional decisions can be made explicit versus are only recoverable implicitly. Different decision-making institutions sit at very different points along this spectrum, and I'm studying several of them in parallel — peer review of academic papers, editorial judgments in journalism, and grant funding decisions. NIH grant review is, for my purposes, one of the most compelling cases. The evaluation rubric is unusually well-articulated (Significance, Investigators, Innovation, Approach, Environment; the 1–9 impact scale; the published criteria for each bin), and yet the empirical literature on competitive-range reviewer agreement — most strikingly Pier et al., "Low agreement among reviewers evaluating the same NIH grant applications" (PNAS 2018, https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.1714379115) — suggests that formal articulation alone does not close the gap. That tension is exactly what makes NIH such an interesting system to study: the rubric is explicit, the outcomes are consequential, and the residual variance is informative rather than noise to be washed out.

What I would love to do, concretely, is use the public RePORTER abstracts of funded R01 applications together with their overall impact scores to ask how much of the score is predictable from the abstract text alone. The answer places an empirical bound on how much of a review outcome lies in the articulated content of the proposal versus in reviewer-specific or panel-specific variance — a complement to Fang, Bowen & Casadevall, "NIH peer review percentile scores are poorly predictive of grant productivity" (eLife 2016, https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.13323), but approached from the text side rather than the productivity side. A natural second question falls out of the resubmission structure that RePORTER already exposes through the SUFFIX field: what distinguishes the ~166,000 abstracts (FY1985–FY2024) that were funded only on A1 or A2 from those funded on A0? We already have the text for both groups; what we don't have is the score.

I realize I'm describing exactly the kind of work that has historically happened through CSR collaborations rather than through open data releases — the Li & Agha dataset of 130,000+ NIH grants ("Big names or big ideas: Do peer-review panels select the best science proposals?", Science 2015, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aaa0185), the Fang et al. re-analysis cited above, Ginther et al. on award probabilities ("Race, Ethnicity, and NIH Research Awards", Science 2011, https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1196783), and the Erosheva/Martinková/Nakamura redaction experiment ("An experimental test of the effects of redacting grant applicant identifiers on peer review outcomes", eLife 2021, https://doi.org/10.7554/eLife.71368) — and I wanted to approach you in that spirit. The data I would need is a strict subset of what has been shared in those prior collaborations: for each funded research project grant in FY1985–FY2024, the APPLICATION_ID (already public in RePORTER) linked to its overall impact score and percentile rank. I would not need any PI identifiers, summary statement text, criterion-level or reviewer-level scores, or any information about unfunded applications.

If there are standard confidentiality or IRB arrangements you prefer, I'm happy to adopt them.
