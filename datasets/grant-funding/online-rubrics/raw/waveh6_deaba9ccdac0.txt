ABSTRACT 

A handbook of foundation grants information is 
provided. Today, there are nearly 25,000 foundations that Support a 
wide variety of activities. On the average, foundations give 
educational institutions 37% of each grant dollar for requests 
ranging from capital support to program development and research. 
Because every foundation is unique, it is important that the 
prospective applicant or campus sponsored programs or grants officer 
complete several steps before presenting an idea to the foundation or 
writing a formal proposal. Four sections focus on the following: what 
foundations support (independent foundations, company/corporate 
sponsored foundations, community foundations, and operating 
foundations); how to identify potential foundations (exploring the 
possibilities and narrowing the list); coordination and commitment; 
and presenting the idea (initial contact and formal proposals). Even 
with careful preparation, not all proposals submitted to foundations 
will be funded. If a proposal is turned down, another try is 
suggested (first finding out why the proposal was not funded and 
asking for ideas on how to improve the presentation of the idea). Two 
appendices are: suggested resources (directories, grants listings, 
and corporate giving) and the Foundation Center Cooperating 
Collections Network (listed by state). (SM)

Today, there are nearly 25,000 foundations that sup- 
port a wide variety of activities. On the average, founda- 
tions give educational institutions 37 percent of each 
grant dollar for requests ranging from capital support to 
program development and research. 

The Foundation Center defines a foundation as "a 
nongovernmental, non-profit organization having a 
principal fund of its own, managed by its own trustees 
and directors, and established to maintain or aid charita- 
ble, educational, religious, or other activities serving the 
public good, primarily by making grants to other non- 
profit organizations.” In addition to these organizations, 
legally defined and practicing as foundations, there are 


-also other organizations that use the words foundation, 


endowment, or trust in their names but do not meet the 
legal definition and may not have charitable aims as 
their objective. 

Another area that reveals the diversity of the founda- 
tion sector is the structure of day-to-day operations: 
foundation grant-making activities can be controlled by 
bank trusts, directed by volunteer boards, or run by pro- 
fessional staffs. Foundation staffing patterns will affect 
the way in which applicant interaction and proposal re- 
view are handled. Large national foundations have pro- 
fessional staff members, often assigned to different pro- 
gram areas or foundation interests, who are available to 
counsel applicants in the grant preparation process and 
who play an important role in the shaping of final pro- 
posals. The vast majority of the foundations, however, 
operate without paid staff, and advance contact is limit- 
ed or nonexistent. 

Most foundations follow procedures that are far less 
structured and uniform than those of federal agencies, 
which must operate under well-defined congressional 
mandates and federal regulations. Unlike federal agen- 
cies, foundations are still free to change program direc- 
tion at any time. They are not, however, entirely free 
from regulation, for they must follow certain guidelines 
set forth in the Internal Revenue Code. Because of their 
tax-exempt status under Section 501(c)3 of the Code, 
foundations are subject to special regulations and taxes, 
e.g., the requirement to pay out in grants at least 5 per- 
cent of the organization's annual investment income 
and to pay a quarterly excise tax on net investment in- 
come. To maintain their tax-exempt status, foundations 
must remain within these defined boundaries of 
practice. 


Every foundation is unique; therefore, a prospective

What Foundations Support 

Before beginning a search for private sponsors you 
should ask, “Is this project appropriate for foundation 
funding?” Demands for the limited resources of the pri 
vate sector make it essential that foundations leverage 
their money to have the greatest impact. Despite the 
diversity of organization, several general rules for foun 
dation funding exist. Most foundations will only fund 
special projects or provide start-up support for activities 


that will become self-sustaining after a short period of 


time. They prefer to fund projects that are on the “cut- 
ting edge,” representing novel approaches to identified 
social problems or needs. In addition, foundations sel- 
dom support projects that can be funded easily through 
state and federal mechanisms. 

Within these general guidelines, each type of founda- 

tion also has specific funding patterns: 
8 Independent Foundations are established under 
broad charters that mandate support of social, educa- 
tional, religious, and other charitable activities. Actual 
giving patterns usually reflect the interests of the 
founder of the endowment. Based on the scope of their 
giving, independent foundations may be further catego- 
rized as local private foundations or national 
foundations. 

Local Foundations account for over 70 percent of 
giving by independent foundations. As indicated by 
their category name, these foundations primarily fund 
projects that benefit the foundation's immediate com- 
munity or a surrounding region. 

National Foundations often develop specific grant 
and fellowship programs in addition to giving across 
broad charter areas, Larger foundations are more willing 
to take a risk to support innovative projects that have a 
demonstrable impact and can be repeated with the 
same results in other communities. 


8 Company/Corporate-Sponsored Foundations 
are established and organized as entities independent of 
the parent company, with separate boards of directors 


These foundations maintain close ties with the company 
by funding areas related to corporate activities. They 
also give money to organizations located near company 
operations and fund projects that will improve the com- 
munities where the company workers live. In most 
cases, foundation grant programs are administered inde- 
pendently of employee matching gift and scholarship 
programs. 

®Community Foundations receive funds from 
many donors, rather than from an endowment estab- 
lished by a single donor, and are therefore also known as 
public foundations. Community foundations support 
projects that will meet the needs of or improve the com- 
munity. They often provide capital support and award 
scholarships and fellowships that promote access to 
higher education for local high school students. 


® Operating Foundations, like independent founda: 
tions, are supported by endowment income but use 
their resources to conduct inhouse research or provide 
a direct service in specific areas of interest. Few, if any, 
external grants are made, and these usually supplement 
the foundation's ongoing program 


How to Identify Potential Foundations 


Exploring the Possibilities 

Once you are sure the proposed project is appropri- 
ate for private funding, you can begin a search for spon- 
sors. First, develop a list of foundations that have dem- 
onstrated an interest in funding the project area or field 
and that support organizations in your geographical re- 
gion. The campus library and sponsored programs or 
grants office will have copies of foundation directories 
with subject and geographical indices that can be used 
to make this initial list. The most common directories 
are The Foundation Directory, Source Book Profiles 
(both publications of the Foundation Center ), and the 
Taft Foundation Reporter. Each of these publications 
provides a different level of detail, as described in the 
Suggested Resources section (Appendix A). 

Read the descriptions in the directories for all of the 
foundations on your list, and ask yourself the following 
questions: 
®@ Do the goals of the project match the foundation's 

priorities? 
® Does the foundation give to public institutions of high- 
er education? 


®Does the foundation support projects in this geo- 
graphical area? 

®Does the foundation support this type of project, 
whether it is research, demonstration, or service 
related? 

®@ Does the foundation provide the type of funding re- 
quired, i.e. seed money, endowment, construction, re- 
search, training? 

@ Is the foundation's grant range and period of support 
in line with the project’s budget request? 

If your answers and a foundation description do not 
parallel each other, eliminate that foundation from the 
list: leaving questionable sources on the list will in- 
crease your level of effort and decrease the likelihood of 
success. While this culling process is time consuming, it 
will increase your familiarity with potential foundations 
as well as help you identify sources for other projects. 


Narrowing the List 

Because foundation priorities are often listed in broad 
terms such as “education” or “arts,” you should review 
lists of the grants actually made by foundations. Small 
sample lists are provided in the Source Book Profiles 
and the Taft Foundation Reporter. The Foundation 
Grants Index, a publication of the The Foundation Cen- 
ter, contains a comprehensive list of grants exceeding 
$5,000 made by foundations, and a more detailed index 
than that in other directories. By reviewing the listings 
you can identify a pattern for the foundation's true prior- 
ities; a foundation that cites a national giving pattern 
may actually concentrate its grants in a particular region 
or a foundation that supports education may actually 
only fund endowment requests. Remove from the list 
any foundation whose funding pattern does not match 
your needs. 

Once you have narrowed the number of your poten- 
tial sources in this manner, try to find out as much as 
you can about the foundations remaining on the list. The 
directories will indicate whether the foundations pub- 
lish descriptive materials such as annual reports, appli- 
cation guidelines, or policy statements, which you 
should request, if available. Because the information in 
directories tends to be two to three years old, materials 
requested directly from the foundations will provide 
the most recent information on funding priorities and 
application guidelines. A public relations or communi- 
cations officer is a good contact for current information; 


program officers are frequently too busy for this type of 
call. The public relations officer will also be able to send 
you other useful publications you may need before 
applying. 

Many foundations do not publish annual reports and 
application materials. If this is the case, contact the near- 
est Foundation Center Cooperative Reference Collec- 
tion (see Appendix B) to find more information. A com- 
plete reference collection will contain standard 
foundation directories published by the Foundation 
Center, copies of the IRS tax return Form 990-PF for 
local foundations, and information, such as newspaper 
articles about local foundations and the philanthropic 
sector, not generally found in campus libraries 

A final way to narrow the list is by talking to people on 
your campus or in your field who have worked with the 
foundation in the past and can provide insight into its 
priorities. 


Coordination and Commitment 


Before sending a concept paper or proposal to the 
foundations on your list, increase your potential for suc- 
cess by coordinating the submission with other offices 
on campus and securing institutional commitment from 
the university's chief executive officer or proxy. 

You should work with your campus-sponsored pro- 
grams or grants officer, who will coordinate with other 
campus Offices that seek external funding to determine 
whether the university already has a proposal pending 
with the foundation. If there is no pending proposal, the 
grants officer will notify the other offices of your sub- 
mission. This coordination ensures you that the univer- 
sity will not further solicit the foundation while your 
proposal is active. 

Interoffice communication can also be an integral 
part of obtaining institutional commitment for your pro- 
ject. Most sponsored projects demand the allocation of 
institutional resources, from overhead and equipment 
to the services of personnel, and in order to allocate 
these resources effectively, the institution needs to be 
aware of all such demands on its resource pool. Coordi- 
nation makes this information available to the adminis- 
trators approving projects and allocating resources, 
who can then guarantee that you will have the needed 
institutional support when the project is funded 


6 


Foundations review the applicant institutions as well 
as the structure and objectives of the proposed projects, 
and therefore, the absence or presence of coordination 
and commitment has definite consequences for the 
funding decision. First, most foundations will not accept 
a proposal without the endorsement of a senior univer- 
sity official and commitment of institutional resources. 
In addition, foundations consider the absence of coordi: 
nation to be an indication of potentially poor project 
management and may reject all proposals from the insti- 
tution. On the other hand, the presence of coordination 
and commitment will increase the likelihood that your 
project will be funded because the foundation will be 
assured that adequate institutional resources will be 
available to support the project 


Presenting the Idea 


Initial Contact 


Because of the volume of requests that foundations 
must review with limited staff, many resort to an infor 
mal, two-stage process for considering grant proposals 
They first request that you submit a brief letter of inqui 
ry (usually no longer than two to three pages ) describ. 
ing the project, the plan of action, the background of 
key personnel, the tentative budget, and the purpose of 
the organization. The first paragraph should contain a 
brief description of the project and the amount of mon. 
ey is being requested—not a description of the organi: 
zation, which should appear in the discussion of facili- 
ties and resources available to complete the project. 

This letter should demonstrate that you are acquaint- 
ed with the work and purposes of the foundation and 
see a clear connection between these and the proposed 
project. An effective letter will outline the significance 
of the project and who it will benefit, and provide an 
indication of step-by-step planning to show that it has 
been thought out. If the foundation is interested after 
this preliminary review, it will request further informa- 
tion in the form of a full proposal and may offer sugges- 
tions on how to strengthen the presentation of the idea. 

It is frequently appropriate to send a letter of inquiry 
to many foundations, but a mass mailing can damage 
your credibility. If, after having done the necessary re- 
search, you decide to solicit several different founda- 
tions, let the program officers know that you have done 
this. The program officers appreciate this information 


and, if they are interested in seeing a project funded but 
cannot afford to support it entirely, may try to arrange 
for joint funding among several foundations 


Formal Proposals 

In reviewing proposals foundation program officers 
first determine whether the project fits with the founda- 
tion's specific priorities, and the match between the ap- 
plicant's needs and the giver's priorities is therefore cru- 
cial to the success of the proposal. Other questions they 
are likely to ask include: 

@ Will the project make a difference? 

® Does the project tackle too much? 

@ Is the budget request sound? 

@ Is there institutional commitment? 

@ Is the evaluation plan sound? 

In essence, the proposed project must make sense, be 
well thought out, be clear in its purpose, and be unique. 
When applying to a foundation, always follow the format 
prescribed in its proposal guidelines. However, if the 
foundation does not distribute specific guidelines, re- 
member that program officers appreciate brevity: pre- 
pare a formal proposal that is approximately ten pages in 
length and includes: 

@a summary letter or one-page precis of the project, 
stating the purpose, amount requested, and expected 
time period of the grant; 

® a description of your institution and the signature of 
an authorized official at the institution; 

@the body of the proposal, containing a rationale or 
needs statement, description of the target population, 
four or five measurable objectives, implementation 
schedule, and plans for evaluation; 

® a description of the qualifications of the personnel in- 
volved in the project; 

® proof of the university's tax-exempt status, as required 
by the Internal Revenue Code; and 

@ a project budget that includes future funding plans if it 
is a multiyear project. 

Foundations leverage their money to achieve the 
greatest possible impact and, in doing so, recognize that 
reimbursing indirect costs reduces the number of 
grants that can be made. In 1984 foundations awarded 
approximately $5 billion in grants, a small amount com- 
pared with federal government outlays for grants and 


contracts. If foundations reimbursed indirect costs at 
the same rate as that applied to government contracts, 
they would have only awarded $2.0 to $3.5 billion in 
direct project costs, rather than $5 billion. As a result, 
they have developed a variety of positions on the 
allowability of indirect costs or overhead. Many founda 
tions do not support any indirect costs because they 
consider these costs to be a part of the institution's com 
mitment to the project. Few, if any, foundations will al 
low the full government overhead rate on a grant, but 
some foundations will provide a portion of the indirect 
cost rate. For example, an institution can apply its full 
federal rate to the proposed budget and then agree to 
cover all but 5-15 percent of those costs. Or, because 
foundations do not follow the same set of regulations as 
that used by the federal government, you can break 
down the items usually included in the indirect cost 
rate—such as rent, use of office equipment or salary for 
administrative personnel—and include some of these as 
direct cost line items and others as indirect in your 
budget. 

Most foundations do not include information on their 
indirect cost policies in their publications. However, 
there are several ways to find out about a particular 
foundation's policy. If you have developed working rela- 
tionships with foundation program officers, they will 
be able to tell you the best way to handle indirect costs 
in your budget. If the foundation does not have a staff, 
try talking to colleagues who have worked with the 
foundation before. The sponsored programs and devel- 
opment offices on campus may also be aware of a foun- 
dation's policy from past experiences. Finally, because 
the budget in the letter of inquiry is tentative, you can 
leave the question of indirect costs off at that phase of 
the process. If the foundation requests a formal propos- 
al, you will be given a contact to work with during this 
submission and that person can outline the policy. 

No matter how you decide to address the indirect 
cost issue, your institution's administration will need to 
be kept informed, because failure to recover full indi- 
rect costs will be a draw on the institution's resources. 
In addition, your proposal will be stronger if the budget 
contains a substantial level of direct cost sharing by the 
university and other groups from the start. 

Finally, grant decisions are often tied to board meet- 
ings, which can be held as infrequently as once or twice 


~] 


a year, Foundations need to receive applications at least 
two to three months in advance of board meetings to 
allow time for review and investigation. Most descrip- 
tions of foundations in the directories list what months, 
or at least how many times a year, the board meets, and 
you should take this schedule into account in planning 
the submission of your proposal 


Final Thoughts 


Even with carefui preparation, not all proposals sub- 
mitted to foundations will be funded. If you are fortu- 
nate enough to succeed on your first attempt, notify the 
appropriate offices at your institution so that all adminis 
trative requirements can be met before you begin work 
On the other hand, if a proposal is turned down, do not 
give up! Try to find out why the proposal was not fund. 
ed and ask for suggestions on how to improve the pre- 
sentation of the idea. The size of the foundation staff will 
determine the extent of the debriefing on your propos. 
al; however, any information you receive will help in 
revising the proposal for resubmission. Do not resubmit 
the proposal without making substantive changes ac- 
cording to the foundation's concerns. Successful pro- 
posals are usually the result of multiple submissions. 

Finally, your chances for success in the search for pri- 
vate funding will be enhanced if you start the search 
long before you actually need the money. Take the time 
to research thoroughly your potential sponsors and to 
develop concise and informative letters of inquiry and 
proposals, and allow enough time for the submission of 
a revised proposal.
