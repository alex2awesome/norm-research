ABSTRACT 

Step-by-step instructions on how to write a proposal 
for funding a program are given in this booklet. While many of the 
examples are related to the needs of arts educators, the information 
and procedures can be adapted to other situations. In a workbook 
approach, 36 questions are posed to clarify objectives and needs, 
including planning a budget, determining staff and facilities, and 


evaluating and disseminating of program results. Also offered are 
suggested techniques for identifying appropriate funding sources and 
approaching foundations, businesses, and government sources. A 
selected bibliography of publications on fund raising is appended as 
well as a list of eight elements of a good proposal, e sample budget, 
and a test to score proposal effectiveness. (JD)

FOREWORD 


In our two years as Arts Education consultants for the 
Educational Improvement Center (Central)--working constantly with 
thousands of New Jersev arts educators and friends of arts 
education in the central region and all over the state--we have 
had repeated requests for help in the writing of grants proposals 
in the arts. It is therefore a great pleasure for us to be able 
to provide you with this direct response: a Funding Booklet which 
is specifically designed to take the fear out of proposal writ- 
ing, and to give you some skills and techniques that can help you 
to bring your best ideas for arts programs to a lively reality. 
We know that it is what you need, and we know you can use it 
effectively. 


While this booklet was designed with arts educators in mind, 
it has been written in such a way that it will prove useful to 
anyone who needs to write a proposal. It gives step-by-step 
instructions on how to go about writing a proposal, any proposal. 
Some of the examples given are directed toward the arts; however, 
they are only examples. The actual information is general and 
(or can be helpful to everyone) can be used by everyone; and we 
hope it will be. 


While it may be true that educators, artists and arts 
educators don't set out to be writers, we live in a day and age 


when it is absolutely critical for you to develop certain 
survival skills. Proposal writing is high on that essential 
list. The goal of this booklet is to encourage you to feel 
positive about your ideas, and comfortable about the whole

THINKING IT THROUGH 


The elements of a proposal are always 
the same, no matter how large or small the 
amount of money you are requesting and no 
matter whom you are addressing. The format of 
the proposal will vary considerably, as will 
its length. Some foundations want very brief 
outlines accompanying a letter; some federal 
agencies have specific guidelines that must be 
followed exactly; a local business may well 
prefer to have you make a verbal presentation 
in person along with a written summary. 


But the foundation of every proposal is 
your own thought process: 


* the determination of exactly what it is you want 
to do 


* why you or your group are the appropriate people 
to do it 


* the specific advantages that will come from doing 
it, both for you and for the source of your funds

EIGHT ELEMENTS OF A GOOD PROPOSAL 


l. Summary. Write this last but make sure it is the 
first thing read. Include it in the cover letter with 
your proposal, at the start of a letter of application, 
or on the first page of an application form. Some 
foundations and agencies prescreen applicants on the 
basis of the summary. 


2. Introduction. This is your opportunity to describe 
how your organization's purpose and activities fit in 
with the philosophy of the funding source. You must 
also establish credibility as a viable, worthy group. 
To back up your statements, include quotations from 
newspaper articles about your work, letters of support 
or statements of endorsement. 


3. Statement of Problem (Needs Assessment). In this 
section, convince the reader that the problem or need 
you plan to address is important. Use persuasive facts 
and figures to document your statements, but don't weigh 
this section down with graphs and charts. Save them for 
an appendix. 


Define the limits of your project. Spell out all 
the areas you plan to deal with, but be careful not to 
overreach your organization's capabilities. 


4. Objectives. Often confused with methods, objectives 
are the specific results you expect from your program. 
Pick a particular target you believe you can meet. 
Avoid vague statements. 


5. Methods. Describe the various activities in your 
program and how they will help you reach your 
objectives. Include a timetable for each phase of the 
program. Demonstrate familiarity with alternative 
methods; explain why yours are better. 


6. Evaluation. Detail just how you will measure the 
success of your program. Include a design for monitoring 
and reappraisal of methods and results. A strict system 


for evaluation and feedback will go a long way toward 
convincing a funding source of your accountability. 


7. Future and Additional Funding. Show that you have 
made plans for your program's future support; name other 
sources you have approached to secure funding for the 
proposed project, such as the PTA, business community, 


etc. 


8. Budget. Break down projected costs of all aspects of 
the project, including those to be funded from other 
Sources. Requirements for reporting budgets vary widely, 


“47 


but the following should be included: 


wages and salaries, employee benefits, fees and 
contractual services, rentals of space and equipment, 
travel expenses, telephone and postage, insurance, 
printing and anything else applicable. 


The trick to proposal writing is holding the reader's 
attention. Write simply and avoid jargon. Keep each 
section as short and succinct as possible. Put as much 
information as you can into appendices so that your 
reader can learn all the vital information quickly, with 
the option of finding out more by reading on.
