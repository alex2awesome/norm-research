The goal posts aren’t moving, you are just shooting at the wrong target

drugmonkey April 12, 2024 Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism / NIH funding / Peer Review

I addressed the limitations to assuming that the review of your NIH grant proposal’s Approach drives your proposal’s outcome in review, in a recent post. My central thesis in this is that the strength and weakness bullet points that are written by the assigned reviewers are an exercise in confirming an overall impact position. This position can be a more implicit impression, it may derive in some arcane way from the reviewer’s consideration of your Approach. But the overall position of a reviewer on a given proposal is most usually not some sort of post-hoc summary of a careful objective review of all strengths and weaknesses in the Approach.

Which is why scores do not consistently improve once the PI has “fixed” the grant by addressing the weaknesses identified in the Approach. This can be true for an amended version (A1) version of the application. It can be for yet another amended version under new-grant guise. It can be for other unrelated proposals in which the PI’s growing grantsmithing skills, honed by disappointing reviews, have headed off the weakness critique(s).

Misunderstanding of this leads to much PI annoyance and hair tearing about the alleged moving goalposts of NIH grant review.

A recent Tweet complains about the apparent moving goalposts of a specific grant criticism as follows.

In the event Elongated Muskrat eventually blows up the microblog platform still known as Twitter, it reads:

Evolution of grant reviewer comments re: data & pubs “But the data is not published” …publishes a Pubmed indexed preprint… “But the data is only a preprint, not a peer-reviewed journal” …publishes in peer-reviewer journal… “But the data is published in a low IF journal”

Now, this particular comment could have been a weakness in Significance (is this a real effect?) or Investigator (productivity!) or Approach, i.e., in the realm of preliminary data. It doesn’t matter much which category is involved, the principle is the same.

It is entirely fair and consistent with a long history of NIH study section behavior to view the inclusion of relevant data that are published as a strength and the lack thereof as a weakness. It is fair and consistent to laud the provision of supportive preliminary data and criticize the lack of evidence. It is fair and consistent to laud “better” data and criticize worse data. Where all of these subjective decision points are entirely in the hands of the reviewer, only to be partially modified by the views of other reviewers. The arguments over such differences of opinion are a legitimate, fair and long accepted part of the review process.

In this case, the tweeter expresses three points of contention. Published versus not-published data. Pre-print deposition versus traditional peer review publication acceptance. Low versus high journal impact factor.

Again, it is a completely legitimate part of the NIH grant review process for individual reviewers to express the belief that published data are superior strengths, that peer reviewed publication is a superior strength and that higher JIF journal publication is a superior strength of a proposal.

You may disagree any of these are relevant to the assessment of a proposal. I may disagree. You or I may agree or disagree with any given other reviewer about the degree to which such things should contribute to the overall impact score recommendation of a given reviewer. But recognize that we are talking about agreement and disagreement that is entirely within the legitimate, accepted and workaday function of NIH’s grant review study sections.

These sorts of Stock Critiques, if you will, make up the language of review. They are convenient accepted shorthands for a reviewer telling the panel a grant sucks and they should vote accordingly. They have the benefit of being objectively verifiable. There are, or are not, preliminary data included. The cited work is either in a peer reviewed journal or not. The publication is in Journal X with a JIF of x’ and not in Journal Y with a JIF of y’. It is by comparison much harder to swallow a claim that the Bunny Hopper model is superior to the Squirrel Jumper model on objective, immediately verifiable grounds.

As I was saying in the previous post, any competent reviewer can deploy these and other conventional, fair, accepted, workaday critiques of a grant application to justify an overall impact score of ANY grant that is going to put it outside of the money. The only way that a grant gets excellent overall impact recommendations from the assigned reviewers is that the reviewers have CHOSEN to overlook the obvious flaws.

A reviewer can CHOOSE to overlook a lack of preliminary data for anything, and to argue to any other reviewers at length as to why it is not needed for this to be an excellent proposal. The reviewer can CHOOSE to say “well this panel is the key thing and whether it is in a manuscript, preprint or pub makes no difference to the support for the excellence of this proposal“. They can do the same for a preprint and make all of the usual scathing remarks about how peer review publication is meaningless. Ditto JIF quality proxy arguments. They can CHOOSE.

The goal posts haven’t moved. The goal post is still demarcated by getting the three assigned reviewers on board with your proposal in a very general and broad way. A preprint is not a shot on goal. Providing preliminary data suggesting your main hypothesis is valid is not the shot on goal. The application as a whole is the shot on goal. None of the NIH application elements should be seen as some sort of essential elements that if you just do a good job of will guarantee funding. All of the components of the proposal are merely the skill moves, and efforts, and passing, and playmaking and work that adds up to the shot on goal. Sometimes the fanciest moves result in nothing useful. Sometimes a well oiled play comes up short. Sometimes a set of skills that produced regular goals in prior games are no longer enough. The field of play has not changed. The location of the goalposts has not changed. It’s most likely that what has changed is the level of the competition.

Getting back to the dismay about the data-preprint-pub-highJIF trajectory, I’m going to suggest you take this as further evidence for my general thesis. Reviewers (the same reviewer or other reviewers) are free to make these seemingly escalating comments because they never promise you in the first place that if your grant didn’t have this weakness, oh, they would have given it a fundable score. That is not what the identification of a weakness means. It simply means that the weakness is present. And this particular weakness is one that makes a meaningful communication with the other members of the study section panel. The fact they didn’t also pile on with every conceivable weakness does not likewise mean your application doesn’t have those weaknesses….or that it wouldn’t in a revised form.

It just means that the reviewer has identified enough weaknesses, this time, to justify their overall position on the proposal. If your next shot doesn’t change their (the same reviewer or even other reviewers) overall position, they will just find other weaknesses to deploy.

Because trust me. Your grant proposal has other weaknesses.

Does Approach really drive your NIH proposal’s Overall Impact score?

drugmonkey April 5, 2024 Grant Review / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism / NIH funding / Peer Review

It has been quite a time now since NIH released data showing that the overall impact score voted for a grant application was best correlated with the Approach criterion scores, over the remaining allegedly co-equal four criteria of Innovation, Investigator, Environment and Significance. This was discussed on the NIH OER blog by prior OER head honcho Sally Rockey, originally reported for NIGMS by then Director Jeremy Berg, and discussed by NIA folks. The blog post from Director Berg reporting on the criterion scores – overall impact correlations for each IC for all of Fiscal Year 2010 is here.

This finding is really quite robust. It may be quite misleading from the standpoint of directing applicant behavior.

I have tried to express on this blog that the review of NIH grants is a highly subjective business. I have often asserted that review comments, critiques and strength/weakness bullet points are an exercise in confirmatory writing. I have claimed on more than one occasion that reviewers reach a sort of gut feeling, stance and/or Gestalt positioning on a grant proposal first, and then write their critiques to support that position. I do not claim this is explicit or that reviewers even understand themselves that this is what they are doing.

As you may deduce at this point, Dear Reader, yes I am looking at a recent summary statement for an amended version (A1) of a NIH grant proposal. I am contrasting this with the summary statement for the original version of the proposal which was reviewed in the same standing study section, albeit across a significant interval of time. The outcome was qualitatively different in the sense that the first score/percentile was a no-way, no-how* result and the current score/percentile is a maybe**. This is not obvious to fund, particularly given the essentially stagnant budget for FY2024, but it is in a qualitatively different place. Certainly, the reviewers know the difference. Oh, yeah, I still also assert that reviewers are anchored to a rough fund/do not fund position when they are assigning scores. The assigned reviewers know that they are assigning post-discussion scores that signal “fund it”, “maybe?” and “don’t fund it” to Program. As do the other members of the panel when they decide where to vote within that range, or whether to go outside of that range.

So what made the difference? Was it that my grantsmithing on the amended version was amazingly better? I would assert no, no it is not. The basic core fundamental idea is the same. The general approaches are the same. The capabilities of staff and environment are essentially the same. Our additional preliminary data are only very thinly “better” and do not really fundamentally address related criticisms on the first version.

Sure, we made changes in response to the prior review, and some reviewers still credit this, even if NIH is fairly overtly against benchmarking review to prior review. But I would argue that for every “fix” in response to review there are three more things in the research strategy that could be used as fodder for a pretty fundamental weakness bullet point. Some of those might be carried over, albeit ignored the first time. Or they could be new issues introduced by the revisions made in response to prior review.

There is no such thing as a perfect, unassailable grant proposal. In my opinion. I assert that any minimally experienced grant reviewer could absolutely eviscerate any grant on grounds that a study section in aggregate sees as fair and consistent with their general behavior.

And this is where we get to my problem with the correlation of Approach criterion scores with overall voted Impact score. It could be assumed, by the naive and not so naive PI, that a fantastic Approach leads to reviewers being convinced to issue a fundable score. And therefore that it is possible to force good scores by writing a “perfect” Approach. You will see these notions endorsed by many a grant writing course. You will see well-intentioned senior mentors and Program Officers issuing helpful advice which basically boils down to “write better grants”. This has always boiled my blood. My experiences as applicant and as reviewer simply do not support the idea that an iron clad approach always leads to a great overall impact score.

My two sets of critiques, and the resumes of discussion, for this recent proposal paint this different picture. The better review is laden with appreciative comments on the basic, fundamental issue under proposed investigation. The worse review also has appreciative comments on the basic issue. If I have convinced them of anything, it is that this is an area in need of further investigation. The reviewers for the original version also had a lot of weaknesses to discuss. In the Significance and in the Approach criteria. The resume underlined how all of this was likely only to have a modest impact. The reviewers for this round have chosen to find the Approach perfectly reasonable and have chosen not to mention any weaknesses. This is otherwise known as “the reviewers get it”. The resume of discussion underlined how this would be high impact on the field.

Is that because there are no weaknesses in the most recent plan? Hell no.

I know this because I have prior grants with structures of scientific investigation that are roughly similar to the plan in this one. And I am quite familiar with how reviewers can find weaknesses in various things. It is impossible, so I assert, to write a 12 page research plan that covers all possible takes. It is impossible for any lab, or even a three lab collaboration, to cover the terrain that all possible NIH grant reviewers might find to be terribly important. Reviewers continue to feel totally comfortable dinging grants because the PI doesn’t do it the way they, the Reviewer, would choose to do it.

I don’t leave these big Approach holes in my plan on purpose. When writing a grant proposal I am constantly trying to figure out what is the most important information to communicate in the limited page space and what critique bait I have to leave unaddressed. I know there are holes. I can look at any proposal I have submitted with my cynical reviewer eye and come up with fundamental weaknesses. Or I can choose to overlook them.

So what made the difference? No idea. For whatever reason, this proposal was assigned to two of three reviewers that found it to be important this time. In a fundamental, general sense. And they wrote their critiques accordingly. And gave Approach and Significance criterion scores to match. The other reviewer was holding firm*** on one issue. And gave Approach and Significance criterion scores more consistent with the outcome of the original version. While the NIH does not require reviewers to generate their all-important pre-Discussion overall impact score as any particular function of the five allegedly co-equal criterion scores, me, I find that the Approach and Significance scores are a pretty good indicator, as reflected in the correlational data from the NIH.

There was a recent tweet which asserted that NIH grant reviewers only gave out good scores to “friends and family****”.

Ok.

Is this true for my grants?

There are seven individuals listed on the study section rosters for both reviews. Of course, I have no idea if any of them were assigned as one of the three reviewers for either version or if they contributed in any way to the discussion. Someone who was reasonably aware of who I know in science, and to what extent they have a reasonably positive or friendly relationship with me, would perhaps say the current roster has four or five names that might be expected to be on my side. To be my “friends”, whether personally, scientifically or both. The first roster has four such names. There is one name on each roster, different people, that may appear to be friendly disposed towards me but are also close enough competitors to raise an eyebrow in the other direction. Maybe two of these names on the first roster. There are a lot of names on each roster (~40 members in all) that I don’t know and I assume don’t know much about me either. Of these, maybe four or five on each roster are people that one would expect to take a more than average interest in the topic. And that would bust my chops for a fairly fundamental approach issue that my lab simply does not do. An issue that fails entirely to interest me and is the entire world to them.

The point here is not that I know that I got strong review input from my “friends” this time, but not last time. It is still a fool’s errand to try to determine who would, or did, give your grant proposal favorable or unfavorable reviews. There are some of the apparently “friendly disposed” types that I have seen give very critical reviews on grants in study section. There are some seemingly antagonistic reviewers (on paper) that hand out really good reviews, sometimes even arguing a middlin’ pair of other reviewers into a fundable position.

The point is that regardless of why, your grant proposal needs to have advocates to get a good score. Assigned as reviewers. And the antagonist reviewer(s), if there are any, can’t be that firm in their position. They have to be somewhat constrained, as this one was, by something. Whether that be their own overall position, their stance relative to other reviewers, their general approach to scores of amended applications or whatever.

The advocates have to like the big picture. This is what drives their score. Significance. Importance. Impact. The main fundamental issue under proposed investigation.

However you’d care to express it.

Once they are in the bag on the big idea, they are willing to overlook obvious weaknesses in the Approach.

But since Approach is conventionally viewed as super important to objective review, they have to give good scores for that criterion. So the tail wags the dog.

*While it is true any voted score could be funded by the NIH, this was way out in the very, very, very unlikely zone.

**This IC asserts they do not have a payline but going by recent funding patterns by the IC over at RePORTER, I have a definitively grey zone score.

***I don’t know that it was the same reviewer, it is just that the issue was mentioned in the first review. And we did not adopt the suggestion*****, but opted to rebut it and retain our original approach.

****Family members would be an automatic conflict off of any NIH grant review. C’mon now. be serious.

*****This is one of those strategy things. Adopting a different experimental variant risks getting dinged for the lack of preliminary data or the relative lack of demonstrated expertise of your group with the experiments in question. A PI has to decide if the risks of sidestepping a critique outweighs these other common grantsmithing risks.

200 hours

drugmonkey March 13, 2024 Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism

Somewhere in the latest rounds of true confessions about how many grants a PI on Twitter has put in for a given number of awards it was asserted that it takes 200 hours of work to prepare a grant the size and scope of a NIH R01.

I was curious about how general that was.

The poll resulted in 61 people saying this seemed about right, 63 who thought this is just a flagrant lie and 57 who thinks perhaps the first category of people just don’t understand what “work” is.

To make this a little more conceptually clear, 200 hours is five weeks of 8 hour uninterrupted workdays devoted to one grant application. Five whole workweeks. With nothing accomplished during an 8 hour day that is not grant preparation. No distractions.

While it is likely that nobody really works like this starting five weeks out from a grant deadline, it gives us some perspective on what 200 hours means.

I think it is somewhat likely that people just don’t really know what solid work on one thing really means. This is not uncommon in the biological lab disciplines in which exaggerated face time and claims of 60+ h work weeks abound.

I think it is also somewhat likely that people are just plain lying to themselves, see above.

I think it is maybe true that the first R01 one has ever written takes a LOT of time compared with normal operating behavior for a grant seeking PI. Thereafter, one can leverage some of the time one has spent on preparing prior awards.

IF, however, there are PIs out there that really are putting in 200 hours of solid, uninterrupted work on each and every R01-scope grant proposal…..there is something wrong* with what they are doing.

And they need to figure out how to fix that.

The cumulative probability of one award (this one assumes a 17.7% success rate) is brutal (Fig. 1). Ten applications to get to a 85.7% probability. And this is no guarantee of award given all the other factors that come into play. How is a newly appointed PI to reach these submission numbers within the ~5-6 year tenure interval? This is 2-3 per year once they get going. If it really takes 200 hours of work for each one, this is essentially impossible.

IMO.

*Unless those PIs are getting awarded for every grant proposal they submit. I’m not going to believe it until I see their eRA commons listing, but hey, this is a big wide world where anything can happen.

NIH guidance on Resubmission Applications

drugmonkey February 28, 2024 Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH funding

The NIH is issuing new guidance (NOT-OD-24-061) on Marking Changes in Resubmission Applications. As you are well aware, NIH watchers, it is permitted to resubmit a previously unfunded NIH grant application for consideration. At present time, one is allowed a single amended version which goes in at a different receipt date (typically one month later than new proposals) and can include a one page Introduction. The Introduction to the Resubmitted grant is supposed to respond to the critiques supplied in review of the original version, and part of the review is supposed to cover how well the PI has responded to the prior criticisms.

At one point of time in the distant past, one could put in Resubmissions endlessly. There are still A6 and A7 funded grants in the RePORTER database, meaning that an original submission was amended 6 or 7 times. When I came along as a PI, the NIH had limited these to two amendments, and then they further limited PIs to one amended version.

In the early part of my career, the Introduction was up to three pages but it eventually was pared down to 1 page. This was possibly coincident with the reduction of the R01 research plan page limit to 12 from the prior 25. I was trained to both address as many of the criticisms as I could in the Introduction and to use some sort of indication in the body of the Resubmission to indicate major changes in the text / figures. I favored the left line in the margin, as instructed by my mentors. Once on study section, I discovered this was probably the most common method, although bold or italics text and sometimes indenting was also used.

NOT-OD-15-030 informed us in 2014 that “NIH has removed the requirement to identify ‘substantial scientific changes’ in the text of a Resubmission application by ‘bracketing, indenting, or change of typography’” which underlined that such indication was not just a good idea and a guide for the reviewers, but was actually an expectation.

What really confused matters was the language used on a NIH website about the Resubmission. On this page, which currently says it was last updated April 3, 2023, the removal of a requirement is transformed into a “do not”.

This happened several years ago and was a complete surprise to me when I finally saw it. I do not recall seeing any notice that transitioned us from the relief from requirement to a prohibition. It was super critical to recognize for two reasons. First, NIH has taken to stringently enforcing rules which may have been overlooked in the past, such as the inclusion of hot links to URL addresses. This makes us nervous about every little rule which likewise has been ignored (and I’m here to tell you I submitted many proposals with changes indicated after the NOT-OD-15-030 was issued). Since I don’t know when the prohibition language on the above mentioned website appeared, I don’t know how many Amended grants I submitted in violation of that prohibition but it was probably more than one!

The second major problem is that of uneven application of these rules by reviewers. If you stick to the voluntary rules of not indicating changes, and other PIs with applications in the same study section do not, you are disadvantaging yourself. Because most reviewers expect to enjoy this guidance to the changes. Many are totally unaware of the NIH alteration of policies. Those who understand the removal of the obligation may mark applications down for taking advantage of this, because they expect to see changes indicated. If there is no enforcement of the prohibition, this is even worse because the rule follower PI thinks they are prohibited, and the reviewer thinks they are voluntarily refusing to indicate changes. Maddening! I have taken to including a statement in my Introductions that changes are not marked per “current NIH policy” to deal with this situation as best I can.

Apparently NIH has finally noticed the oversight. In NOT-OD-24-061 we read:

effective May 25, 2024, this guidance replaces previous guidance on marking changes in Resubmission applications. The use of markups such as bracketing, indenting, highlighting, bolding, italicizing, underlining, margin lines, change in typography, font, or font color, or any other type of markup should not be used to identify changes in Resubmission applications.

This is accompanied by a RESCINDED notice on the 2014 NOT-OD-15-030 which mentions this new NOT and no others. So again, this is pretty clearly correcting an oversight* and resolving the apparent disconnect between NOTiced policy and website instruction.

Just exactly the kind of thing that drives applicants and reviewers nuts.

I think I will leave the insanity of NIH expecting that

“The Introduction must include a summary of substantial additions, deletions, and changes to the application. It must also include a response to weaknesses raised in the Summary Statement.”

can be accomplished in one page using approved font size for another day.

*ETA: There’s some chatter on the Bluesky interpreting the “should not” in the new NOT as softening the “do not” of the web page and asserting that marking changes is “now allowed”. I think this is a bizarre interpretation of the language, particularly within the context of rescinding/replacing the 2014 notice. I interpret this “should not” to mean “do not”.

NIH budget cuts and expenditure categories

drugmonkey February 21, 2024 Day in the life of DrugMonkey / Grant Review / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Budgets and Economics

There’s a recent tweet complaining about NIH budget cuts that is worth a bit of comment. I’m not going to link to it directly because I don’t know the details, which could be crucial, and in the wide world of NIH extramural grant award almost anything is possible. And if it is possible, it has probably occurred. More than once. I’ll be talking here about the most usual cases and not some rare out of the blue exception.

The motivating tweet claims that the most recent Notice of Award (NOA, aka Notice of Grant Award or NGA) for an R01, NIH has cut the budget for personnel by $100K, reducing their personnel budget to $68k. This, of course, was tied to some point or other about how this was insufficient to support a single postdoc, with current salaries and fringe rates being what they are.

Cue the usual instacomplaints in the replies and QTs about how this PI is just using this to advocate against fair wages for postdocs, blah, de blah. I’m not going to descend into this particular fantasy land today.

What I want to address is the apparent claim that NIH has cut the personnel part of the budget specifically. This is, to my experience and understanding, not something that is generally done. Particularly not done for R01s, even more pointedly outside of the U-mechanisms. The “cooperative agreement” U-mechs involve a lot more meddling by Program Officers.

First up though, if you are not aware, the NIH routinely cuts the budget proposed in a grant application. Frequently, routinely, often….habitually even. You need to be aware of this if you work in any way under NIH extramural grant funded projects. They cut budgets by announced NIH-wide policy. For example if the Congress has not yet passed a budget for the Fiscal Year (FY) and the government is under a Continuing Resolution, the NIH issues a Notice. NOT-OD-24-059 informs us that “NIH Institutes and/or Centers may, at their discretion, issue non-competing research grant awards at a level below that indicated on the most recent Notice of Award. Upward adjustments to awarded levels will be considered after FY 2024 appropriations are enacted, but NIH expects institutions to monitor their expenditures carefully during this period.“

If you want to know why, this is quite simple and there is no excuse for anyone in this game to pretend it is not this, or that this is not entirely understandable from the NIH point of view. They reduce budgets so that they can fund more grants for the same fixed allocation for the Fiscal Year. Period.

Second finger wag: There is correspondingly no justification for NIH watchers to simultaneously or sequentially advocate for “basic grants for everyone” type award policies and to also complain about budget reductions. This is the unrelenting arithmetic of NIH grant award. It is dishonest to talk out of both sides of your mouth depending on which of these scenarios is currently in front of you.

Some ICs have longer standing announced policies, such as NIGMS’ policy to not fund any R01 to established investigators for more than 4 years. Even under normal budgeting when the FY allocation to the IC is known, some ICs may announce across the board policies such as that all awards will be reduced by 10%. Or maybe they just apply that to grants submitted with traditional budgeting and leave the modular proposals’ budgets intact.

The ICs also cut award budgets without any general announcement. They can, and do, alter budgets on an essentially one-by-one individualized fashion for a host of reasons. This ranges from “that well funded investigator has too much money now” to “this untried junior person can’t be trusted with the full amount, let’s give her less and see how she does with it”. Let me just note that my first R01 as PI was awarded in circumstances much like this last comment. They first tried to halve the annual direct costs. For reasons, we negotiated them away from that but to save face they cut a four year proposal down to three. Yes, budgets can be cut in annual direct costs. They can be cut in number of years awarded. They can be cut in both. They have an entire mechanism, the R56, which can be used to award less than the proposed budget and number of years as a bridge type thing.

The NIH can cut the budget on the initial award year and then propagate that budget level for the entire 5 year award, even if such routine cuts are not levied in the subsequent years for other new awards. They can cut the budget for one year and leave the other years un-modified.

Are you getting the sense that the NIH ICs can do whatever the heck they want when it comes to awarding the budget amount for a given extramural grant? Good. They can and they do.

In all of my years of paying attention to this stuff, I have never heard of a case in which a normal run of the mill R01 is issued with a NOA that specifies which category of expenditure, such as personnel, has to be cut. A grant is not a contract. A R01 is not a U01. Even with a traditional itemized budget, there is always a process available to “rebudget” from one category into another. Even to and from the capital Equipment category* which requires some foofraw because of the IDC implications. The budget reductions that I have seen are always conveyed in the NOA as “here’s how much you are able to spend”.

The NIH NOA does not say “you have to fire your postdoc and keep all of the money in the supplies budget”.

There are a couple of things that I could imagine lead to the sort of expressed misconception that got me off and running today. First, a summary statement could very well contain a comment from review that says “recommend to reduce the postdoc effort” or “cut one technician”. That is because, somewhat weirdly to newcomers, the SRO will insist. If the panel is to recommend a budget reduction, IMO the SRO will not take a monetary value. It is not valid for reviewers to say “cut those greedy basstiges by $25k per year“. They have to say what part of the expenditure they find to be excessive and make recommendations for operational cuts, not dollar figures. Too much effort is a common target, particularly when it is a modular budget proposal and that is all the reviewer can really see of the proposed expenditures. When it is a traditional budget, reviewers can say things like “the PI should not pay a senior expensive tech**, a junior tech will do”. Still, comments like those in the summary statement, and a corresponding reduction of the budget on award, do not force*** the PI to actually reduce expenditure in the specified domain.

The NIH likely did not cut this tweeps “personnel budget”. The most likely scenario is that the budget was reduced and the PI is faced with dealing. This may mean reducing the scope, it may mean having fewer people on the project. It might involve a lot of fancy tap dancing to try to keep existing staff paid, while making juuuuust enough progress to get the next budget awarded.

Them’s the breaks.

This is the way.

*OK, there are some things about capital equipment expenditure late in a grant’s lifecycle that can be denied, but this is for the advanced class.

**Yes I get this occasionally and yes, I sometimes really dislike my colleagues on review panels.

***As always, the PI is at risk of annoying their Program Officer. If the PO has been assertive about you expending 100% of a postdoc effort and not the 300% you proposed, you’d risk the next year of award if you hire three postdocs instead of one. Particularly if you have no progress to show on the scientific front.

On Review Biases, Environmental and Otherwise

drugmonkey December 20, 2023 General Politics / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism

Some Twitt made a comment involving evaluation of the Environment on NIH grant applications in light of steps some States have been taking that may make grant recipient institutions have trouble meeting various DEI goals.

To make this explicit, we have some states which are enacting draconian laws regulating women’s reproductive health and choices. Lots of active scientists, trainees especially, are in their childbearing years or who have life partners who are. These laws do not just put women at health risk but they also threaten them, and anyone who may assist them with some health needs, with legal consequences ranging from steep fines to jail sentences.

Other places are criminalizing same-sex relationships, or permitting LGBTQ+ people to be fired from their jobs simply due to their identity. Some places are putting academics at risk of being fired from their jobs on the basis of teaching content (or conducting research?) deemed to be too “woke”, i.e., focused on social justice and equity. Or on the accusation that they are offending the tender sensibilities of majoritarians or right wingers.

There are hints of more to come pending the mere passing of legislation. The same SCOTUS decision which appears to permit States to retrench on same-sex marriage gives every indication of permitting them to restore anti-miscegenation laws. For example.

It seems quite reasonable to think that Universities in these states are going to face steep headwinds in recruiting academics who are at threat from these laws, those who are partnered with people who may be at threat from such laws, and people who just don’t want to be living in States which pass such laws.

Correspondingly, the equitable access to NIH funded activities in such States is reduced.

Knowing how anonymous poll responses compare with actual Twitter responses I did a stupid little poll. 1,607 votes is pretty high for my polls, I must say.

Clearly our opinions vary.

Naturally, this poll triggered a lot of defensiveness. Many of those making comments saying it was unfair and counterproductive to do this have clues in their Twitter bios that suggest they are from consensus Bad States.

Leaving aside the discussion of whether such Environments are indeed counterproductive to some or all equity and opportunity goals of the NIH, I want to get into another complaint. The defensive types say it is unfair to punish investigators in those States with some sort of biased take on the Environment because the PIs can’t help those things.

Is it an unusual or unfair bias? Now some of this is moot, because the NIH has announced changes in the way Environment will be assessed for grants submitted after 1/25/2024. It will become a drop down menu with options of “satisfactory”/”unsatisfactory”. But even so, these types of considerations will influence review.

Biases about the category of applicant institution, the PI’s department or job type, etc have always been part of the review of grants and are often revealed in the critique. Chatter around the campfire and online about how NIH dollars should be “fairly” allocated, to whom and to where abound. There are analyses of NIH funding patterns by geography and classifications of the investigators that suggest additional biases at the point of initial review. And all of these are touchpoints for reviewer biases to influence the merit review.

One such issue relates to the R1 versus not-R1 status of the applicant University. Mostly we hear resentful comments from individuals from not-R1 institutions about supposed biases in favor of R1 institution applicants. One can occasionally hear specific complaints about med school departments versus academic campus departments in a related vein

Another complaint pops up in the discussion of PIs in full, or partial, soft money jobs. Those with FTEs, generally with instructional loads, will typically be the aggrieved party in this domain.

Those two tend to interact with each other and with ZIP code bias. This is the one where the coastal regions are perceived by the heartland PIs to have an unfair advantage in NIH grant getting.

Women. We hear muttering about biases, despite the fact that NIH has aaaaaalllmost equated (statistically indistinguishable!) male and female PI success rates for half a career now.

The Ginther gap. nuff said.

The defensiveness in the case of the Bad State discussion touched on how unfair it was to penalize PIs / applicants for circumstances they did not choose and can do nothing about, save try to move.

I would point out that this is also the case for many of the above mentioned biases. And at the very least people on the good and bad side of such biases do not all contribute directly to those biases.

Do NIH peer reviewers who perceive themselves to be at some sort of categorical disadvantage fight back for their category? Do reviewers from not-R1 schools give applications from not-R1 investigators better scores? Do Asian investigators give other Asian investigators better scores in response to a perceived bias (that may not be completely supported by Ginther)? Do women?

Do hard money faculty give the old shiv to soft money applicants?

It is hard to imagine there is not at least an implicit effect. And it gets worse, potentially creating a vicious cycle. .

Do the previously privileged class or category of PI now feel like there is a bias against them which they now need to combat with explicit favoritism of their own class?

I’ve heard some ugly comments about how Asian investigators give other Asian investigators good scores- does this make nonAsian peers introduce a perceived corrective, now explicit, bias? Zerhouni famously pointed out that the introduction of ESI policy made ESI scores get worse. Comments about how diversity efforts have totes disadvantaged straight white men, despite data showing this to be nonsense, abound in our biz….does this cause a doubling down at grant review?

I can’t say I’ve heard anyone get too explicit about this lately but I’ve heard comments about “my grant was killed by some Associate Prof from a University nobody ever heard of” enough to suspect the NIH geographical diversity rules for study section appointment may have occasionally led to a sort of payback bias. Again, for perceived biases. That may not only not be true, or may be merely a small reduction in the magnitude of the bias in favor of, e.g., R1 PI apps.

The point is, bias against job (and individual) circumstances that the PI cannot help creeps into grant review all of the time. Talking about one potentially looming source of such bias is not somehow changing this game. Pretending that the bias which may disadvantage you and yours is somehow worse than those other biases, for reasons, is a bad and selfish look.

If the shock and outrage that you feel about possibly being subject to such biases wakes you up to the bias that other PIs have been subjected to, well, the medicine is working.

You can’t force them to see it your way….but what else are you going to do?

drugmonkey November 17, 2023 Conduct of Science / Grant Review / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism / NIH funding / Peer Review / Science Communication / Science Publication / Scientific Publication

One of perhaps the most frustrating things about NIH grant review is the fact that it is almost entirely subjective and you cannot force reviewers to see things your way. This comes up fairly explicitly with demands to see particular preliminary data to “support” your application, or, more accurately, criticism for the lack thereof. So the applicant PI dutifully supplies some preliminary data in their revised grant only to see their score go backwards. Very frustrating.

Especially when the critique has now done some version of goal post moving, ridiculous (mis)interpretation of what is necessarily a limited amount of data and absurdly demanding that the preliminary data need to cover a scope of work equal to the $250K first year you just proposed.

A long time ago a reader challenged me on the LPU, the Least Publishable Unit. This is a term often used dismissively to denigrate what one person sees as an insufficiently broad or deep paper. I am relatively unbothered by such fevers and I am in fully in support of people publishing small or limited papers. After all, this used to be the entirety of what was published in Science. I tend to find that arguments against LPUs fail to hold water. I summarized a topical case study in why rapid publication of narrow aspects of a developing story assisted science progress. For me the key is the word “Publishable”. That means peer reviewed published in regular old journals published by the BigBad normal publishing houses. In sum, papers that slot into the traditional, established system with no sneering about predatory journals, OA self-publishing, etc. (That’s another argument for another day.)

Some journals have Short Communications that permit 1-3 “display” items (figures, tables) and have very limited word counts. Other journals simply have a range of what they will publish, without actually calling it a different publication type. Many of the bread and butter journals of modest JIF in my fields of closest interest have LPUs. They are normal.

Getting back to NIH grant review, despite the fact that preliminary data and even published papers are not going to force the entrenched skeptic to see it your way….what else are you going to do? First, your data may give the advocate ammunition to argue harder. Second…if the critic has a question that can be answered, generally speaking the field probably needs that answer. Eventually. Even if it is a partial answer.

We recently gained publication acceptance for a LPU paper that has a lot of “flaws” going by the usual reviews I get. And it was very limited in scope. As it happens in this case, we miraculously managed to avoid a lot of ticky-tack complaining by reviewers. These folks GOT it. They agreed that at least one or two of the fundamental points we were making was good enough to justify publication. But even if they had not, I would have fought to get this in there or elsewhere.

I have strategic reasons to do so.

I call it driving pitons. (My trainees probably get sick of this rock climbing metaphor.) My point is that sometimes you need to drive home a semi-permanent attachment point in the middle of a climb. You have to show you made it this far. That this climbing route is possible, at least to this point. And that you have a midway station that enhances your chances of making it to the top. The goal is to provide a flag to point to in future cases when skeptic grant reviewers say “but you can’t climb that!”. Or when they say “nobody has climbed that”.

Small papers can be these pitons. They can supply one flag or several. Sometimes the intermediate flags can point to multiple future routes of success.

and oh by the way. This is the community enterprise of science. You are also signalling to other climbers that they can succeed on this climb. Or can use your midway point to start off on another route.

We drove at least three pitons on this particular LPU paper and I feel good about that.

Whether it convinces others to fund our future climbs or not.

Another NOSI annoyance

drugmonkey September 1, 2023 GettinYourSkillzOn / Grant Review / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH funding

Some years ago NIH started using Notices of Special Interest (NOSI) more consistently in place of some prior uses of Program Announcements. The rationale appeared to be that they could move more quickly with these, but I was never really clear on the whys, the advantages. At the moment NIDA has 162 active NOSI, compared with 57 PAs, 116 PAR, 3 PAs and 147 RFAs. NOSI are a substantial part of their plans to djinn up applications on certain topics of interest to them.

Of course, much like Program Announcements, it is never really clear how much advantage you get from submitting under a NOSI versus the parent omnibus solicitation for an R01 or whatever. From the side of Program, well, you still need a preeeety good score for subtle “Programmatic priority” nudges to help you. From the side of review, well…

I was initially trained as a reviewer to make explicit comment on whether a NIH proposal which referenced some sort of targeted PA actually accorded with the intent of the PA. This is entirely consistent with the notion that review is there to assist the Program Officers in making their decisions about which are the most meritorious proposals to fund.

However, I have recently been told by an SRO that reviewers are not supposed to make any comment on the match to the PA / NOS. I was shocked, given my approximately 18 years of reviewing grants prior tho this. This is a nasty little story for another time but it does remind us that there may possibly be some differences across time, study section or SRO that give reviewers a different impression about the job vis a vis the FOA.

What I am really annoyed with today, however, is the fact that you cannot search RePORTER for all of the funded grants submitted with a given NOSI. You can do this for RFAs and Program Announcements. There’s a search field for Opportunity Number. This was one way that you could keep an eye on what Program was doing with respect to certain targeted interests. It could give you an idea of whether certain interests were likely to be over- or under-subscribed at a given point in time. It could give you a general idea of whether PAs served any useful purpose from the PI perspective.

You can’t do that with NOSI.

You can search RePORTER for a given NOSI but this pretty much depends on the PI having quoted the NOSI number in their Abstract. Some do this. Some undoubtedly do not. I don’t know how consistently I do this but I certainly have included the number of specific funding opportunities in my grant proposal Abstract in many of my submissions over the decades. So any attempt to see what Program really means, or potentially how reviewers are reacting to proposals on the topic, is not really going to bear any fruit. Not to the extent it can for a PA or RFA.

ICs are trickling in to join the NIH PAR for workplace diversity

drugmonkey January 5, 2023 Fixing the NIH / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism / NIH funding

In June of 2022 PAR-22-181 (NIDA, NIMH and NINDS Research Opportunities for New and “At-Risk” Investigators to Promote Workforce Diversity) informed us that NIDA, NIMH and NINDS would like to solicit R01 applications that proposed research which would “support New Investigators and At-Risk Investigators from diverse backgrounds, including those from groups underrepresented in the health-related sciences“. The diverse backgrounds are defined in NOT-OD-20-031 (Notice of NIH’s Interest in Diversity) and include A) underrepresented racial and ethnic groups, B) investigators with disabilities, C) investigators with socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds and D) women from the above backgrounds. (No, I am not clear on why D needs to be further emphasized, and the following sentences sound almost like they mean any women.)

The PAR came with one month earlier receipt dates (Sep 8 instead of the Oct 6 for new R01 and Oct 6 instead of Nov 6 for revisions, in 2022), an emphasis that proposals had to meet the usual research goals of the issuing ICs, and statements of intent to commit funds in the usual weaselese (e.g. “NIDA intends to commit up to $5 million per fiscal year, 12-15 awards, dependent on award amounts“). No guarantee of set aside funds. A bit of questioning around revealed that despite the PAR designation, these would be reviewed in standing study sections.

I am definitely down with something this specific directed at the Ginther gap. It provides cover for what I’ve been saying they should do for over a decade now- fix the gap immediately via exception pay decision making.

The PAR was joined by NINR (August 4), NCCIH (Sept 7), NIAAA (Sept 14) and NEI (Sept 14), in each case adding their abbreviation to the PAR title. NIBIB joined Jan 5, 2023, and at this point NIH apparently decided to stop using the IC indicators in the title. For unknown reasons, NIAID decided to issue their own version, PAR-22-241, Sept 21. NIDCD did theirs by RFA (RFA-DC-23-001 issued 4/5/22) but limited it to ESI and New Investigators only. I predict we are now running downhill and most if not all ICs will be participating in this initiative eventually. This is good news for those PIs who tend to seek funding from ICs other than the original three. A reminder for those of you who do not yet see your IC on the list to stay tuned.

You will recall at this point that NINDS tried something like this via NOSI in May of 2021. It was joined by NIDA and NIAAA and then no other ICs. Crickets. Increasingly suspicious crickets. In October 2021 the Office of the Director rescinded the NOSI. Reporting on the matter had the NIH claiming that it was duplicative with the above referenced statement on diversity and furthermore it was confusing. Some implication from NIH that it was illegal to award grants on the basis of skin tone or some such. Actually, Bernard and Lauer wrote “may have led to an impression that by linking demographic characteristics to grant proposals, applications supporting scientists from underrepresented groups would be automatically prioritized for funding.” I cannot for the life of me figure out what the difference is with this PAR that differentiates it on these issues from the prior NOSI.

The ONLY difference that is obvious is that Francis Collins retired as the Director of the NIH at the end of 2021.

There does not appear to be much that is unusual in the PAR about the preparation and review of these R01s. The one added item is a letter signed by the Authorized Organization Representative certifying that the PI meets the eligibility criteria and stating how the “participation of the identified PD/PI(s) would further the goals of the New and At-Risk Investigators to Promote Workforce Diversity Research program, consistent with the Notice of NIH’s Interest in Diversity“. This information is to be evaluated “at the time of application“, presumably at receipt and referral. It is unclear from this whether this letter goes along with the application through the review process, whether it is only retained by Program for their reference, or whether it is solely for receipt/acceptance purposes. One presumes at the very least it will go to Program. [UPDATE 01/10/2023: I have received confirmation that the letter remains with the application as it goes to peer reviewers on study sections.]

I admit to being slightly perturbed by the idea that the eligibility letter would be included with the application being reviewed by study section. It is bad enough that these are being reviewed in regular standing study sections, for structural reasons that are going to have to wait for another post. But putting some of these criteria in front of peers in the PI’s field seems…invasive. Not the racial, gender or ethnicity criteria, although I can imagine there are those who might prefer their peers do not know their race or ethnicity. I am most bothered by the disability and the disadvantaged background criteria. The latter include being homeless, in foster care, being eligible for free or reduced lunch, receiving WIC benefits, being eligible for Pell grants. For some reason you have to have two of the poverty criteria checked…I am entirely unclear on how that defines disadvantage- why not three? why not four? anyway.

It all seems sort of invasive to me.

Beyond the personal humiliation factor, we have a focal version of the problem that is inevitable merely from the FOA being listed on the application. It is also a version of the broader issue of dealing with representation, equity and bias in the academy, namely the argument by anecdote and proven, specific, individual harm.

I’ve covered this before on this blog but it boils down to those that feel that any affirmative action has to only apply to individuals who it can be proven have specifically and individually suffered the worst of the worst hinderances, all of which are more severe than the factors suffered by the most disadvantaged of the majoritarian population of reference. It holds that we only extend help to individuals who have not been at all successful. If an individual has experienced any measurable success, see, this proves that they are not affected* by any biases we are trying to fix.

This is the kind of headwind at peer review that this PAR is facing. The pre-potent tendency of academics to want to litigate bias and disparity on an individual basis and have it proved to them beyond all doubt that the person getting a leg up has faced more disadvantage than any person who is not receiving a leg up.

I would say that of all the NIH diversity categories, the one most likely to let people “pass” is the socioeconomic disparity one. However, another way to look at the phenomenon of passing is through the lens of the “well she clearly wasn’t held back in any way, look at how well she’s done and nobody has any idea she grew up poor” litigator. It will be counter productive to the attempt to fix the Ginther gap to have reviewers fighting this out, overtly or covertly, between or within reviewer.

I think it’s a mistake to do affirmative action as the Oppression Olympics. Both on this individual basis, to try to glean out who is “most” deserving of help, but also in the more general sense that a PI who checks, say, all 4 of the Diversity letters is more deserving than the PI who only checks one. I will admit that this is one possible counter to my preference that these should be reviewed in special emphasis panels. Putting them all in competition with each other could sharpen this kind of disparity comparison. OTOH it could also make sure that everyone is more or less on board with the idea that each PI identity is equal, once accepted by NIH as qualifying under the PAR.

*Despite the fact that Ginther and Hoppe showed persisting disparity of grant award, no matter whether a PI worked in the most fundable topics, had seniority, prior grants, prior study section experience, etc, etc.

Reconsidering “Run to Daylight” in the Context of Hoppe et al.

drugmonkey January 7, 2022 Careerism / Fixing the NIH / Grant Review / Grantsmanship / NIH / NIH Careerism / Peer Review

In a prior post, A pants leg can only accommodate so many Jack Russells, I had elucidated my affection for applying Vince Lombardi’s advice to science careers.

Run to Daylight.

Seek out ways to decrease the competition, not to increase it, if you want to have an easier career path in academic science. Take your considerable skills to a place where they are not just expected value, but represent near miraculous advance. This can be in topic, in geography, in institution type or in any other dimension. Work in an area where there are fewer of you.

This came up today in a discussion of “scooping” and whether it is more or less your own fault if you are continually scooped, scientifically speaking.

The trouble is, that despite the conceits in study section review, the NIH system does NOT tend to reward investigators who are highly novel solo artists. It is seemingly obligatory for Nobel Laureates to complain about how some study section panel or other passed on their grant which described the plans to pursue what became the Nobel-worthy work. Year after year a lot of me-too grants get funded while genuinely new stuff flounders. The NIH has a whole system (RFAs, PAs, now NOSI) set up to beg investigators to submit proposals on topics that are seemingly important but nobody can get fundable scores to work on.

In 2019 the Hoppe et al. study put a finer and more quantitatively backed point on this. One of the main messages was the degree to which grant proposals on some topics had a higher success rate and some on other topics had lower success rates. You can focus on the trees if you want, but the forest is all-critical. This has pointed a spotlight on what I have taken to calling the inherent structural conservatism of NIH grant review. The peers are making entirely subjective decisions, particularly right at the might-fund/might-not-fund threshold of scoring, based on gut feelings. Those peers are selected from the ranks of the already-successful when it comes to getting grants. Their subjective judgments, therefore, tend to reinforce the prior subjective judgments. And of course, tend to reinforce an orthodoxy at any present time.

NIH grant review has many pseudo-objective components to it which do play into the peer review outcome. There is a sense of fair-play, sauce for the goose logic which can come into effect. Seemingly objective evaluative comments are often used selectively to shore up subjective, Gestalt reviewer opinions, but this is in part because doing so has credibility when an assigned reviewer is trying to convince the other panelists of their judgment. One of these areas of seemingly objective evaluation is the PI’s scientific productivity, impact and influence, which often touches on publication metrics. Directly or indirectly. Descriptions of productivity of the investigator. Evidence of the “impact” of the journals they publish in. The resulting impact on the field. Citations of key papers….yeah it happens.

Consideration of the Hoppe results, the Lauer et al. (2021) description of the NIH “funding ecology” in the light of some of the original Ginther et al. (2011, 2018) investigation into the relationship of PI publication metrics is relevant here.

Publication metrics are a function of funding. The number of publications a lab generates depend on having grant support. More papers is generally considered better, fewer papers worse. More funding means an investigator has the freedom to make papers meatier. Bigger in scope or deeper in converging evidence. More papers means, at the very least, a trickle of self-cites to those papers. More funding means more collaborations with other labs…which leads to them citing both of you at once. More funding means more trainees who write papers, write reviews (great for h-index and total cites) and eventually go off to start their own publication records…and cite their trainee papers with the PI.

So when the NIH-generated publications say that publication metrics “explain” a gap in application success rates, they are wrong. They use this language, generally, in a way that says Black PIs (the topic of most of the reports, but this generalizes) have inferior publication metrics so this causes a lower success rate. With the further implication that this is a justified outcome. This totally ignores the inherent circularity of grant funding and publication measures of awesomeness. Donna Gither has written a recent reflection on her work on NIH grant funding disparity, which doubles down on her lack of understanding on this issue.

Publication metrics are also a function of funding to the related sub-field. If a lot of people are working on the same topic, they tend to generate a lot of publications with a lot of available citations. Citations which buoy up the metrics of investigators who happen to work in those fields. Did you know, my biomedical friends, that a JIF of 1.0 is awesome in some fields of science? This is where the Hoppe and Lauer papers are critical. They show that not all fields get the same amount of NIH funding, and do not get that funding as easily. This affects the available pool of citations. It affects the JIF of journals in those fields. It affects the competition for limited space in the “best” journals. It affects the perceived authority of some individuals in the field to prosecute their personal opinions about the “most impactful” science.

That funding to a sub-field, or to certain approaches (technical, theoretical, model, etc, etc) has a very broad and lasting impact on what is funded, what is viewed as the best science, etc.

So is it good advice to “Run to daylight”? If you are getting “scooped” on the regular is it your fault for wanting to work in a crowded subfield?

It really isn’t. I wish it were so but it is bad advice.

Better advice is to work in areas that are well populated and well-funded, using methods and approaches and theoretical structures that everyone else prefers and bray as hard as you can that your tiny incremental twist is “novel”.
