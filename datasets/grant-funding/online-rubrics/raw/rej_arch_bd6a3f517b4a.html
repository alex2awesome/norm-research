NIH funding by priority score

0 Comments NIH / NIH Budgets and Economics / NIH funding drugmonkey September 22, 2022

Somehow I managed to forget that there is a section of the NIH RePORT / NIH Data Book where you can look at the distribution of funded/unfunded R01equiv and funded R56 awards by percentile. The 2021 Fiscal Year data for all NIH R01equiv awards looks like this. If you are new to NIH talk, a lower percentile indicates a higher merit ranking assigned during the peer review process. A percentile is used in place of a raw score voted by the review panels to account for any possible differences in panel calibration/set point. The R56 awards were submitted and reviewed as R01equiv proposals, but were funded in a reduced way, generally 1 or 2 years only and often at a reduced annual budget.

Thanks to the large numbers, this is a great representation of the general NIH funding trends. Although it is not represented as success rates within each bin, the provision of the Not Awarded bars makes this pretty easy to interpret. The NIH includes Institutes and Centers that use different strategies for funding, with one of the key variables the “strictness” with which they use the peer review percentile rankings to decide on what is funded. A “strict” I or C (hereafter IC) might fund every application with a 9%ile or better ranking and nothing with a 10%ile or worse ranking. In such a case we refer to the 9%ile as a “payline” for that IC, for that Fiscal Year (FY). Most, if not all, ICs use something less than a “strict” funding approach. As represented in the aggregate data here, there is a percentile under which virtually all grants will be funded; here it is anything 10%ile and lower that was funded, with rare exception. Above that percentile, some are funded and some are not funded, with a probability that declines with increasing distance above that payline. At some percentile rank, a grant proposal is very unlikely to be funded indeed.

The ICs may vary from each other, and from FY to FY, in where the payline is placed. Some of the ICs insist for public consumption that they do not even have a payline, but this is nonsense. NIDA is one of these and you can clearly make out in the below that they have a virtual or implicit payline at 6%ile. The smaller ICs which fund fewer grants may have distributions in a given year that are hard to fit into the general scheme, but you will find they do, if imprecisely.

There are modifications to funding policies within the ICs which can distort the pattern slightly, most pertinently ESI policy. For a long time, many ICs have been awarding grants to Early Stage Investigators and/or New Investigators (who have never been PI on a major grant from NIH) at higher percentiles than would fly for established PI applications. This may put a bump in the distribution, here, perhaps at around 15%ile here for NIDA? Note that ICs vary in how public they are about their ESI policy, check writedit for links. If you look at the funding distributions for ICs that broadcast their policy (you’ll have to navigate back to a prior fiscal year to match the data), you’ll see how it likely distorts the otherwise monotonic trends for decreasing award probability as the percentile worsens.

This came around to my attention today because someone accused NIAAA of being some sort of insider club which funded grants “way past the payline” with the implication this was to glad handing Old Boyz who did not deserved their awards. I surmise this was based on the fact that the distribution looks pretty flat.

Is that what this shows? I dunno. They fund many fewer R01equiv grants than does NIDA and you can see from this that the lack of a bulge at the lower percentiles is not because they are skipping awards, they only skipped funding of 1 below 6%ile, the exact same virtual payline as NIDA seems to have. And the fact that they fund everything up to 10%ile is that they have the cash to pay everything up to this ranking, not that they are less selective. NIAAA hits a 50/50 zone from about 12-13%ile whereas this is about 7-10%ile for NIDA- this is not evidence of some extreme difference in funding poorly-scoring grants. Then we get into what is possible a little ESI policy bump for NIDA around 14-15%ile and 17-20%ile for NIAAA. We can’t know for sure that is what is extending the distribution but it is a very likely possibility. Current FY22 funding policy at NIAAA says “Applicants eligible for consideration as first-time R01 investigators in FY 2022 will be paid using an extended percentile payline…and work towards supporting new investigators on R01 equivalent awards at success rates equivalent to that of established investigators“. NIDA policy for 2021 says: “NIDA continues to give additional consideration to New Investigators and especially Early Stage Investigators“.

This leaves us with the four grants selected by NIDA for funding at 20, 21, 27 and 28%ile and the 15 grants funded by NIAAA from 20-31%ile. Given the small numbers at NIAAA we don’t actually know where ESI/NI policy leaves off. We don’t know if their attempt to equalize success rates is a slightly more aggressive position compared to NIDA’s “additional consideration”. We don’t know if they have an equivalent position on this, but study sections are handing NIDA enough ESI apps to equalize success rates at better priority scores.

I think there is very little evidence from these distributions to support the accusation that NIAAA is hugely unusual in the way they select grants for funding in relationship to voted priority rankings. I suspect a lot of the perception is driven by the small numbers and the representation of these as awards instead of award rates. It is also driven by the inclusion of ESI/NI awards without any identification.

With that said, there are many other ways that ICs choose to spend their money. And these data do not get into additional detail that could support the accusation of relative “insider club” funding allocation. For all we know there is a larger/smaller percentage of R01equiv competing continuations, of single-award PIs, of dollars awarded per grant to some over others. There are additional large budget research awards that are not captured here- it is possible that there are differences in the percent of the overall IC budget that goes to R01equiv versus P-mechs and U-mechs.

Interestingly, NIDA awarded no R56 and stepping back through the FY has not done so from at least 2015 onward. Neither has NIAAA. At least based on this database. When I go to RePORTER I find 20 R56 for NIDA and 7 for NIAAA from 2015-2020, so…maybe don’t trust this Data Book database entirely*.

Because I am in the substance use research worlds, NIAAA and NIDA funding policies are most salient to me. I rub elbows with those who get funding from one or the other or often both. I am constantly amused by the fact that those who align primarily with one camp over the other are often heard declaring with great certainty that those undeserving folks over there get their grants easily, without rigorous competition and only because of politics or insider clubness. The above analysis is one of the reasons I laugh at their assertions, because I think they are both right and totally wrong. The NIAAA and NIDA folks are the same. It is roughly equivalently hard to get funded. It is roughly equivalently subject to insider bias. Any deviations are just noise in the system, or of such small effect size as to be irrelevant to broad brushing.
