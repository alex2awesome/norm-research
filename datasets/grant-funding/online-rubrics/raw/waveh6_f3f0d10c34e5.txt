ABSTRACT 

This book is the first of two comprising the Guide to 
Proposal Writing and Project Management in Vocational Education that 
outiines processes involved in the writing and fundings of proposals. 
The second book is available separately (see note). Iwo ci. the four 
parts into which the guide is divided are found in each of the books. 
Part I, Planning a Proposal, is an overview which proceeds through 
each step in the development of a proposal and the implementation 
procedures of an awarded project. The section, Proposal Components, 
defines components and gives examples. The components are problea 
statement, review of literature, objectives, methodology, and budget. 
The sectiun, Project Management, highlights implementation procedures 
so that a potential proposal writer/project director will be aware of 
steps involved in carrying out an awarded project. They are copyright 
Clearance, project monitoring and evaluation, project amendaent, 
final report, and product description. Part II, Preliminary Proposal, 
explains how to submit a preliminary proposal to the Division of 
Vocational Education. It also explains how to learn what funding 
Opportunities are available and shows a sample preliminary proposal 
form. (YLB)

INTRODUCTION 


Why was this guide written? The purpose of this guide 


to proposal writing is to increase the number of people who 
can write eligible proposals to the Division of Vocational 

Education. This goal has been approached in two ways: (1) 
by communicating the process to a greater number of people 

and (2) by explaining it so thorovgaly that the proportion 

of quality proposals will be increased. 

This guide was written to outline the processes involved 
in the writing and funding of projects. An awareness of 
these processes should result in more thorough planning and 
more successful communication of a proposal. Necessary forms 
in the application process will also be explained. 


How should the guide be used? This document is divided 


into two books containing the following four parts: 


Part I, "Planning a Proposal," is an overview which pro- 


ceeds through each step in the development of a proposal and 


the implementation procedures of an awarded project. Defini- 
tions and examples are given to help the proposal writer refine 
an identified problem into a proposal suitable for funding. 

A final, fully-developed proposal will contain all components 
described in Parc 1.A. "Proposal Components." Therefore, this 


section is a guide to both planning and writing. After a 


project is awarded, the project director snould follow the 
implementation procedures described in Part I.B. "Project 
Management ."’ 

Part II, "Preliminary Proposal," deals with the forms 
and information necessary for an initial submission of a 
proposal. The writer should read Part I first, however, for 
a complete overview of proposal development. Although the 
preliminary proposal may be abbreviated in form, an under- 
standing of the total process should ensure that this first 
submission to the funding agency will reflect careful and com- 
plete planning. 

Once a preliminary proposal has been reviewed and invited, 
the writer will progress to Part III, "Final Proposal." This 
section explains how to write the fully developed narrative 
which details not only the problem to be investigated but also 
the procedures to be used. 

The last section, Part IV, "Project Management and Re- 
porting,’ describes in detail responsibilities which the proj- 
ect director or manager will have. This information will be 
useful after a project has been awarded. 

In summary, the guide progresses from focusing on 2 prob- 
lem and planning the procedures to investigate a solution; to 


submitting a preliminary proposal; to fully developing a final 


proposal; to conducting and reporting on an awarded project. 


What are some basic definitions? A proposal is an appli- 


cation for funding submitted to the Division of Vocational 


Education. 


A project is a proposal which has been awarded funding by 
the Division. 


What assistance is there in the proposal writing process? 


The first step in proposal development should be to determine 
whether the problem under consideration addresses one of the 
state's funding priorities. These priorities are identified in 


the Florida State Plan for Vocational Education and are summna- 


rized in the Application for Federal Vocational Funds. These 


cgocuments are available from county superintendents and voca- 
tional directors, community college presidents and occcupational 
deans, presidents and department chairmen of vocational educa- 
tion in state universities, and from the Department of Correc- 
tions, the School for the Deaf and the Blind, and the Seminole 
and Miccosukee Indian tribes. 

Should the problem be appropriate to these priorities, one 
might discuss the proposal idea either with the local or insti- 
tutional director of vocational programs, regional consultants, 
or with research staff in the Division of Vocational Education. 
Some school districts or institutions may even have a proposal 
writing specialist. Additionally, input and review by col- 
leagues can provide many kinds of valuable assistance. A 
proposal writer can often benefit from help in refining the 
problem statement, conducting a needs assessment, formulating 
appropriate objectives and procedures to solve the problem, 
planning and conducting evaluation of the project, reviewing 
proposal drafts, and figuring budgets. Few individuals have 


expertise in all these areas. 


Once a proposal has been accepted and a project awarded, 
the Division may call on additional support services. Project 
monitors from the Research Section staff will provide contin- 
uing evaluation to ensure that the project is on track and its 
objectives are being met. Dissemination Section personnel work 
with the project director to plan release of products and in- 
formation from the project. In some cases, the Center for 
Studies in Vocational Educacion may provide assistance in 
instructional desigr, graphic design, and printing. 

However, a project begins as an idea in someone's mind, a 
problem in need of a solution. The next section, “Planning a 
Proposal," will help a prospective proposal writer develop that 
idea.

Problem Statement 


What should be included in the problem statement? The 


problem statement includes a description of the problem as 


well as the rationale for the project. A well-written problem 
Statement is crucial to the success of the proposal. It 
should convince the funding agency that the problem is impor- 
tant and the proposed solution is appropriate and likely to 
make a contritution to vocational education. The most worthy 
idea can be implemented only when the problem has been clearly 


defined.

What is the problem? The problem is a need that can be 


identified, a gap, a documented discrepancy between what is 
and what should be. 


How is the problem idertified or recognized? Problems 


in vocational education may be identified through observations. 
For example, a teacher observes declining enrollment in a 
drafting class. This may appear to be a problem, but other 
observations are necessary to make that determination. If 
demand for drafters (persons with drafting experiences) were 
also declining, the drop in er.rollment would be a realistic 
adjustment to the demand. Declining enrollment is one obser- 


vation, a description of a present situation or condition. If

In the preceding list, notice the word "documents." It

What is a needs assessment? A needs assessment is a 


formalized search for information. It is usually conducted 

by survey, whether oral or written, and is an attempt to learn 
of discrepancies or gaps between what is and what should be. 
It is, therefore, the search for a problem. 


How is a needs assessment conducted? A needs assessment 


is conducted in one of two general ways. In the first approach, 
the assessor may seem to be merely surveying a field, such as 
a specific program area in vocational education. Students, 
teachers, administrators, and employers may be asked to react 
to specific questions about enrollment, quality of training, 
or characteristics of program completers. Alternately, they 
may be asked to describe an "ideal" process or result and ana- 
lyze how the current situation differs. This seeking approach 
may reveal problems that no one was aware of before the needs 
assessment was conducted. 

The other approach to a needs survey is initiated as a 
result of knowing about a specific problem. The survey is 


more directed in this case and seeks information about the 


causes and the extent of the identified problem. An approach 
such as this is more likely to produce quantifiable data which 
may be useful in communicating the need to colleagues and 

to proposal reviewers. It is likely that &lt;: investigator 
might begin with the more general needs assessment and then 
move to the more specific one. 


What kinds of information might be sought in a needs 


assessment? One of the first things to be identified after 


a problem has been located is what the local district, insti- 
tution, or community has done to try to solve it. Knowledge 

of previous efforts will lead to a more intelligent selection 
of an alternative solution and may be useful assistance in 
justifying the project to reviewers. Discuss other programs or 
recent, related projects, and explain the relationship between 
previous activities and the proposed project. 

The proposal should present as much of the background of 
the problem as necessary to document the need and convince 
reviewers of its validity. The next section, "Review of Liter- 
ature," explains one method of providing background information. 
However, the proposal writer shouid investigate as many sources 
as possible. What other factors influence the situation? Are 
there current legislative directives? What is the intent of 
relevant laws and regulations? Is there a process used in a 
different area that may be applicable to the present situation? 
Build a good case for the proposed project in the problem 


statement section. 


Look back to the example of the drafting problem descrip- 
tion. It includes not only the identified problem (the gap) 
but also the attending conditions. The problem description 
reports an observed condition of declining enrollment. The 
observed condition can easily be documented by a check of class 
enrollment figures. The need for 75 trained drafters may be a 
documented condition reported by the local employment agency, 
or it may be the result of a formal needs suivey. The ideal 
conditions would be to meet the needs of industry and to pro- 
vide an adequate number of completers. Therefore, the problem 
description identifies the gap between the present and the 
ideal conditions and documents the discrepancy between what is 
and what shouid be.

In the drafting situation described above, the problem

the need remains unmet? How could result) from the project be 
disseminated to people who need the information? Answers to 
these questions will help develop the sign#ficance of the 


problem.

Statement of the Problem 


What is a problem statement? The problem statement in- 


cludes a description of the problem and the rationale for the 


project. 


How is a problem statement written? State the problem 


and its significance to vocational education in clear, concise 
terms. Problems should not be described in general terms 


such as, "I am going to examine the facts relating to 


Include both the present conditions and the desired conditions 
so that the discrepancy between them, the problem, is made 
obvious. 

Avoid wordy or too brief descriptions of the problem. 
State the problem so the reader will know what is included 
and what is not included. NOTE: The reader may or may not 
have an ungerstanding of the problem. 

It is particularly important that the problem be limited 
enough to be workable. If the problem is broadly stated, the 
funding agency may conclude that the writer has not focused on 
a specific, solvable aspect. For example, sex bias cannot be 
treated as a problem; however, specific problems involving sex 
bias in guidance and counseling, admissions, job recruitment, 
etc., can be identified. Although the problem cannot be solved 
for the entire country, the solution in one particular school 
may have widespread applicability. The problem statement, then, 
including the description and the rationale, naturally leads 
the proposal writer to an explanation of the proposed solution, 


which is the purpose of the project.

Review of Literature 


Why write a review of literature? This section is an 


excellent place to provide justification for the study and to 
show an understanding of related research activities. 


What is included in a review of literature? Present 


background information relevant to the problem statement. If 


possible, include studies currently underway which could 


contribute to the proposed project. Select and review pertinent 


studies which provide a foundation for the proposed project, 
citing references and data which support the significance of 

the problem. If there seem to be no related studies, rethink 
the descriptors used to locate them. Perhaps a problem is so 
current that recent  »rofessional journals and newsletters would 
provide more relevant information than scholarly texts. Masters’ 


and doctoral studies are additional potential sources. 


How is a review of literature written? Select studies 


which mark important points in the development of the problem 
area. A balance must be reached between exhaustive reporting 
and neglecting crucial information. Try to describe studies 
briefly but with enough detail to show their relevance and 
impact on the problem area. Be sure to relate also how this 
study moves beyond them. Summarize previous studies accurately. 
The review of literature should be written in simple past 
tense if the studies have already been completed. Ongoing 
studies may be reported in present tense. Deiine any terms 


which may be unfamiliar to the reviewer. 


Summary 
The review of literature should fit logically into the 


proposal and should show writing and research competence. 


REMEMBER: Of prime importance, the review of literature must 


be related to the proposed study. This gives continuity to the 


proposal and helps the reviewer to better understand the intent 


of the project.

Methodology 
It is in the methodology section that the proposal writer 


explains HOW the project will be conducted. This is one of the 


most carefully read sections of the proposal because objectives 


21 


are implemented through methodology. Explicit details should 
be provided to convince the funding agency that the objectives = 
can be accomplished. 

The methodology section should include information re- 
garding the plan of the project (the procedures) and the 
organizational and administrative arrangement for conducting 
the project (the project management). 


What is a good way to plan effective methodology? One 


planning technique which might be helpful is this. Take a 
sheet of paper and divide it into four columns. The first 
column is the "problem" column, the second is headed "objec- 
tives," the third "methods," and the fourth "evaluation." 
Describe the problem in the first column, and then list all 
objectives separately in the second. The listing of objectives 
will make it easy to determine the following: 
1. whether each contributes to the solution of the 
problem; 
2. what specific methods could accomplish the objective; 
3. what criterion of success is necessary in reaching 
the objectives; and 
4. which method of evaluation is most appropriate. 
This helps to show whether the proposal is dealing effectively 
with the problem, whether the objectives relate to the problem, 
whether there is a method for reaching each objective, and 


whether there is an evaluation process to deal with the entire 


program.

Participants 


Who will participate in the project? In contrast to staff 


or consultants, who manage or conduct the project, the term 
"participants" refers to those persons who are measured or 
evaluated or are otherwise affected by the proposed project. 
Participants are sometimes referred to as subjects, population, 
or sample. Describe the participants, if any, in the proposed 
project. Indicate the number of participants to be selected 
and the eligibility criteria. For example, does the project 
involve seventh grade students, seventh grade females, or 
eighth grade students who qualify for Title I program? Consider 
special characteristics of the participants such as age, educa- 
tional level, ethnic origin, rurality, socioeconomic levels, 


program participation, and/or sex. Note any generalization of 


23 


the findings which will apply. Make certain that findings 


or results will not be biased by these variables. 


How will participants for the project be selected? 


Describe the group of subjects from which the participants in 
the project will be drawn. If a sampling method is used, 
provide rationale for the type of sampling (such as stratified, 
clustered, random). If an entire unit of some type (such as 

a third period class; all classes within a particular subject 
area; all classes within a program area) is selected to partic- 


ipate, explain the reason for its selection. 


REMEMBER: The reviewer will want to know who is to be selected 
to participate in the study, how participants will be selected, 


and why it is important that these persons be included.

Facilities and Equipment 


The proposal should include a description of any special 
facilities or equipment that will be requi:zed to azcomplish 


the objectives. Indicate the rationale for the purchase or 


rental of required equipment or facilities, if necessary, in 
terms of the project objectives. Also consider whether or 
not the equipment or facilities could be made available on 
some basis other than rental or purchase, such as by a 


cooperative arrangement. NOTE: Approval for equipment pur-

What description of facilities is necessary? Describe 


library, office, or other special facilities which will aid 

in conducting the project. Indicate the method and criteria

Data Collection and Analysis 


In developing procedures it is crucial to plan for data 
collection and analysis early. Information to be gathered must 
be relevant to the objectives--what is to be accomplished--and 
a systematic plan for obtaining and interpreting it should be 
developed. This will prevent the collection of inappropriate 
data. 

The importance of data collection and analysis is somewhat 
determined by project design. An experimental design including 
selection of experimental and control groups and identification 
and manipulation of variables might involve a substantially 
greater amount of data than in a curriculum development project. 


Selection of instruments to obtain data, chosen for their 


appropriateness for the task, should be done as early as possible. 


Description and/or inclusion of the instruments in the final 
proposal may aid in convincing reviewers of the care of the 
planning. If no instruments presently exist to measure antici- 
pated changes, they will have to be adapted or developed. 
Describe procedures and conditions for collecting data. 

Analysis and interpretation of the data will be essential 
to the successful! communication of project results. It will be 
necessary to determine validity (that the right things are meas- 


ured) and reliability (that the measures are consistent) of 


the findings. Many school districts or institutions have an 
evaluation or computing specialist who may be helpful in 
planning or collecting data and translating it into a meaning- 
ful form. Identify and contact that person during the planning 


stages of the proposal. 


Evaluation 


Why evaluate? Project evaluation serves three purposes: 


(1) to provide feedback necessary to make appropriate changes 
in the project as it proceeds (formative evaluation), (2) to 
monitor progress in relation to the proposed timeline, and 
(3) to determine how effective the project is in achieving 
the objectives. Evaluation may be internal (project staff) or 
external (either a contracted outside agent or funding source 
monitors). Both are needed. Internal evaluation has the 
advantage of immediate feedback; however, external evaluation 
may offer greater objectivity and, therefore, greater credi- 
bility. The proposal writer is concerned at this point with 
evaluation which project staff initiates--evaluation rather 
than monitoring. 

The evaluation section should include a description of 
the methods intended to measure and evaluate attainment of the 
proposed objectives. Considering the objectives identified in 
the proposal and perhaps using the planning method suggested 
earlier, the proposal writer should answer three questions: 

1. What objective evaluation techniques will be used? 

2. How frequently will evaluation occur? 


3. Who will evaluate the project?

Subjective evaluation, such as attitude surveys, may give an

1. interviews;

4. testing instruments, including pre- and post-test

Budget 


What is the purpose of the budget? The budget section 


contains the best estimate of the total cost of the project 

and a breakdown of the kinds of expenses that will be incurred. 
In other words, the budget is an operational statement of the 
project in monetary terms. Discovering a good research idea 
(the problem statement) and devising effective means of inves- 
tigating it (the objectives and methodology) are the challeng- 
ing parts of writing a propsal. A budget is simply a monetary 
interpretation of these steps. Once objectives and methodology 
have been determined, preparing a budget is a relatively simple 
matter of interpreting project activities into salaries, travel 
funds, support services, and supplies. NOTE: Especially in 
the early stages a budget is an estimate. It may be adjusted 
or fine-tuned at any point in the project to be a more accurate 
reflection of actual expenditure. 


Why is the budget important? Some project reviewers 


consider the budget to be one of the most important sections 


of the proposal. After a well-developed and clearly-written 


problem statement, it is the budget section which often reveals 
the quality of planning that has gone into the proposal. Some 
of the criteria by which a budget may be judged are these: 
1. How do the categories and specific expenses relate 
to the proposal? 
Are the amounts of money budgeted in each category 


both reasonable and adequate to accomplish the task 


necessary? 


For what time period is the budget written? The budget 


should reflect the expenses for the duration of the project. 

In most cases this will be for a year, although some projects 
may be funded for shorter time periods. In rare instances a 
project may be planned for more than one year, in which case 
the original proposal should itemize expenses for the first 
year and estimate annual totals for each successive year. How- 
ever, a project can be funded for only one year at a time. 
Acceptance of a project that proposes second- and third-year 


continuation does not guarantee subsequent funding. 


Who has fiscal responsibility? Once a project is awarded, 


the fiscal responsibility rests with the institution which 
contracts the research. All projects are subject to institu- 
tional, state, and federal audits. 


Are there restrictions on allowable expenses? Although

Opportunities for Funding 


How does the funding process work? Every year the 


Division of Vocational Education receives federal funds to 

be distributed to local education agencies. Some of the 

funds are shared on a formula basis according to the number 

of eligible students served by the vocational education pro- 
grams. The remainder is set aside by the Division for use 

as discretionary funds. This amount may be distributed at 

the discretion of the Division to support programs or projects 
which fulfili the needs of Florida schools and comply with 


federal guidelines and regulations. Needs addressed hy these 


funds are identified in the Florida State Plan for Vocational 


Education and summarized in the Application for Federal Voca- 


tional Funds. Both documents are provided to county superin- 


tendents and local vocational directors, presidents and 
occupational deans of public community colleges, and presidents 
and department chairmen of vocational education in state uni- 
versities. The vocational administrator in the Department of 
Corrections, the president and principals of the School for the 
Deaf and the Blind, and the chairpersons of the Miccosukee and 
Seminole Indian tribes also receive copies. 


The Application for Federal Vocational Funds defines 


categories of funding, listing amounts of money available in 
each and examples of kinds of projects eligible. Submission 
of this application signals an intent to pursue certain proj- 
ects in vocational education. Proposals for projects are out- 


lined on a five-page Application for Federal Vocational Funds 


Proposal Form. There are two ways to submit a proposal for 


funding. In the first proposals returned to the Division as 
part of the institution's annual plan are reviewed by advisory 
councils and the Vocational Division Administrative Council. 
Upon approval, final proposals are invited. 

Submission of the proposal form is a way of informing the 
Division of an institution's plans for research or develop- 
mental activities. The brief format saves a writer the time 
and effort of producing a full proposal until it can be 
determined whether or not the proposal idea is acceptable. 
Some funding sources accomplish this screening process by re- 
quiring a prospectus, a brief narrative of intent. However, 
for Division funds, the proposal forms provide a uniform pro- 
cedure for sounding out one's ideas, performing the same func- 
tion as a prospectus. 

The second way to submit a proposal is by amendment to 
the funding application. Again, a preliminary proposal is 
submitted to the Division noting that it is an amendment and 
signed by the chief school officer. Review by the appropriate 
councils is required, and approval leads to the invitation of 
a final proposal. Approval of amendments is dependent on the 
availability of appropriate funds. 

Anyone wishing to write a proposal should be familiar with 


the information contained in the Application for Federal Voca- 


tional Funds. Once a project idea has been developed, the 


proposal writer should work through the proper channels in his/ 


her own institution to submit a proposal. Proposals are not 


to be submitted directly to the Division of Vocational Education

What is meant by "sex equity’? In order to qualify for 


federal funding, institutions are required to sign assurances 
of non-discrimination on the basis of sex. Such assurances 
are further specified in laws regarding the disbursement of 
vocational education funds. Programs in vocational educa- 
tion must be both available and easily accessible to persons 
of either sex. 

In reading proposals, reviewers are looking for evidence 
of sex equity in both the nature of the project and the lan- 
guage of the proposal itself. On first reading, reviewers 
will check to see that the project description uses neutral 
language and avoids stereotyped and biased references. 

Reflecting the nature of the project, objectives will 
be examined to assure that they are free of sex bias, stereo- 
typing, or discrimination. Program design should be charac- 
terized by availability to females and males. Availability 
must be documented either by specific evidence of special 
services to ensure equal access to the program or by evidence 
of support services to overcome the results of previous sex 


stereotyping and/or discriminatory programming.
