# Gordon & Poulin 2009: NSERC Peer Review Cost vs Baseline Grant

Influential cost-accounting argument that the time and money spent on NSERC peer review exceeds what would be spent simply giving every qualified Canadian scientist a baseline discovery grant.

## The Cost-Accounting Calculation

For NSERC's Discovery Grant program in 2007:
- **Cost per failed application** (preparation + review): ~$40,000 CAD
- **Average baseline discovery grant**: ~$30,000 CAD
- **Conclusion**: Eliminate peer review, give baseline grants to all qualified investigators, save money AND fund more research

## Components of "Cost per Failed Application"

1. **Applicant time** preparing the proposal (6-8 weeks of professor time)
2. **Reviewer time** for external review and panel discussion
3. **Administrative overhead** of NSERC running the competition
4. **Opportunity cost** of researcher time not spent on research
5. **Resubmission cycles** — many proposals require multiple submission rounds

## Implicit Anti-Rubric (what the analysis criticizes)

Gordon & Poulin's analysis implies that NSERC's elaborate rubric (excellence of researcher, merit of proposal, HQP training — see NSERC entry) is operating in a context where:

1. **Funding rates are too high** for fine-grained ranking to add value (~70%+ rate)
2. **Marginal differences between proposals don't justify** the cost of distinguishing them
3. **The rubric serves administrative legitimacy** more than allocation efficiency
4. **Per-proposal effort exceeds per-grant value** for marginal applicants

## Proposed Alternative Rubric (the baseline grant)

Replace competitive Discovery Grants with:
1. **Eligibility threshold** — qualified investigator at Canadian university
2. **Standard funding amount** — same grant for all qualified
3. **Elimination of fine-grained ranking** — no review beyond eligibility
4. **Periodic continuation review** — based on past grant productivity, not new proposals
5. **Separate competitive program** for ABOVE-baseline funding for ambitious projects

## Counter-Arguments (Roorda 2009 and others)

Roorda (2009) challenged the cost estimates:
- Argued Gordon & Poulin overestimated costs by factor of ~23
- The "real cost" of NSERC peer review may be <5% of a baseline grant
- The rubric serves multiple functions beyond allocation (training, signaling, quality control)

## Implications for Rubric Design

Gordon & Poulin's analysis raises a fundamental question for any grant rubric:
1. **What is the marginal value** of fine-grained ranking vs simple eligibility?
2. **What is the cost** of preparing applications and conducting reviews?
3. **Does the rubric's allocation efficiency** justify its overhead?

The argument is most powerful for high-funding-rate programs (e.g., NSERC at ~70%) where the marginal applicant differs little from the funded applicant. It is weaker for low-funding-rate programs (NIH at 20%) where there is substantial difference between marginal vs funded applicants — though even there the question of "is fine-grained ranking statistically defensible" remains (per Pier 2018, Fang 2016).

## Connection to Modified Lottery Argument

Gordon & Poulin's baseline-grant proposal is a more radical version of the modified-lottery proposal (Fang & Casadevall 2016): both argue that rubric-based ranking has limited validity at the cost of significant overhead. The modified lottery preserves competitive selection at a coarse level (meritorious vs not); the baseline-grant proposal eliminates competitive selection entirely.
