# Harvard Catalyst - Responding to an NIH Summary Statement

## Understanding the Summary Statement
An NIH summary statement includes:
- Overall impact score (or unscored designation)
- Reviewer assigned to your application
- Strengths and weaknesses
- Areas of concern
- Recommended budget
- Short synopsis based on peer reviewer critiques (prepared by Scientific Review Officer)

## Decoding Reviewer Feedback

When analyzing reviewer comments, assess:

### 1. Did Reviewers Find the Topic Significant?
- Did they seem excited about the research (read between the lines)
- Look for words like "important," "novel," "timely," "significant"
- Or warning signs: "incremental," "well-trodden," "unclear significance"

### 2. Are Problems Fixable or Conceptual?
- **Fixable problems**: missing detail, unclear figure, methods question
- **Conceptual problems**: research not state-of-the-art, fundamental flaw in hypothesis
- **Fatal flaws**: unprovable hypothesis, ethical concerns, wrong study design entirely

### 3. Did Reviewers Misunderstand?
- Some criticisms may stem from unclear writing
- Misunderstandings can be addressed by clarification
- Multiple reviewers raising same misunderstanding signals deeper writing problem

### 4. Convergent vs. Divergent Critiques
- Convergent: All reviewers raised same concern → must address
- Divergent: One reviewer's idiosyncratic critique → address but with judgment
- Conflicting: Reviewers disagree → acknowledge, choose direction

## Steps for Responding

### Step 1: Wait Before Reacting
- Do not overreact; resist the urge to immediately fire off an emotional email
- Wait at least 24-48 hours before drafting any response
- Take notes on initial reactions but do not act on them

### Step 2: Contact Your Program Officer
- As soon as you receive your summary statement, contact your program officer
- Ask about the probability of funding (depending on percentile/payline)
- Get advice on what to do if your application scores outside the funding range
- Ask if they attended the review meeting and can provide additional insight

### Step 3: Determine Resubmission Strategy
- If close to payline, may resubmit with modest changes
- If far from payline, fundamental rethinking needed
- If a true fatal flaw, consider new direction entirely

### Step 4: Prepare Your Resubmission Introduction
The 1-page Introduction to Resubmission should:
- Summarize the reviewers' criticisms
- Address them all politely and constructively
- Use "We thank the reviewers for their thoughtful comments..."
- Provide reasons if you can't address a concern or disagree
- Don't simply ignore criticisms

### Step 5: Strengthen Your Application
- Revise weaknesses identified by reviewers
- Be careful not to change elements the reviewers liked
- Highlight ongoing work by including new findings or new preliminary data
- Update biosketch and references with newest publications
- Add new collaborators if expertise gap was noted

### Step 6: Get Outside Eyes
- Have colleagues read both summary statement and revised application
- They can spot if you adequately addressed reviewer concerns
- Mock review with mentors if possible

## Key Statistics
NIH data show that resubmitted applications have higher success rates than initial submissions do.

## What NOT to Do
- Don't argue with reviewers
- Don't make ad hominem attacks
- Don't change too much (or you'll make it a different proposal)
- Don't ignore reviewer concerns
- Don't appeal/protest unless there's procedural error (rarely successful)
