# Bornmann, Mutz & Daniel 2007: Meta-Analysis of Gender Differences in Grant Peer Review

Meta-analysis of 21 empirical studies of grant peer review across multiple countries and funding agencies, testing for systematic gender effects.

## Studies Included

Geographic and agency coverage:
- US (NIH, NSF)
- Sweden (Swedish Medical Research Council — Wennerås & Wold)
- UK (MRC, Wellcome Trust)
- Germany (DFG, Bf F)
- Netherlands (NWO)
- Australia (NHMRC, ARC)
- Austria (FWF)
- Canada (NSERC, CIHR)

## Outcome Variable

Probability of grant award given application; effect size = log odds ratio of male vs. female success.

## Bias Variables Studied as Moderators

The meta-analysis investigated whether gender effects vary by:
1. **Funding type** — research grants vs fellowships vs scholarships
2. **Country / system**
3. **Field** — life sciences, physical sciences, social sciences, humanities
4. **Career stage** — junior vs senior applicants
5. **Year of study** — testing for trend over time
6. **Applicant pool size**

## Main Findings (the bias rubric)

- **Pooled effect**: men had ~7% greater odds of grant success than women.
- **Effect size: d = 0.04** (small but statistically significant).
- **Substantial heterogeneity** across studies — some show clear bias, some show none.

## Heterogeneity Sources Identified

- Effect was **larger in older studies** — newer agencies may have reduced bias.
- Effect was **larger for fellowships than for project grants** in some cases.
- Effect was **larger when reviewers had access to applicant identity** vs anonymized review.
- **Field effects** were inconsistent — no single field uniformly worse.

## Implicit Quality Criteria Where Bias Operates

The meta-analysis suggests gender bias operates through:
1. **Investigator-quality criterion** — perceptions of competence/promise
2. **CV evaluation** — identical CVs read differently for women
3. **Letter-of-recommendation language** — gendered descriptors
4. **Productivity expectations** — "raw" publication counts may favor men due to baseline disparities (career interruptions, etc.)

## Implications for Rubric Design

- Gender bias is real but modest in pooled estimates; large effects in particular contexts.
- Anonymizing applicants reduces measured bias.
- Rubrics that emphasize the investigator (CV, letters) generate larger gender gaps than those emphasizing the science.
- Reform suggestions:
  - Anonymize where possible
  - Audit historical decisions for gender effects
  - De-emphasize unstructured CV evaluation in favor of operationalized criteria
  - Track effects of reform efforts longitudinally

## Methodological Note

Subsequent meta-analyses (e.g., Marsh et al. 2009, larger N) found smaller and less consistent effects, but the qualitative finding — that bias is detectable but modest in pooled data — has held up.
