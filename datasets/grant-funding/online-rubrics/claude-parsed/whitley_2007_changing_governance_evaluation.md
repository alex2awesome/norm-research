# Whitley & Gläser 2007: The Changing Governance of the Sciences

Edited volume on the proliferation of research evaluation systems internationally. Provides cross-national comparison of evaluation rubrics and their effects on scientific knowledge production.

## Five Key Characteristics of Research Evaluation Systems (Whitley's framework)

Whitley identifies dimensions that vary across national evaluation systems:

1. **Frequency** — annual, periodic (REF every 5-7 years), continuous, or one-off.
2. **Formalization** — explicit rubrics with operational definitions vs informal expert judgment.
3. **Standardization** — same criteria across fields/institutions vs tailored.
4. **Transparency** — public criteria and data vs closed processes.
5. **Effect on funding** — direct (REF allocates QR funding) vs indirect (informational only).

## Context Factors That Mediate System Functioning

How a research evaluation system operates depends on:
1. **Variety of funders** — monopoly funder (UK historically) vs diverse (US)
2. **Standing of scientific elites** — strong elite control vs democratic
3. **Degree of organizational autonomy** — universities free or state-controlled
4. **Field-specific norms** — humanities vs life sciences norms differ
5. **National science policy traditions** — Mertonian norms vs more managerial cultures

## Cross-National Cases Examined

### United Kingdom (RAE/REF)
- High frequency (every 5-7 years), high formalization, high stake (block funding)
- Effect: cultural transformation of UK academic publishing toward "quality over quantity"
- Significant gaming behaviors documented

### Australia (ERA)
- Modeled partly on UK REF
- Discipline-specific journal rankings (later abandoned due to gaming)
- Effect: substantial reorganization of departmental research strategies

### Germany (Excellence Initiative)
- One-off competitive evaluation for "Universities of Excellence"
- Effect: concentration of elite funding, restructuring of university hierarchies
- Resistance from established research culture

### Italy (VQR)
- Bibliometric-heavy evaluation tied to ministerial funding
- Effect: aggressive optimization of publication patterns
- Identified problems with field-normalization and gaming

### France (AERES, later HCERES)
- Government agency assessment of universities and labs
- Effect: integration into broader research-policy reform
- Mixed reception by scientific community

## Effects of Research Evaluation Systems

The volume documents systemic effects:
1. **Strategic reorientation** — researchers and institutions optimize for what's measured
2. **Concentration of funding** — winners-take-more dynamics
3. **Conservatism** — risk-averse research strategies become rational
4. **Disciplinary homogenization** — pressure toward fields/methods that score well
5. **Career-stage stratification** — established researchers benefit; early-career researchers face pressure
6. **Cross-national convergence** — countries copy each other's systems, often without local adaptation

## Implications for Grant Rubric Design

A grant rubric is not isolated — it sits within a national/international evaluation ecosystem:

1. **A rubric's effects depend on its institutional context** more than its content.
2. **Convergent rubrics across funders** create compounding incentive effects on researchers.
3. **High-stake rubrics produce gaming**; low-stake rubrics may be ignored.
4. **Transparency is double-edged** — enables auditing but also enables gaming.
5. **Periodic rubric revision** is essential because systems learn to game stable rubrics.

## Connection to Other Frameworks

- **DORA / Leiden Manifesto**: principles for responsible metrics use
- **Lamont 2009**: how individual panels actually work within these systems
- **Luukkonen 2012**: how the ERC's mission-driven rubric interacts with operational practice
- **NIH Simplified Framework 2025**: example of major rubric reform within an established system
