# Wessely 1998: Peer Review of Grant Applications — What Do We Know?

Influential Lancet review article (note: source listed as Wave 1 was wrong year — this is the 1998 piece) that synthesized the empirical literature on grant peer review available at that point. (Wave 1 already has a Wessely 1998 file; this entry adds the explicit criteria framework.)

## The Problem Wessely Frames

Since the 1940s and 1950s, peer review has become the principal mechanism for allocating scientific research resources. Peer review of grant proposals is more consequential than peer review of publications, because rejected grants represent science that is *never done*.

## What the Pre-1998 Literature Said About Grant Review Criteria

Reviewers across systems were typically asked to consider:

1. **Scientific quality / originality** of the question
2. **Research approach / methodology** — appropriateness, rigor
3. **Applicant track record / qualifications**
4. **Feasibility** of proposed work in the time/budget
5. **Significance** of expected results to the field
6. **Resources / institutional environment** for the work
7. **Cost-effectiveness** of the request

## What the Empirical Evidence Showed (Wessely's synthesis)

- **Inter-reviewer reliability is low** (echoing Cole et al. 1981).
- **Predictive validity is weak** — reviewer scores poorly predict subsequent productivity.
- **Bias is sometimes documented** but inconsistent across studies — gender effects vary by system.
- **Conservatism dominates** — high-risk proposals systematically disadvantaged.
- **Time costs are massive** — both for applicants and reviewers.
- **Few experimental tests** of alternative procedures had been conducted.

## Reform Suggestions Wessely Endorsed

1. Lottery elements for proposals in the borderline / fundable zone.
2. Explicit risk-tolerance criteria.
3. Audit of bias variables.
4. Reduction in proposal length / preparation burden.
5. More comparative (forced-ranking) and fewer absolute scoring schemes.

## Implications for Rubric Design

Wessely's piece is a watershed: it gathered the prior 40+ years of research and concluded that the standard rubric (significance/approach/PI/feasibility) was too unreliable to be trusted in its current implementation. Most modern empirical research on grant peer review (Lauer, Pier, Marsh, Erosheva, Hoppe) builds on this critique.
