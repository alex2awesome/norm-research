# Boudreau et al. 2016: Intellectual Distance, Novelty, and Resource Allocation

Field experiment that randomly assigned reviewer-proposal pairs at a leading research university (N = 2,130 evaluator-proposal pairs). Tests how intellectual distance between reviewer expertise and proposal content affects scoring.

## Experimental Design

- Designed and executed a real grant-proposal competition at a major research university
- **Randomized assignment** of evaluators to proposals (escaping selection bias of normal review)
- 2,130 evaluator-proposal pairs created
- Each proposal received multiple independent evaluations
- Intellectual distance measured via topic overlap between evaluator's prior publications and proposal content

## Two Key Findings (the dual bias rubric)

### Finding 1: Closer Expertise → Lower Scores
Evaluators give **systematically lower scores to proposals close to their own expertise**.
- Effect size: substantial (~1 point on a 10-point scale for close vs. distant proposals)
- Possible mechanisms: harsher standards for one's own area; threat from competitors; better detection of weaknesses

### Finding 2: Novelty Penalty
Highly novel research proposals also received **systematically lower scores**.
- Novelty measured by combinations of citation patterns and language unusual for the field
- The novelty penalty operates independently of intellectual distance
- Both effects compound: novel proposals close to evaluator expertise face the largest penalty

## Implicit Quality Criteria Reviewers Apply (revealed by the study)

When evaluating proposals, reviewers (across academic fields) implicitly weight:

1. **Conformity to existing knowledge** — proposals fitting current paradigm score better
2. **Familiar methods** — proposals using techniques the reviewer recognizes
3. **Predictable outcomes** — proposals with clear expected results
4. **Distance from reviewer's exact area** — proposals slightly outside (but understandable to) the reviewer score better than proposals exactly in or far from the reviewer's area

## The "Goldilocks Zone" of Reviewer Expertise

The data suggest an optimal intellectual distance between reviewer and proposal:
- Too close = reviewer over-critical, sees competitors, applies own field's harsh standards
- Too far = reviewer cannot evaluate, defaults to surface features
- Moderate distance = reviewer can engage but not threaten/overcriticize

## Implications for Rubric Design

1. The reviewer-proposal matching algorithm matters as much as the rubric content.
2. Pure "match by expertise" minimizes Type I errors (funding bad proposals) but maximizes Type II errors (rejecting good novel proposals).
3. Novelty rubrics that REWARD novelty must counteract the documented novelty penalty in baseline reviewer behavior.
4. Triangulation by reviewers at varying distances may be more robust than concentration of close-expertise reviewers.
5. The findings explain why "high-risk, high-reward" funding programs have struggled — standard peer review structurally penalizes novelty.
