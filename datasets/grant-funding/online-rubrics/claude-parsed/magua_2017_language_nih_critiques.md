# Magua et al. 2017: Language in NIH Critiques and Gender Bias

Text-mining + qualitative analysis of NIH R01 reviewer critiques from a single institution. Tests whether evaluative language differs between reviews of male vs female applicants.

## Methodological Approach

- Algorithmic text mining of full reviewer critiques (R01 summary statements)
- LIWC (Linguistic Inquiry and Word Count) categorization
- Qualitative coding of evaluative themes
- Comparison of language used for male vs female applicants

## Linguistic Categories Analyzed (the language rubric)

### Evaluative descriptors counted
1. **"Standout" / superlative language** — "outstanding", "exceptional", "remarkable"
2. **Ability descriptors** — "talented", "skilled", "capable"
3. **Achievement descriptors** — "accomplished", "productive", "impressive"
4. **Promise descriptors** — "promising", "potential", "developing"
5. **Hedging language** — "may", "could", "appears to"
6. **Negative descriptors** — "weak", "limited", "concerning"

### Categories of references
1. References to research / project (impersonal)
2. References to PI personally (gendered pronouns)
3. References to PI's training environment / mentors
4. References to team

## Key Findings (the gendered language rubric)

- **Male applicants disproportionately receive "standout" / superlative language**.
- **Female applicants more often described in terms of "competence" / "productivity"** but not "brilliance" or "exceptional ability."
- Female applicants' critiques contained more **hedging language** even when overall scores were similar.
- Reviewers more often referenced **female PIs as "team members"** vs. independent investigators.
- Male PIs more often described as **"creative"** or **"innovative"** independent of similar productivity.

## Implicit Bias Operationalized

The findings parallel the broader literature on letters of recommendation and tenure evaluations: the same objective achievements get described in different vocabulary depending on the applicant's gender, and that vocabulary then shapes downstream impact-score judgments.

## Implications for Rubric Design

1. Even with the SAME formal scoring rubric, **the verbal critique mediates the score**.
2. Linguistic auditing of historical critiques can detect bias the formal rubric does not.
3. Reform options:
   - Train reviewers on language patterns
   - Provide structured templates that constrain descriptive language
   - Have a second-pass linguistic review of critique language before scores are finalized
   - Use NLP-based bias detection on submitted critiques

## Connection to Erosheva (2020) and Witteman (2019)

Magua et al. provides the linguistic mechanism that Erosheva 2020 (criterion-score disparities) and Witteman 2019 (PI-focused criteria amplify gender gaps) document at the outcome level. The vocabulary reviewers use is a critical intermediate variable in producing scored disparities.
