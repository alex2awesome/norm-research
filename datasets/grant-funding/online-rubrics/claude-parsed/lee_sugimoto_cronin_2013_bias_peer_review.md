# Lee, Sugimoto, Cronin & Zhang 2013: Bias in Peer Review

Systematic review of empirical research on bias in peer review (covering both publication and grant peer review). Provides a taxonomy of bias types and assesses evidence for each.

## Bias Types Cataloged (the bias taxonomy rubric)

### Bias Tied to Reviewer Identity
1. **Cognitive cronyism / particularism** — reviewer favors proposals using their own theoretical/methodological commitments (Travis & Collins 1991)
2. **Affiliative bias** — reviewer favors institutions/networks they belong to
3. **Disciplinary bias** — reviewer favors their own subfield
4. **Conservatism** — reviewer favors incremental over revolutionary work

### Bias Tied to Author Identity
5. **Gender bias** — male applicants/authors favored (Wennerås & Wold 1997, Bornmann et al. 2007)
6. **Race/ethnicity bias** — white applicants/authors favored (Ginther 2011, Erosheva 2020)
7. **Nationality bias** — favoring applicants from "central" vs "peripheral" countries
8. **Institutional prestige bias** — favoring elite institutions
9. **Career stage bias** — favoring established vs early-career researchers (or vice versa)
10. **Language bias** — favoring native English speakers

### Bias Tied to Content
11. **Novelty penalty** — penalizing high-risk innovative work (Boudreau 2016)
12. **Methodological bias** — favoring quantitative over qualitative or vice versa
13. **Topic bias** — favoring fashionable over unfashionable topics
14. **Publication bias** — favoring positive over null results (esp. in publication review)

### Bias in the Process Itself
15. **Reviewer-applicant assignment bias** — non-random matching introduces systematic effects
16. **Halo effect from prior funding** — past success predicts current success regardless of merit
17. **Anchoring** — first reviewer's score influences later reviewers
18. **Group dynamic bias** — panel deliberation amplifies dominant voices

## Three Approaches to Studying Bias (methodology rubric)

1. **Empirical** — test for systematic effects in real review data
2. **Methodological** — study how the review process produces variable outcomes
3. **Normative** — what SHOULD count as bias (vs. legitimate quality judgment)

## Critique of the Bias-Research Literature

Lee et al. argue the bias literature has problems:
- **Conceptual confusion** — "bias" used to mean different things in different studies
- **Inconsistent operationalization** — what counts as "the same proposal" varies
- **Underspecification of the counterfactual** — what would unbiased review produce?
- **Difficulty separating bias from disagreement** — when reviewers disagree, how do we know which is "biased"?

## Reform Alternatives Reviewed

1. **Double-blind review** — anonymize authors and reviewers
2. **Open review** — make reviewer identities public
3. **Post-publication peer review** — community judgment after dissemination
4. **Lottery-based allocation** — randomization removes individual bias
5. **Algorithmic decision aids** — structured decision support tools
6. **Diversity in reviewer panels** — heterogeneous panels reduce monoculture

## Implications for Rubric Design

A rubric that aspires to be unbiased must:
1. Define operationally what "bias" means in its context.
2. Audit historical decisions for each bias type.
3. Recognize that some "biases" reflect legitimate quality differences vs. unjust prejudice — rubric must distinguish.
4. Build in structural protections (anonymization, randomization, diverse panels) that don't depend on individual reviewer awareness.
