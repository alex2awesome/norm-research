# Langfeldt, Reymert & Aksnes 2024: Distrust in Grant Peer Review

Qualitative study of researcher distrust in grant peer review across European systems. Identifies sources of distrust and proposed remedies.

## Comparative Trust Findings

Researchers trust grant peer review:
- **Less than journal peer review**
- **Less than colleagues' assessment of their work**
- **Less than they trust their own self-assessment**

This is striking because grant decisions have larger career consequences than publication decisions.

## Sources of Distrust (the failure rubric)

### 1. Low Success Rates Compound Distrust
- Lower success rates → more proposals → harder to distinguish best
- Increased reviewer burden → less time per proposal
- Increased frustration → lower trust in process
- Vicious cycle of distrust generation

### 2. Reviewer Competence Concerns
- Specialized fields may lack reviewers with appropriate expertise
- Mismatch between proposal and reviewer expertise generates distrust
- Small fields have high reviewer-applicant familiarity (perceived conflicts)

### 3. Variability and Subjectivity
- Reviewers apply criteria inconsistently
- Different reviewers weight criteria differently
- Same proposal scored differently by different reviewers
- Researchers experience this as unfair / random

### 4. Perceived Reviewer Bias
- Favoritism observed in others' decisions
- Inappropriate behavior witnessed (conflicts of interest, retaliation)
- Old-boy network perception persists

### 5. Procedural Opacity
- Researchers don't know how reviewers were selected
- Don't see reviews before final decision
- Cannot rebut errors of fact
- Cannot understand why specific decision was made

## Proposed Remedies (the legitimacy rubric)

### Transparency Requirements
1. **Public information** about selection procedures
2. **Public information** about review panel composition
3. **Public criteria** for evaluation
4. **Adequate feedback** to applicants explaining decision

### Procedural Improvements
1. **Reviewer competence verification** — reviewers must be in same field as proposal
2. **Reviewer impartiality safeguards** — close enough for thorough review, distant enough for objectivity
3. **Allow applicants to suggest competent experts**
4. **Allow applicants to respond to reviews** before final decision
5. **Multi-stage review** with applicant rebuttal opportunity

### Structural Changes
1. **Diverse reviewer panels** to reduce monoculture bias
2. **Periodic review of review processes** for emerging gaming
3. **Training reviewers** on rubric application
4. **Calibrating reviewers** with shared training cases

## Implications for Rubric Design

A rubric is not just an evaluation tool — it is a **legitimacy artifact**. To be trusted by researchers, a rubric must:

1. **Be transparent** about criteria and weights
2. **Include procedural safeguards** (rebuttal, expertise matching)
3. **Be applied consistently** across reviewers
4. **Provide meaningful feedback** to unsuccessful applicants
5. **Be auditable** for bias and gaming

## Key Insight

The empirical literature (Pier 2018, Fang 2016) shows peer review has limited reliability. The Langfeldt et al. study shows researchers KNOW this and respond with distrust. A rubric that pretends to more precision than it has — claims of fine-grained ranking, certainty, expert objectivity — actively undermines its own legitimacy.

A more honest rubric (acknowledging uncertainty, providing rebuttal opportunities, using lottery for borderline cases) might paradoxically be MORE trusted because it doesn't claim precision the data doesn't support.
