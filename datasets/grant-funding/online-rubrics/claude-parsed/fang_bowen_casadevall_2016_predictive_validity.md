# Fang, Bowen & Casadevall 2016: Predictive Validity of NIH Scores

Bibliometric analysis of 102,740 funded NIH grants showing percentile scores have only weak ability to predict grant productivity within the funded range.

## Productivity Outcome Measures (the validity rubric)

1. **Citation counts** of grant-supported publications.
2. **Number of publications** acknowledging the grant.
3. **Median citations per publication**.
4. **Maximum citations** to top paper.
5. **Citation rate normalized** by field, year, and article type.
6. **Citation productivity above the median** (binary outcome).

## Key Findings

- For citation productivity > median, **AUC = 0.54 (95% CI: 0.53-0.54)** — barely above chance (0.5).
- Discrimination is somewhat better when comparing the full range of percentile scores (top 1st percentile vs 50th), but it **collapses to near-random in the critical 3rd-20th percentile range** — exactly the range where funding decisions actually happen.
- Percentile scores cannot reliably distinguish "very good" from "outstanding" grants.

## Implicit Quality Criteria Embedded in NIH Scoring (that fail to predict)

The NIH percentile score aggregates reviewer judgments on:
- Significance
- Investigator
- Innovation
- Approach
- Environment

None of these, individually or combined, reliably predicts subsequent citation productivity within the fundable range.

## Implications for Rubric Design

- Fine-grained percentile distinctions are **not statistically defensible** within the fundable zone.
- A rubric for grant review should either (a) accept that decisions in the fundable range are essentially indistinguishable and proceed by lottery / random selection, or (b) define success metrics other than citations.
- Reform implication: the entire infrastructure of percentile-based ranking creates an illusion of precision the underlying signal does not support.

## Methodological Note

Counter-arguments include: citations are an imperfect proxy for grant productivity; high-risk grants may have high variance in outcomes; grant scores may predict other outcomes (training, knowledge translation) better than citations.
