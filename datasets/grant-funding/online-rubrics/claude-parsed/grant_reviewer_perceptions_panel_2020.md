# Pier et al. 2020: Reviewer Perceptions of Panel Discussion Quality

Mixed-methods survey of NIH grant reviewers about their experiences with panel discussion in study sections. Complements Pier 2018 PNAS paper on inter-rater agreement.

## Discussion Quality Construct (the perceptual rubric)

Reviewers were asked to evaluate "discussion quality" along multiple dimensions:

### Discussion Quality Defined
1. **Openness** — all panel members can speak
2. **Balance** — discussion not dominated by one or two voices
3. **Lack of bias** — discussion stays on scientific merit
4. **Focus** — stays on key points rather than tangential issues
5. **Efficiency** — appropriate time per proposal

### Discussion Effectiveness Defined
1. **Reviewer participation** — both assigned and unassigned reviewers contribute
2. **Clarification of opinions** — assigned reviewers' positions are clarified
3. **Whole panel informed** — unassigned reviewers know enough to score after discussion

## Findings — Positive Reviewer Perceptions

Reviewers generally rated panel discussions favorably on:
1. **Participation** — most reviewers felt they had opportunity to contribute
2. **Clarification of differing opinions** — discussion helped surface disagreements
3. **Informing unassigned reviewers** — discussion brought non-experts up to speed
4. **Chair facilitation** — most chairs ran discussions effectively
5. **Influence on review outcome** — most reviewers felt discussion affected scores
6. **Choice of "best science"** — reviewers believed discussion helped select better proposals
7. **General fairness and balance** — discussion seen as procedurally fair

## Findings — Negative Reviewer Perceptions

Concerns identified:
1. **Uneven focus** — some proposals get extensive discussion, others perfunctory
2. **Limited unassigned reviewer participation** — non-assigned members may stay silent
3. **Short discussion times** — esp. in late-meeting fatigue periods
4. **Dominance by senior reviewers** — junior reviewers may defer
5. **Anchoring** — first reviewer's score influences subsequent discussion

## Important Tension with Pier 2018 Finding

This study found reviewers BELIEVE discussion improves outcomes; Pier 2018 found discussion does NOT improve agreement. This is a "perception-vs-reality" finding:

- Reviewers value the deliberative process
- Reviewers feel it improves decisions
- Empirical analysis of scoring shows it doesn't actually improve reliability
- The deliberation may serve OTHER functions (consensus-building, training, legitimacy) that justify it

## Implications for Rubric Design

1. **Procedural legitimacy is real even when reliability gains are illusory**.
2. **Reviewers value participation more than they value precision**.
3. **Panel discussions can be retained** for non-reliability functions while:
   - Acknowledging they don't improve agreement (Fogelholm 2012, Pier 2018)
   - Using simpler aggregation rules where reliability is the goal
   - Investing in chair training to mitigate domination patterns
4. **A rubric that depends on panel discussion** must specify what it expects discussion to do — and ensure discussion serves that purpose rather than illusory reliability.
