# Berg (NIGMS) 2010-2011: Productivity Metrics vs Peer Review Scores

Series of data analyses by then-NIGMS Director Jeremy Berg posted on the NIGMS Feedback Loop Blog. Examined the relationship between NIH peer review scores and subsequent grant productivity — pre-dated the formal Fang/Bowen/Casadevall 2016 study.

## Data Analyzed

- **789 R01 grants** competitively funded by NIGMS in FY2006
- All received both priority scores and percentile scores during peer review
- **6,554 publications** linked to these grants between October 2006 and September 2010
- **79,295 total citations** as of April 2011
- Median: 7 publications per grant; 73 citations per grant

## Productivity Metrics Analyzed (the validity rubric)

1. **Number of publications per grant**
2. **Total citations per grant**
3. **Citations per publication**
4. **Publication rate over time**
5. **Highest-cited publication count**

## Key Findings

- **Peer review scores DO correlate with productivity** — relationship is statistically different from random ordering.
- **BUT substantial variation in productivity occurs at every score level** — grants with similar peer review scores show wide range of productivity.
- The correlation is detectable but **weak** — scores in the top decile vs second decile do not reliably predict productivity differences.
- **Lab size analysis**: midsize labs (3-6 grants) most productive per dollar; very small and very large labs less efficient.
- **Multiple grants per PI**: returns diminish as the same PI accumulates more grants.

## Implications for NIH Funding Policy

Berg's analyses informed NIH's later policies:
1. **Cap on per-PI grant total** (NIGMS implementation of MIRA program)
2. **Diversity-of-investigator focus** (Next Generation Researchers Initiative, Lauer 2017)
3. **Skepticism about fine-grained percentile distinctions** in funding decisions
4. **Recognition that citation-based productivity** is the right outcome to track

## Implications for Rubric Design

Berg's analyses (academic in style, foundational in influence on NIH policy) showed that:
1. The NIH percentile-score rubric has SOME predictive validity but it is weak.
2. Rubrics that imply fine-grained ranking are not justified by the data.
3. Lab-size and multi-grant effects suggest funding decisions should consider portfolio effects, not just per-application merit.
4. The "Berg blog" methodology — transparent posting of data and analysis — is itself a rubric for funder accountability that few other agencies have matched.

## Significance

Berg's blog analyses are an unusual hybrid: agency director using academic-style methods to publicly analyze the agency's own decisions. The substantive findings prefigured the academic literature (Fang 2016, Lauer 2015) but they reached policy audiences before peer-reviewed publication. They are the closest the NIH has come to publicly auditing its own rubric.
