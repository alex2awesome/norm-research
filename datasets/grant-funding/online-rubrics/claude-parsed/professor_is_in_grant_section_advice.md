# Karen Kelsky / The Professor Is In — Grant-Related Advice

Karen Kelsky is a former tenured anthropologist who runs a popular academic career advising blog. While not exclusively about grants, she covers the role of grants in academic CVs and job applications.

## Themes Relevant to Grant Strategy

### Grants on the CV
- External grants matter more than internal grants
- Federal/foundation grants matter more than university seed grants
- Listed by total $ amount and PI/Co-PI status
- "Submitted but not funded" should generally be listed only if competitive
- Reviewer service for funders signals seniority

### The Research Statement (Which Doubles as Grant Pitch)
- Open with the big question, not the methods
- Show progression from dissertation to current to future work
- The "future work" section IS your future grant pitch
- Include at least 2-3 named potential funders
- Demonstrate awareness of funding landscape

### Junior Faculty Grant Strategy (Per Kelsky)
- First year: write 2-3 small grants ($5-50K)
- Second year: target a major external grant (NSF, NEH, foundation)
- Third year: have at least one funded major grant
- Tenure clock: 5-6 years means grants must be in pipeline immediately

### Humanities Grant Specifics (Kelsky's Specialty)
- ACLS, NEH, Mellon, SSRC have very different priorities
- Mellon: long-term, transformative, often institutional
- NEH: clear public humanities angle helps
- ACLS: strong scholarly contribution within established field
- SSRC: comparative, international, social-science methodologies

### Common Mistakes Kelsky Sees
- Junior faculty applying for senior fellowships
- Senior faculty applying for junior fellowships out of laziness
- Grant proposals that read like dissertation chapters
- No engagement with current scholarship
- Vague timeline / no clear deliverable

## Implicit Rubric for Humanities Funders

### What Wins
1. Clear, novel argument or contribution to field
2. Demonstrated mastery of relevant scholarship
3. Realistic methodology (archives, fieldwork, interviews)
4. Clear timeline with checkpoints
5. Evidence the project will produce a book or comparable output
6. Strong letters of support from senior scholars
7. Career stage match for the funder

### What Loses
1. "Important new work on X" without specifying the contribution
2. Methodology that's actually a research question disguised
3. Unrealistic timeline (a book in one year)
4. No engagement with archival access logistics
5. Letters from too-junior referees
6. Mismatch with funder's stated priorities

## Kelsky's Big-Picture Advice
- Grants are part of an ecosystem; one grant signals capacity for another
- Apply early and often; rejection is the norm
- Use program officers strategically
- Network through grant-writing workshops
- Build a "grant-writing habit" of one application per quarter

## On the Cover Letter / Project Summary
- Hook with the question, not your methods
- Position yourself as the right person to answer it
- Be specific about contribution to field
- Don't oversell — funders smell hype
- Match length to instructions exactly
