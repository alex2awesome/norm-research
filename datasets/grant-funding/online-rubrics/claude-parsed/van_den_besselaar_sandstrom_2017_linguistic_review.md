# van den Besselaar & Sandström 2018: Linguistic Analysis of Grant Reviews

Analyzes the actual text of grant peer review reports using LIWC-style categorization to identify what reviewers reward, what they punish, and how rhetorical patterns relate to scoring outcomes.

## Linguistic Categories Tested as Predictors of Score

### Positive Predictors (correlated with HIGHER applicant scores)
1. **Positive evaluation words** ("excellent", "innovative", "outstanding")
2. **Superlatives** ("best", "most original")
3. **Affirmation language**

### Negative Predictors (correlated with LOWER applicant scores)
1. **Negation words** ("not", "no", "never")
2. **Negative evaluation words** ("weak", "unclear", "insufficient")
3. **Exclusion words** ("but", "however", "except")
4. **Tentative language** ("maybe", "possibly")

### Neutral / Counterintuitive Findings

- **Research project descriptive words** correlated NEGATIVELY with score: more discussion of the project = lower score (sign of disagreement).
- **Track record discussion** correlated NEGATIVELY: more text about CV = lower score.

These patterns reflect that long discussions in panels tend to be triggered by problems rather than enthusiasm.

## Implicit Reviewer Criteria Surfaced

Through linguistic patterns, the reviewers' actual scoring criteria emerge:

1. **Methodology rigor** — most-discussed weakness category.
2. **Theoretical framing / novelty** — most-discussed strength category.
3. **Track record sufficiency** — frequently mentioned as a marginal concern.
4. **Feasibility of work plan**.
5. **Significance / contribution to field**.

## Panel Behavior Patterns Inferred

1. **Panels concentrate on identifying weakest proposals** to reject, not on identifying strongest to fund.
2. **High-risk proposals trigger longer discussions**, which correlate with lower scores.
3. **Reviewers signal disagreement through hedging language**, which converts to lower formal scores.
4. **Conservative scoring** is the equilibrium when panels can't reach enthusiasm consensus.

## Implications for Rubric Design

- **Discussion length itself is a score signal** — longer reviews indicate more contention, lower outcomes.
- A rubric that wants to fund risky work must somehow decouple "amount of discussion" from "score."
- Linguistic auditing of past reviews can surface implicit criteria that no formal rubric captures.
