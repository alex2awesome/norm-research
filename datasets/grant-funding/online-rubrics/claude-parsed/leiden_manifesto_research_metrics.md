# Leiden Manifesto for Research Metrics — Ten Principles

Ten principles for the responsible use of metrics in research evaluation, published in Nature 2015. Forms (with DORA) the dominant international consensus on research-assessment principles.

## The Ten Principles (in order)

### 1. Quantitative evaluation should support qualitative, expert assessment
- Numbers inform but do not replace expert judgment
- Reviewers should retain authority over final assessment
- Metrics are inputs to deliberation, not substitutes for it

### 2. Measure performance against the research missions of the institution, group, or researcher
- Goals should be defined first, metrics chosen to track them
- Different missions (basic research, applied research, training) require different metrics

### 3. Protect excellence in locally relevant research
- Some excellent research targets local/regional needs
- Global metrics (international citations) penalize this work
- A defensible rubric must accommodate locally important research

### 4. Keep data collection and analytical processes open, transparent, and simple
- Researchers should be able to verify the data
- Methods should be replicable
- Black-box assessment is not legitimate

### 5. Allow those evaluated to verify data and analysis
- Researchers can access their own metrics
- Errors can be identified and corrected
- Evaluation is auditable

### 6. Account for variation by field in publication and citation practices
- Citation rates vary 10x across fields
- Author count conventions differ
- Cross-field comparison must use field-normalized metrics

### 7. Base assessment of individual researchers on a qualitative judgement of their portfolio
- Don't rank individuals by single metrics
- Holistic judgment of body of work
- Weight context (career stage, field, role) appropriately

### 8. Avoid misplaced concreteness and false precision
- Metrics suggest precision they don't have
- Small numerical differences are not meaningful
- "Top decile" classifications can be misleading

### 9. Recognize the systemic effects of assessment and indicators
- Goodhart's Law applies: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure
- Researchers will optimize for whatever is measured
- Assessment criteria shape research itself

### 10. Scrutinize indicators regularly and update them
- Metrics that worked in 1990 may not work in 2025
- Periodically audit whether indicators still measure what they're supposed to
- Be willing to abandon indicators that have been gamed or have lost validity

## Implications for Grant Rubric Design

A Leiden-Manifesto-aligned grant rubric must:

1. **Use metrics as input to expert judgment, not as decision rules.**
2. **Tailor criteria to the funding mission** (basic vs applied; early-career vs established).
3. **Use field-normalized metrics** when comparing across disciplines.
4. **Be auditable and transparent** — applicants can see what's being scored.
5. **Avoid implying precision that doesn't exist** (consistent with Pier 2018, Fang 2016).
6. **Anticipate gaming** — design criteria robust to optimization.
7. **Periodically audit the rubric** — does it still predict what we want it to predict?

## Connection to Other Rubric Frameworks

The Leiden Manifesto is more procedural and methodologically detailed than DORA, which is normative-prescriptive. Together they form the international consensus principles:
- DORA: don't use JIFs as proxies for individual quality
- Leiden Manifesto: how to use metrics responsibly when you do use them

Both have been signed by major funders and are increasingly required by institutional research evaluation policies.
