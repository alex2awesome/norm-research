# Erosheva et al. 2020: Criterion Scores Account for Racial Disparities

Analysis of NIH R01 reviews from council years 2014-2016, examining the relationship between the five criterion scores and overall impact scores by applicant race.

## NIH's Five Criterion Scores Analyzed

1. **Significance** — importance of the problem.
2. **Investigator(s)** — qualifications and experience.
3. **Innovation** — novelty of concepts/approaches.
4. **Approach** — strategy, methodology, analysis.
5. **Environment** — institutional support.

Plus the **Overall Impact** score (1-9 scale, lower = better).

## Key Findings (the rubric of disparity)

- **Black researchers receive worse scores on ALL FIVE criteria**, even after controlling for career stage, area of science, and other application/applicant characteristics.
- **The five criterion scores fully account** for the black-white gap in overall impact scores — meaning reviewers do NOT differentially weight criteria for black vs white applicants. The disparity is "upstream" in how the criteria themselves are scored.
- 2014-2016 award rate: **black researchers 10.2% vs white researchers 18.5%** (~55% of white rate).
- Differential weighting hypothesis is rejected — the criteria scores themselves carry the disparity.

## Implications for Rubric Design

- Disparities are not in how criteria are *combined* but in how each criterion is *individually scored*.
- Removing or de-emphasizing any one criterion would not solve the disparity.
- The introduction of the five-criterion structure in 2009 (intended to add transparency) did NOT reduce disparities.
- Suggests bias is operating at the level of perception/judgment about each component, not at the integration step.

## Methodological Innovation

Used preliminary (pre-discussion) reviewer scores from individual reviewers, isolating the rubric-application step from group-deliberation effects.
