# Hoppe et al. 2019: Topic Choice and NIH Award Disparities

Analysis of 2011-2015 NIH R01 applications showing that AA/B scientists' lower funding rate is partly explained by which research topics they propose.

## Six Stages of NIH Application Process Studied

1. **Submission** (which proposals are sent in)
2. **Decision to discuss** (whether the application makes it past triage)
3. **Impact score assignment** (the numeric rating given by reviewers)
4. **Funding decision** by program staff and council
5. **Topic choice** (a previously unstudied stage — which subject areas applicants choose)
6. **Award rate by topic cluster**

Disparities arose at three of the six: decision to discuss, impact score, and topic choice.

## Topic Cluster Findings (implicit criteria embedded in NIH topic preferences)

- AA/B applicants disproportionately propose research on topics with **lower award rates** across all applicants.
- Topics over-represented among AA/B applicants: **community / population-level health, health disparities, patient-centered outcomes, behavioral interventions**.
- Topics under-represented among AA/B applicants: **basic mechanism / molecular biology** (which has higher overall award rates).
- The award-rate gap attributable to topic choice is roughly comparable in magnitude to other studied gaps.

## Implicit NIH Quality Criteria Revealed

- Reviewers apparently apply **higher rigor standards or lower enthusiasm to applied/translational/health-disparities work** compared to molecular/basic work.
- The criteria for "significance" appear field-dependent in ways that disadvantage applied work.
- **Methodology preferences** (mechanistic over observational) may compound topic-based disparities.

## Implications for Rubric Design

Any rubric that does not adjust for topic-cluster baseline award rates will replicate the documented disparities. Hoppe et al. implicitly argue NIH must either equalize topic-cluster funding rates or transparently justify why some topic clusters merit lower investment.
