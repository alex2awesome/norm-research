# DrugMonkey Blog - NIH Peer Review Insider Tips

## About the Blog
DrugMonkey is a long-running anonymous blog by an NIH-funded biomedical scientist with extensive study section experience. Provides candid commentary on NIH grant review practices, often from an inside-the-system perspective.

## Recent NIH Peer Review Trends Covered

### Center for Scientific Review (CSR) Changes
- CSR now reviews about 66,697 applications annually
- CSR plans to create 52 new chartered study sections to manage R01 applications previously handled by funding ICs
- Consolidation of review may improve cross-comparison

### The 2025 Simplified Review Framework
- Two scored Factors + one binary assessment
- Concerns about "iron law of unintended consequences"
- May reduce score discrimination at top of distribution
- May increase emphasis on Approach (Factor 2 = Rigor and Feasibility)

## Strategy Tips for Applicants

### When You're "Not Discussed"
For applicants receiving a "competitive but not discussed" outcome:
- **Contact your Program Officer immediately** - this is the #1 strategy
- Particularly important for experienced PIs who qualify for "geographical diversity" affirmative action policies
- Ask about funding outlook, possible administrative supplements, RFA fits

### When You Are Discussed but Unfunded
- Read summary statement carefully
- Identify convergent vs. divergent reviewer critiques
- Plan resubmission addressing convergent concerns
- Consider whether to switch study sections

## Understanding Peer Review Bias

### Inherent Bias
There is inherent unfair bias in NIH grant review that operates all the time and is essentially inevitable. A small subset of people making highly subjective judgements about grant proposals that differ on numerous axes cannot be reasonably compared in any objective way.

### Sources of Bias Identified
- **Topic bias** - Some topics systematically scored lower
- **Methods bias** - Reviewer preferences for certain approaches
- **Career stage bias** - Rich-get-richer effect
- **Geography bias** - Coastal/elite institution preference (offset by some affirmative action)
- **Personal connections** - Cronyism in study sections
- **Conservatism bias** - Innovative work systematically penalized

### Inherent Conservatism
NIH grant review is structurally conservative. The discussion process, the need for consensus, and risk-aversion all favor incremental work over genuinely novel approaches.

## Zoom Study Section Insights

### Advantages
- The electronic "hand up" feature reduces deference to the loudest voice
- Rapidly identifies the "windbag" for the whole group
- More even participation
- Time discipline easier to enforce

### Disadvantages
- Less side discussion
- Reduced advocacy for borderline applications
- Reviewer fatigue (longer screen time)

## Career Strategy Per DrugMonkey

### Decrease Competition
The blog recommends seeking ways to **decrease competition** rather than increase it:
- Take your skills to a place where they represent near-miraculous advance
- Topic differentiation
- Geographic differentiation
- Institution type differentiation
- Working in an area where there are fewer people like you

### Don't Chase Trends
- Reviewers often score "trendy" applications more harshly because of competition
- Find your niche and own it
- Build sustained programs of work

## Reviewer Behavior Insights

### Score Calibration
- Pre-meeting scores serve as anchors
- Discussion can move scores significantly
- Strong primary reviewer advocacy can rescue a borderline grant
- Weak presentation can sink a strong proposal

### "Score Creep"
Tendency for scores to converge toward the middle during discussion, often pulling exceptional applications down and dragging mediocre ones up.

### Reviewer Identity Effects
- Some reviewers consistently score high; others low
- Calibration training only partially addresses this
- Random assignment to reviewers means luck plays a role

## Implications for the Pier et al. (2018) Findings
DrugMonkey has extensively discussed the Pier et al. study showing low inter-rater reliability:
- Confirms what insiders have long known
- The system relies more on aggregation than individual reliability
- Random differences are amplified by competitive funding lines
