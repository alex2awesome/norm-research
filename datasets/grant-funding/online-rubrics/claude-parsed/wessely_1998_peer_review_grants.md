# Wessely (1998) - Peer Review of Grant Applications: What Do We Know?

## Citation
Wessely, S. (1998). Peer review of grant applications: what do we know? The Lancet, 352(9124), 301-305.

## Author Affiliation
S Wessely was affiliated with the Department of Psychological Medicine, King's College School of Medicine and the Institute of Psychiatry, London.

## Article Type
An extensive, although non-systematic, review of existing studies on grant giving peer review.

## Key Themes Examined

### Reliability of Peer Review
- Inter-rater reliability is consistently low across funding agencies
- Two reviewers often disagree more than chance would predict
- Variability in scoring norms across reviewers and panels

### Sources of Bias
A number of criticisms about peer review of grant applications have focused on the reliability of the process and the existence of biases including:
- **Age** of applicant
- **Institution** (prestige bias / "Matthew effect")
- **Cronyism** / personal connections
- **Discipline** (interdisciplinary work disadvantaged)
- **Gender** (descriptive evidence at Swedish Medical Research Council; mixed elsewhere)
- **Innovation** vs. conservatism in peer review (innovative proposals tend to receive lower scores)

### Predictive Validity
- Researchers were unable to find comparative studies assessing the actual effect of peer review procedures on the quality of funded research
- Experimental studies assessing the effects of grant giving peer-review on research quality were urgently needed
- Grant scores have weak predictive validity for downstream productivity (citations, impact)

## Key Conclusions

1. **Grant giving relies heavily on peer review** for the assessment of the quality of proposals
2. **Evidence of effects of these procedures appeared scarce**
3. The conventional wisdom that peer review is the gold standard is largely **based on faith rather than evidence**
4. Need for more systematic empirical studies of the peer review process

## Influence
This paper is foundational in the literature critiquing grant peer review and is widely cited alongside:
- Pier et al. (2018) - Low agreement among reviewers
- Lindner & Nakamura (2015) - Examining the predictive validity of NIH peer review scores
- Fang et al. (2016) - Research funding: the case for a modified lottery
- Cole et al. (1981) - Chance and consensus in peer review

## Reform Suggestions Inspired by This Line of Work
- Modified lotteries above a quality threshold
- Multiple anonymous reviewers
- Calibration training
- Pre-registration of evaluation criteria
- Structured rubrics rather than holistic scoring
