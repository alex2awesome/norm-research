# Chubin & Hackett 1990: Peerless Science

Foundational STS book examining peer-review quality control in U.S. science. Identifies the criteria reviewers actually use and the ways those criteria fail.

## Criteria Reviewers Apply (per Chubin & Hackett's analysis)

1. **Significance / importance of the question** — does the proposal address a problem of recognized scientific importance?
2. **Scientific merit and rigor** of the approach — soundness of design, methods, and analysis plan.
3. **Investigator competence** — track record, prior publications, prior funding, ability to complete the work.
4. **Feasibility** — whether the proposed work can be accomplished in the time and budget specified.
5. **Originality and innovation** — does the proposal break new ground or merely incrementally extend known work?
6. **Institutional environment and resources** — does the host institution provide adequate support?

## Identified Failure Modes / Defects of Peer Review (rubric for what to AVOID)

- **Cronyism / "old boy" networks** — reviewers favor known researchers from elite institutions.
- **Conservatism** — reviewers reward incremental, safe, well-grounded proposals over genuinely novel ideas.
- **Bias against unorthodoxy and interdisciplinary work**.
- **Self-serving bias** — reviewers may protect their own subfield's funding share.
- **"Halo effect" from prior funding success** — successful applicants get rewarded again, regardless of new proposal merit.
- **Lack of accountability** — reviewers face no consequences for low-quality reviews.
- **Confidentiality misused as cover for poor reasoning**.
- **Inability to detect fraud** in submitted preliminary data.

## Proposed Reform Criteria

Chubin & Hackett argue that good peer-review systems should be: (1) transparent, (2) accountable, (3) inclusive of diverse reviewer perspectives, (4) responsive to applicant rebuttals, and (5) periodically audited for systematic bias.
