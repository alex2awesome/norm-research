# Cole, Cole & Simon 1981: Chance and Consensus in Peer Review (NSF)

Landmark experimental replication study where 150 NSF proposals were independently re-evaluated by a new set of reviewers to test whether peer-review outcomes depended on which reviewers were drawn.

## Research Design / Implicit Quality Criteria Studied

The Cole-Cole-Simon study evaluated NSF reviewer ratings on the criteria implicit in NSF's then-existing review process:

1. **Scientific merit of the proposed research** — the central rating dimension reviewers were asked to score.
2. **Competence of the principal investigator** — including productivity, prior publications, and standing in the field.
3. **Probable impact of the proposed work on its field**.
4. **Suitability of the methods proposed** to accomplish the research aims.
5. **Likely effect of the work on training of students and personnel**.
6. **Reasonableness of the requested budget** for the proposed scope.

## Key Empirical Findings (criteria for what went WRONG)

- **Inter-reviewer reliability was low** — the reliability coefficient between original and replicate reviewer panels was approximately 0.25.
- **No evidence of systematic bias** by reviewer institution, gender, or "old-boy" networks was found in the NSF reviewer assignments.
- **"Luck of the reviewer draw"** — for proposals near the funding line, whether a proposal got funded depended substantially on which reviewers happened to be selected.
- The disagreement was not random noise; it reflected genuine substantive disagreement among eligible experts about what counts as good science.

## Implications for Rubric Design

The study established that any grant rubric used by humans must be evaluated against an inter-rater reliability ceiling that is empirically very low (~0.2-0.4). Rubrics that imply higher precision are misleading. The study is foundational for the entire empirical literature on grant peer review.
