# Pier et al. 2018: Low Agreement Among NIH Reviewers (PNAS)

Direct experimental test where 43 ad-hoc reviewers each evaluated the same 25 NIH R01 grant applications (all of which had previously been funded by NIH). Measures inter-rater agreement on identical proposals.

## NIH's Five Criterion Scoring Rubric (the rubric being tested)

Each reviewer scored each application on:
1. **Significance** (1-9 scale)
2. **Investigator(s)** (1-9 scale)
3. **Innovation** (1-9 scale)
4. **Approach** (1-9 scale)
5. **Environment** (1-9 scale)
6. **Overall Impact** (1-9 scale, holistic)

Plus structured written critique with strengths and weaknesses for each criterion.

## Key Findings (the failure rubric)

- **Inter-rater reliability ≈ 0** for overall impact scores.
- Reviewers wrote extensively about the SAME applications but **arrived at radically different conclusions**.
- The same proposal might be scored top-decile by one reviewer and below-bar by another.
- **No agreement on how to translate strengths/weaknesses into a numeric score** even with identical instructions.
- Pre-discussion individual scores showed essentially zero agreement.
- **Discussion among reviewers did not improve agreement** — and in some cases made it worse, as group dynamics amplified individual disagreements.

## Implications for Rubric Design (the diagnostic rubric)

1. The five-criterion rubric does not constrain reviewers enough to produce reproducible scores.
2. The translation from qualitative critique to numeric score is the unreliable step.
3. **Outcomes depend more on which reviewer is assigned than which proposal is submitted** (within fundable range).
4. Group consensus does not solve the agreement problem; it can mask individual variability while not actually reducing it.
5. The rubric structure itself may need fundamental redesign — perhaps comparative rather than absolute scoring.

## Reform Implications

- Forced-choice or rank-order methods may be more reliable than absolute scoring.
- Lottery elements among "fundable" proposals may be more honest than precision-suggesting percentile scores.
- Training interventions could marginally improve agreement but cannot fix structural noise.
