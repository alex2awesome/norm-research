# Langfeldt 2001: Decision-Making Constraints in Grant Peer Review

Case study of the Research Council of Norway combining direct observation of panel meetings, interviews, and document analysis across multiple grant-review models.

## Reviewer Criteria Variation Documented

There is no single shared rubric — reviewers emphasize different criteria. Common criteria identified:

1. **Scientific quality / merit** of the proposal
2. **Originality / innovativeness**
3. **Feasibility**
4. **Applicant qualifications / track record**
5. **Methodology**
6. **Strategic / mission relevance**
7. **Cost-effectiveness / budget reasonableness**

But the **weight** assigned to each varies dramatically by reviewer and panel.

## Key Empirical Finding: Procedure Drives Outcome

The decision-making **method** the panel uses has a substantial effect on which proposals win, independent of the proposals' content:

### Three Decision-Procedure Variables Studied

1. **Rating scale design**: granular numeric vs. categorical labels vs. "fund/no-fund" binary.
2. **Budget restrictions**: panels with hard caps make different choices than those without.
3. **Ranking method**: averaging individual scores vs. consensus discussion vs. comparative ranking.

### Effects of Each Method

- **Consensus-based ranking** → tends to fund **safer, less controversial** proposals.
- **Averaging individual scores** → tends to fund proposals with broadly **moderate enthusiasm**.
- **Hard budget caps** → forces panels to triage on **cost-benefit** rather than pure quality.
- **No budget cap** at panel stage → panels can recommend more risky proposals; deferral to higher levels.

## Implications for Rubric Design

- Rating scales and budget constraints have larger effects on which proposals get funded than written review guidelines do.
- "Improve the criteria" reforms are likely less effective than "improve the procedure" reforms.
- Each decision procedure carries its own implicit value: consensus = stability, averaging = moderation, ranking = comparison.
