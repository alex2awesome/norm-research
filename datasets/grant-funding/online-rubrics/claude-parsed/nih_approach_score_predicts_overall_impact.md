# How Criterion Scores Predict Overall Impact - Eblen et al. (2016)

## Citation
Eblen, M.K., Wagner, R.M., RoyChowdhury, D., Patel, K.C., & Pearson, K. (2016). How Criterion Scores Predict the Overall Impact Score and Funding Outcomes for National Institutes of Health Peer-Reviewed Applications. PLoS ONE, 11(6): e0155060.

## Key Finding: Approach Dominates

The Approach criterion score had the strongest association with overall impact, with **more than double the effect of the next largest predictor**, the Significance score.

## Variance Explained by Each Criterion

A stepwise regression analysis of the five scored review criteria shows:

| Order | Criterion | Variance Explained (incremental) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Approach | 72% |
| 2 | Significance | +4% |
| 3 | Innovation | +1% |
| 4 | Investigator | +<1% |
| 5 | Environment | +<1% |
| **Total** | All five | **~78%** |

So Approach alone explains about 72% of variance in overall impact scores. All five criteria together explain ~78%, meaning ~22% of overall impact variance is NOT predicted by criterion scores (potentially holistic judgment, intangible factors, or noise).

## Implications

### For Applicants
- The Approach section is by far the most important
- Spend disproportionate effort on:
  - Methodological detail
  - Statistical rigor
  - Sample size justification
  - Alternative approaches and contingencies
  - Preliminary data demonstrating feasibility
- Don't neglect Significance (second most important)
- Innovation, Investigator, Environment have surprisingly low marginal predictive power

### For Reviewers
- Approach concerns dominate review discussions
- "Death spiral" - one major Approach concern can sink an otherwise strong proposal
- Reviewers may be conflating Approach quality with overall scientific judgment

## Caveats
- The study uses post-hoc data; cannot conclude reviewers SHOULD be weighting Approach this heavily
- Possible that "Approach" subsumes other dimensions in reviewer mental models
- May reflect biomedical research norms, less applicable to other fields
- Doesn't capture "discussion" effects in panels

## Related Findings

### Lindner & Nakamura (2015)
- Examined predictive validity of NIH peer review scores
- Found scores have only modest correlation with downstream productivity (publications, citations)
- Suggests review may not pick the "best" proposals among funded vs. unfunded

### Fang et al. (2016)
- Argued for partial lottery above quality threshold
- Cited above weak predictive validity findings

### Pier et al. (2018)
- Showed low inter-rater reliability (independent finding)

## Implications for the New Simplified Review Framework
The 2025 SRF reorganizes criteria:
- Factor 1: Importance (Significance + Innovation) — 1-9 score
- Factor 2: Rigor and Feasibility (Approach) — 1-9 score
- Factor 3: Expertise and Resources (Investigator + Environment) — binary

This roughly maps to Eblen et al.'s findings:
- Approach (most predictive) gets its own dedicated factor
- Investigator/Environment (least predictive) downgraded to binary
- Innovation (low predictive) bundled with Significance

## Key Statistics
- Study analyzed ~123,927 NIH R01 applications from 2010-2013
- Across all institutes and study sections
- Strong consistency across study sections
- Scores correlated with funding outcomes (lower score = higher funding probability)

## Practical Application
When writing/revising:
- Allocate most effort to Approach (methods, design, analysis)
- Address Significance thoroughly
- Innovation, Investigator, Environment matter but less
- A weak Approach is the most common reason for low overall scores
