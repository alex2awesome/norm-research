# Lamont 2009: How Professors Think

Qualitative study of 5 humanities/social-science grant competitions, 12 panels, 2 years. Based on observation of panel deliberations and post-deliberation interviews. Identifies the implicit criteria and "epistemological styles" panel members deploy.

## Customary Criteria of Excellence (the consensus rubric)

1. **Originality** — does the proposal break new ground intellectually?
2. **Significance** — does the question matter to the field or beyond?
3. **Methods / clarity of approach** — is the research design coherent?
4. **Feasibility** — can the applicant pull this off?
5. **Cultural and social significance** — does the work speak to broader concerns?

## Emergent / Tacit Criteria (the things panelists actually used in deliberation)

1. **"Quality of the soul"** — perceived passion, dedication, commitment in the proposal voice.
2. **Elegance / aesthetic appeal** of the proposal.
3. **Authenticity** — does the applicant seem to "own" the question?
4. **"Promise"** of the applicant as a scholar (separable from the proposal itself).
5. **Diversity considerations** — geographic, institutional, demographic balance across awards.
6. **Strategic balance** in the awarded portfolio.
7. **Cognitive contextualization** — applying field-appropriate evaluation standards.

## Four Epistemological Styles Panelists Use

1. **Constructivist** — emphasizes interpretive, social-constructionist framing.
2. **Comprehensive** — values broad synthesis, integration across domains.
3. **Positivist** — values methodological rigor, hypothesis testing, replicability.
4. **Utilitarian** — values practical applicability, policy relevance.

A "fair" panel deploys the style appropriate to the proposal's discipline rather than imposing a single style across all proposals.

## Customary Procedural Norms ("rules of deliberation")

1. **Deference to expertise** — the in-field panelist's view weighs heavily.
2. **Pragmatic fairness** — taking turns, avoiding dominance.
3. **Cognitive contextualization** — judging proposals within their field's standards.
4. **Methodological pluralism** — accepting that multiple approaches can be excellent.
5. **Strategic horse-trading** — "I'll support yours if you support mine" within informal limits.
6. **Avoiding personal attacks** on absent applicants.

## Implications for Rubric Design

Rubrics that only enumerate explicit criteria (significance, methods, etc.) miss the much larger universe of tacit judgments. A rubric should make space for:
- Discipline-specific epistemological norms.
- "Promise" / aesthetic / character judgments that happen anyway.
- Procedural norms of deliberation.
