# Travis & Collins 1991: New Light on Old Boys

Ethnographic observation of UK SERC grant-awarding committees. Distinguishes "institutional particularism" (cronyism by institution) from the more important "cognitive particularism" (favoring proposals matching reviewers' own theoretical and methodological commitments).

## Two Forms of Particularism (criteria reviewers covertly apply)

### Institutional Particularism ("Old Boy")
1. Favoring applicants from prestigious universities (e.g., Oxford, Cambridge, top US schools).
2. Favoring applicants known personally to panel members.
3. Favoring applicants from the same departmental tradition.

### Cognitive Particularism (the more important bias they identify)
1. **Favoring proposals using reviewers' own theoretical paradigm**.
2. **Favoring proposals using methodologies the reviewer practices**.
3. **Penalizing interdisciplinary work** that crosses paradigm boundaries.
4. **Penalizing heterodox or revolutionary research programs** even when scientifically rigorous.
5. **Favoring incremental/normal-science proposals** over Kuhnian revolutionary work.
6. **Implicit boundary work** — defining what "counts" as the field, and excluding what doesn't fit.

## Implications for Rubrics

Travis & Collins argue any rubric that does not surface and audit cognitive particularism will systematically suppress paradigm-shifting research, regardless of how it controls for institutional affiliation. They suggest panel composition itself is a rubric-design choice with stronger effects than any written criteria.

## Methodological Note

Based on direct observation of SERC grant committee meetings — one of the few ethnographic studies of grant deliberation prior to Lamont (2009).
