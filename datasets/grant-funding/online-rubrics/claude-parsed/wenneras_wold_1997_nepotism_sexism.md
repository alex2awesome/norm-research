# Wennerås & Wold 1997: Nepotism and Sexism in Peer-Review

Landmark Nature paper, the first systematic empirical analysis of peer-review scores. Studied Swedish Medical Research Council postdoctoral fellowship reviews, obtained via court order.

## Three Criteria Reviewers Scored (Swedish MRC fellowship rubric)

1. **Scientific competence** of the applicant
2. **Quality of the proposed methodology**
3. **Relevance of the research proposal**

Each criterion scored 0-4, summed to overall ranking.

## Findings (rubric of bias documented)

### Gender Bias Magnitude
- Female applicants received systematically lower competence scores than males of equivalent productivity.
- A woman needed to be **2.5 times more productive** than a man to receive the same competence score.
- Operationalized "productivity" via:
  - Total impact-factor-weighted publications
  - First-author publications
  - Citations
  - These were the strongest objective predictors.

### Nepotism / Affiliation Bias
- Applicants affiliated with a member of the review committee received scores ~0.5 points higher (on the 4-point scale) than non-affiliates of equivalent productivity.
- The effect of affiliation was **comparable in magnitude to having an extra ~100 impact-points of publications**.

### Combined Effect
- Being female AND non-affiliated created the worst outcomes; being male AND affiliated created the best — independent of measurable productivity.

## Productivity Measures the Authors Treat as Objective Truth-Reference

1. Total publications
2. First-authorships
3. Total impact factor of publishing journals
4. Citations to the applicant's work
5. Educational level / years post-degree

## Implications for Rubric Design

Any rubric with a "competence" or "investigator" criterion is vulnerable to capturing rater stereotypes rather than productivity. Wennerås & Wold's productivity benchmarks became a template for subsequent fairness audits.
