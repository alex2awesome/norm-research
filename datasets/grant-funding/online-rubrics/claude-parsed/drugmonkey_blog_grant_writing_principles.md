# DrugMonkey Blog — Aggregated NIH Grant Writing Wisdom

DrugMonkey is one of the most-cited anonymous biomedical PI blogs covering NIH grant strategy, study section behavior, and review culture from a mid-career PI perspective.

## Core Themes Across Posts

### Reviewers Are Tired and Skim
- Assume your assigned reviewer reads at midnight after dinner
- The first paragraph of Significance must be self-contained
- Bold/underline matters more than you think — guide the eye
- Make every Aim title legible standalone

### Triage Is the Real Game
- Half of submitted apps get triaged (not discussed)
- A triaged app gets ~12 minutes of attention from one reviewer
- The Specific Aims page IS the application for triage purposes
- Avoid anything that gives the assigned reviewer an easy reason to lowball

### Innovation Is a Trap
- Reviewers say they want innovation but reward feasibility
- "Highly innovative" + thin preliminary data = unscored
- Frame innovation as "novel application of established methods" rather than "no one has ever done this"

### The Three Reviewer Profiles
1. **The Champion** — likes you, advocates, picks the discussed score
2. **The Hatchet** — picks something to kill, often a methods reviewer
3. **The Indifferent** — splits the difference, follows the room

You must write for all three. The Champion needs ammunition; the Hatchet needs no openings; the Indifferent needs clear bullet points.

### Preliminary Data Hierarchy
1. Published in your lab >>> 2. Submitted/in review >>> 3. Unpublished from your lab >>> 4. From collaborators >>> 5. From the literature
- Anything below tier 3 is treated as suggestive at best
- A figure caption that says "(unpublished)" must be supplemented elsewhere

### The "Old Boy" Reality
- Senior PIs get the benefit of the doubt
- Junior PIs must over-document feasibility
- ESI status helps with paylines, not with substantive review
- Co-Is with prior R01s buy you credibility

### Resubmission Strategy
- Address EVERY critique even if you disagree
- Use the Introduction page (one page allowed) to itemize changes
- Quote the reviewer directly when possible
- Even minor critiques must be acknowledged ("addressed by adding text on p.4")
- Never argue without conceding something

### The "Approach" Section Is Where You Lose
- Significance and Innovation get scored generously
- Approach is where reviewers find faults
- Methods must be specific enough to evaluate but not so detailed as to bore
- Always include: rigor, sample sizes, statistical plan, alternative approaches, pitfalls, timeline

### Pitfalls and Alternatives Sections
- A required ritual — write them or get dinged
- Show you've thought about what could go wrong
- Provide a backup that doesn't require the original to fail
- Don't list pitfalls so scary the reviewer worries the project will fail

## Writing Voice
- Write declaratively. "We will determine X." Never "We hope to elucidate."
- Active voice, first-person plural is fine in NIH grants
- Avoid jargon outside your subfield
- Define every acronym at first use, even obvious ones (assigned reviewer may be 2 fields away)

## What DrugMonkey Says Wins
- Clear hypothesis explicitly stated
- Aims that test the hypothesis (not "characterize" or "explore")
- Quantitative preliminary data with effect sizes
- A timeline figure
- A team that visibly covers all the methods
- Rigor language addressing sex as biological variable, blinding, randomization

## What Loses
- "Fishing expedition" framing
- Aim 1 must succeed for Aim 2/3 to work
- No power calculation
- Vague "we will use X to study Y"
- Author lists too many tangentially relevant papers
- Specific Aims page that doesn't fit standard four-paragraph structure
