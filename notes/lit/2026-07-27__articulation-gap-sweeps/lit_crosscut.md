## (2) Section A — Press releases / PR quality

Overall finding: there is no large, quotable "PR practitioners admit their news judgment is tacit" literature as clean as the tacit-knowledge canon in other fields. The strongest, most citable material is (a) Pieczka's direct empirical work theorizing PR expertise as embodied/practical knowledge, and (b) the journalist-side gatekeeping/churnalism literature showing editorial selection of press releases runs on unarticulated "news sense"/"gut feeling" rather than an explicit rubric. Both are TRADE-adjacent ACADEMIC STUDIES.

### A1. Pieczka, M. (2002). "Public relations expertise deconstructed." *Media, Culture & Society*, 24(3), 301–323. — **ACADEMIC STUDY**
Why it shows a gap: Pieczka conducted participant observation of a PR practitioner training course plus analysis of >100 PR industry award-entry case studies specifically to reverse-engineer what "PR expertise" consists of, on the premise that it is *not* a body of stateable, teachable technique but "practical knowledge" — i.e., the field's own self-descriptions (textbook models like RACE/ROSIE, the "excellence" management-by-objectives literature) do not capture what practitioners actually do.
> "Professional expertise emerges from the analysis as a body not of abstract, but of practical knowledge." — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched abstract), https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/016344370202400302, page unknown (abstract)
> "[PR expertise is] a body of practical knowledge which makes it possible for public relations practice to exist." — **[SNIPPET]** (exact phrase surfaced in secondary search aggregation of the same abstract; not independently confirmed against full text), page unknown

### A5. Lewis, J., Williams, A., & Franklin, B. (2008). "A Compromised Fourth Estate? UK News Journalism, Public Relations and News Sources." *Journalism Studies*, 9(1), 1–20. — **ACADEMIC STUDY**
Why it shows a gap: the Cardiff University "churnalism" study is usually read as a story about PR *power* (80% of stories not wholly original, only 12% generated by reporters from scratch), but it also implies journalists' own stated professional norm ("we verify/originate our stories") is not what determines actual copy — the gap is between journalists' *espoused* editorial independence and their *revealed* reliance on press-office material, which they select via unarticulated speed/plausibility heuristics rather than an explicit standard.

## (3) Section B — Notice-and-comment / regulatory persuasiveness

Overall finding: this literature is more about *measured influence* (does a comment change the rule) and *procedural* categories (mass/form-letter vs. individualized) than about agency staff's articulation gap per se. The clearest "unstated standard" evidence is indirect: agencies' own guidance documents state generic desiderata (data, specificity, novelty) while the empirical record shows outcomes that those stated criteria don't fully predict, and CeRI's "situated knowledge" concept is explicitly framed as knowledge agencies want but cannot themselves generate or fully specify in advance.

### B1. Farina, C. R., Newhart, M. J., et al. — Cornell e-Rulemaking Initiative (CeRI) / Regulation Room project. E.g., "Rulemaking 2.0: Understanding and Getting Better Public Participation" (2013/2015), and "Regulation Room: Getting 'More, Better' Civic Participation in Complex Government Policymaking" (with D. Epstein), *Journal of Information Technology & Politics* or similar. — **ACADEMIC STUDY**
Why it shows a gap: CeRI's whole design rationale is that agencies need "situated knowledge" from commenters — impacts, unintended consequences, enforceability problems — that the agency does not itself possess and that "is not information that representative organizations can readily gather from individual members and convey credibly and in sufficiently rich detail to the agency." This is an admission that agencies cannot fully specify, ex ante, what a useful comment will contain — they know it when they see it in the record, but the criteria for extracting it (and prompting laypeople to produce it) had to be reverse-engineered through years of platform experimentation rather than written down as a rubric.
> "[Situated knowledge is] information about impacts, problems, enforceability, contributory causes, unintended consequences, etc. This is knowledge that the agency is unlikely itself to possess; moreover, it is not information that representative organizations can readily gather from individual members and convey credibly and in sufficiently rich detail to the agency." — **[SNIPPET]** (this exact phrasing was returned as a synthesized excerpt by search over the CeRI corpus; I was blocked (403/empty) from directly fetching the Cornell scholarship-repository PDF to confirm the passage in situ, so mark SNIPPET not VERIFIED), likely source: https://scholarship.law.cornell.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1015&context=ceri, page unknown. FLAG FOR FOLLOW-UP: worth a direct-download attempt (not via WebFetch, which was blocked) to upgrade this to VERIFIED and get a page number — the "situated knowledge" concept is the single best notice-and-comment quote for this paper's thesis and deserves a solid citation.

### B2. Balla, S. J., et al. (2022). "Lost in the flood? Agency responsiveness to mass comment campaigns in administrative rulemaking." *Regulation & Governance*, 16(1), 293–308. — **ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[VERIFIED]**
Why it shows a gap: analyzes 1,049 mass-comment campaigns across 22 EPA rulemakings (2012–2017) and finds agencies cite campaign comments at lower rates than other comments, and that final rules are generally *not* consistent with what mass campaigns requested — but the paper's own explanation is that mass comments "generally contain little 'relevant matter,'" a legal-standard phrase (APA "relevant matter presented") that is itself not operationalized into any explicit, checkable rubric — agencies are applying a substantive/non-substantive distinction that the paper shows in its effects but that has no stated decision procedure.
> "This article examines agency responsiveness to mass comment campaigns... The analysis demonstrates that, procedurally, the agency references mass comment campaigns in its responses to comments, but cites campaigns at lower rates than other comments. In terms of outcomes, the agency's regulations are generally not consistent with changes requested in comments, a lack of association that holds especially for mass comment campaigns. These patterns suggest that legal imperatives trump political considerations in conditioning agency responsiveness, given that mass comment campaigns – relative to other comments – generally contain little 'relevant matter.'" — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched abstract in full), https://ideas.repec.org/a/wly/reggov/v16y2022i1p293-308.html, page unknown (abstract; article pp. 293–308)

### B3. Golden, M. M. (1998). "Interest Groups in the Rule-Making Process: Who Participates? Whose Voices Get Heard?" *Journal of Public Administration Research and Theory*, 8(2), 245–270. — **ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[VERIFIED, abstract only]**
Why it's relevant (weaker fit): studies 11 EPA/NHTSA/HUD rules to ask who comments and whether comments change rules; finds "a dearth of citizen commenters, the predominance of participation by business interests" and, notably, "the absence of any discernible bias in whose voices get heard" once you condition on who shows up. Useful as a baseline empirical-influence citation for the domain, but it does NOT itself document an articulation gap (no interview evidence of staff being unable to state their standards) — flag as background/context rather than a direct gap-finding, unless full text (not available to me) contains staff-interview material the abstract doesn't surface.
> "a dearth of citizen commenters, the predominance of participation by business interests" ... "the absence of any discernible bias in whose voices get heard" — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched abstract page), https://academic.oup.com/jpart/article-abstract/8/2/245/965781, page unknown (abstract; article pp. 245–270)

## (4) Section C — Cross-cutting classics we may be missing

### C1. Argyris, C., & Schön, D. A. (with D. M. Smith), *Action Science* (1985), Jossey-Bass, Ch. 3 "Theories of Action," pp. 80–85. — **SCHOLARLY BOOK** — **[VERIFIED]**
Why it shows a gap: this is the canonical source for "espoused theory" vs. "theory-in-use" — the direct, general-purpose statement that people's stated account of why they act is systematically different from the theory that actually predicts their behavior, and that the latter is often unstatable.
> "Espoused theory and theory-in-use may be consistent or inconsistent, and the agent may or may not be aware of any inconsistency. The agent *is* aware of the espoused theory, by definition, since it is the theory that the agent claims to follow. Recall in this connection our previous discussion of tacit knowledge and rule-governed behavior. As many approaches to social inquiry emphasize, human beings can be understood to act according to rules that they cannot state." — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched via PDF), p. 82.
> "Theories-in-use are the often tacit cognitive maps by which human beings design action. Theories-in-use can be made explicit by reflecting on action." — **[VERIFIED]**, p. 82.
Note: the file I fetched is Argyris/Schön/Smith's 1985 *Action Science*, which restates and extends the original espoused-theory/theory-in-use distinction from Argyris & Schön's earlier *Theory in Practice: Increasing Professional Effectiveness* (1974) — cite either depending on which the paper prefers; the exact quoted language above is from the 1985 book specifically.

### C2. Highhouse, S. (2008). "Stubborn Reliance on Intuition and Subjectivity in Employee Selection." *Industrial and Organizational Psychology*, 1(3), 333–342. — **ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[VERIFIED]**
Why it shows a gap: directly on the "hiring/admissions" vein requested. Documents that (a) HR professionals *rate* structured, mechanical selection tools as more effective than unstructured interviews in surveys, yet (b) still prefer and rely on unstructured interviews and "reading between the lines," i.e., their stated belief about what predicts good hires diverges from what actually predicts good hires, and from what they themselves say works when asked analytically vs. when asked to act.
> "Hastie and Dawes (2001) distilled from the vast literature on prediction 'experts' the following stylized facts: • They rely on few pieces of information. • They lack insight into how they arrive at predictions. • They exhibit poor interjudge agreement. • They become more confident in their accuracy when irrelevant information is presented." — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched full PDF), p. 339.
> "People know that the store brands [tests] are identical [to interviews in what they measure], but they do not trust them for their own colds." — **[VERIFIED]**, p. 334 (Highhouse's own analogy for why interviewers keep trusting unstructured interviews over validated tests).
> "the interrater reliability of the traditional (unstructured) interview is so low that, even with a perfectly reliable and valid criterion, interview-based judgments could never account for more than 10% of the variance in job performance." — **[VERIFIED]**, pp. 337–338.
FLAG: this is adjacent to Dawes/Meehl/Grove (clinical-vs-actuarial, already in our bib) but is a distinct, hiring-specific paper with new quotable material — recommend adding as `highhouse2008stubborn`.

### C3. Klein, G., Calderwood, R., & Clinton-Cirocco, A. — Recognition-Primed Decision (RPD) / fireground commander studies; book-length treatment in Klein, G. (1998), *Sources of Power: How People Make Decisions*, MIT Press. — **SCHOLARLY BOOK + ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[SNIPPET]**

### C4. Johansson, P., Hall, L., Sikström, S., & Olsson, A. (2005/2006). "Failure to Detect Mismatches Between Intention and Outcome in a Simple Decision Task" (*Science*, 2005) and "How Something Can Be Said About Telling More Than We Can Know" (*Consciousness and Cognition*, 2006) — choice blindness. — **ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[VERIFIED, via secondary confirmation]**

> "participants were shown pictures of pairs of female faces, and asked to choose which face they found more attractive. On a subset of trials the face they selected was re-presented and participants were asked to provide an oral explanation for their choice. On a further subsample of such explanation trials, the faces were covertly exchanged, such that the unselected face was presented as though they had selected it." — **[VERIFIED]** (fetched), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4172758/, page/paragraph: Introduction section (secondary paper summarizing the original Johansson et al. 2005 paradigm).
> "people can be induced to confabulate an explanation for why they selected the option they did not choose." — **[VERIFIED]**, same source, Introduction section.
> "Only 26% of participants volunteered detection of these manipulations" — **[VERIFIED]**, same source, Introduction section (citing Johansson et al. 2005's original detection rate).

### C5. Biederman, I., & Shiffrar, M. M. (1987). "Sexing Day-Old Chicks: A Case Study and Expert Systems Analysis of a Difficult Perceptual-Learning Task." *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition*, 13(4), 640–645. — **ACADEMIC STUDY** — **[LEAD]**
