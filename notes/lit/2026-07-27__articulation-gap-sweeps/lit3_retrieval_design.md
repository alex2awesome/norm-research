Key verified short quotes (all directly from the primary article, position noted, no fabricated page number):

- Framing of the whole essay (early, ~p.2): "People have in their doing a tacit kind of knowing. They know more than they can say, and in zones of uncertainty, uniqueness, and conflict, they are sometimes able to reflect on what they know but cannot say... Their knowing-in-action is incongruent with their descriptions of it."

- The Quist/Petra protocol setup (mid-article): "The studio master, Quist, examines the drawings of a student, Petra. He places a piece of tracing paper over her drawing and begins to draw over it, and at the same time, to talk. His talking is neither an explanation of the drawing, nor a parallel activity. Rather, talking and drawing take up a single language... I call this drawing and talking the language of designing."

- Schön's own gloss on the dialogue's opening exchange, on Quist's demonstrated-not-stated judgment: "Quist has not applied to the particular situation before him the general rules drawn from his past experience... He constructs variations on themes with which he is familiar."

- On the tacit, unreflected-on character of the studio's core competence — directly on point for "cannot state as rules" (near end of the reflection-in-action section): "Even here, I think, the process is still largely implicit. Architects appear to reflect very little on their own practice of reflection-in-action."

- The framing question that opens the pedagogy section (this is close to the paper's exact "the student cannot understand the telling until she has done it" theme): "The question is, how do students learn, with Quist's help, what Quist already knows how to do? What kinds of help can Quist, or other studio masters, give, especially to students who have trouble 'getting it'?"

- The Lauda case (a struggling student, quoting his instructor Leftwich) — illustrates a teacher who also cannot state the criteria: "Lauda is the hardest guy to deal with. Intelligent, articulate, comes up with something that works, but architecturally it's horrible. Now, what do I do? In a way, it's the kind of case which precipitates the weakest responses, because he has not internalized some of the covert things..." and the student's own words: "I want to go out and learn first. I want to know what it is we are arguing about."

- On students' felt experience of not being able to grasp criteria until later (this is the clearest primary-source line for "cannot understand until she has done it"): "Many students... spoke about the problem of 'getting it.' One described it as a Kafkaesque situation. You don't know where you are and have no basis for evaluation. You hang onto the inflection of the tone of voice in your crit to discover if something is really wrong."

Verbatim quotes with page cites (all quoted directly by Newman from the primary Schön texts, with page numbers):

- **THE PARADOX-OF-LEARNING / "cannot understand until she has done it" passage** (the single most valuable get for target 2), from *Educating the Reflective Practitioner*, p.83:
  > "The design studio shares in a general paradox attendant on the teaching and learning of any really new competence or understanding; for the student seeks to learn things whose meaning and importance she cannot grasp ahead of time. She is caught in the paradox Plato describes so vividly in his dialogue the Meno" (Schön, 1987, p.83).
  > "Like Meno, the design student knows she needs to look for something but does not know what that something is. She seeks to learn it, moreover, in the sense of coming to know it in action. Yet, at the beginning, she can neither do it nor recognize it when she sees it [...] The instructor is caught up in the same paradox: he cannot tell his student what she needs to know, even if he has words for it, because the student would not at that point understand him" (Schön, 1987, p.83).
  > "The logical paradox of the Meno accurately describes the experience of learning to design" (Schön, 1987, p.83).

- On the coach being unable to make the criteria explicit even if he tried, *Educating the Reflective Practitioner*, p.100:
  > coach "could produce good, clear, and compelling descriptions of designing, students [...] would be likely to find them confusing and mysterious" (Schön, 1987, p.100).

- Petra "does not and cannot understand what designing means" (Schön, 1987, p.82). [Note: Newman's own running text glosses this as a paraphrase-with-embedded-quote of Schön; the quoted fragment itself — "does not and cannot understand what designing means" — is presented in quotation marks with the p.82 pin, so treat the quoted fragment as verbatim, not the surrounding sentence.]

- On the resolution mechanism ("congruence of meaning"), dialogue becoming "elliptical," from *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983), p.81:
  > Quist and Petra "become more confident that they have achieved congruence of meaning" (Schön, 1983, p.81), following which "their dialogue tends to become elliptical and inscrutable to outsiders" (Schön, 1983, p.81).
  > "verbal and non-verbal dimensions are closely connected" (Schön, 1983, p.81).

- Quist described in *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983): "a master designer" (p.79); "unfailing virtuosity" and his demonstration is "masterful" (p.104).

- On reflection-in-action not being paralyzing in practice, *The Reflective Practitioner* (1983), p.281:
  > "in actual practice, practitioners do, without paralysis, reflect-in-action" (Schön, 1983, p.281); "The fear of a paralysis induced by reflection [...] comes not from the experience of practice" (Schön, 1983, p.281).

- The three "essential features" of the dialogue that bridges the communication gap, *Educating the Reflective Practitioner*, pp.100-101:
  > "Many succeed [...] in crossing over an apparently unbridgeable communication gap to a seeming convergence of meaning" by a dialogue having "three essential features: it takes place in the context of the student's attempts to design; it makes use of actions as well as words; and it depends on reciprocal reflection-in-action" (Schön, 1987, pp.100-101).

- From Schön (1981) "Learning a Language, Learning to Design" (in Porter & Kilbridge, eds.) — cited by Newman with page pins, relevant to the "designing is like speaking a language you don't yet have the rules for" framing: "the artifacts produced by the designer can themselves be conceived as utterances" (p.470, fn.5); "drawing and talking are complementary ways of designing; together [...] making up] the language of designing" (p.349); to design is "to 'speak,' in a way, and like speaking, designing can be fluent, halting, firm, or tentative" (p.396); "becoming an architect involves learning to play those games" (p.346); student "stuttering" who must "learn to speak fluently and clearly" (p.396). NOTE: this is a different, non-priority Schön source (1981 book chapter) not one of the 5 target keys — flagging in case it's independently useful, but not building a bibtex entry for it unless asked.

> "What designers know about their own problem-solving processes remains largely tacit knowledge – i.e. they know it in the same way that a skilled person 'knows' how to perform that skill. It may be satisfactory, or at least understandable, for practising designers to be inarticulate about their skills, but teachers of design have a responsibility to be as articulate as they possibly can about what it is they are trying to teach, or else they can have no basis for choosing the content and methods of their teaching."
