## (2) Top finds, ranked

### #1 — Guetzkow, Lamont & Mallard (2004), "What Is Originality in the Humanities and the Social Sciences?" *American Sociological Review* 69(2): 190–212. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** This is the strongest, most directly usable find. It documents, empirically and with interview evidence, that (a) written panel criteria are generic and *deliberately unspecified* — panelists get no operational definition of what the criteria mean or how to weigh them — yet (b) the single criterion panelists invoke most (~240 mentions, more than any other), "originality," isn't even named in most of the panels' own guidelines. What panelists actually reward under that label is a moral-aesthetic judgment ("passion," "authenticity," "aroma," courage) that never appears in any codified rubric. This is a clean instance of stated criteria (clarity, significance, feasibility) diverging from the substantive thing that actually drives the score.

> **[VERIFIED]** "Although reviewers are made aware of the suggested criteria for judging the proposals, they are not required to apply them. Indeed, reviewers are given almost complete autonomy — they are only accountable to the other panel members to whom they must explain their judgments. Moreover, the guidelines are very general, and panelists are given no indication of the specific meaning of the suggested criteria (such as feasibility or significance) or the weight to be given to each one."
> — p. 194. URL: https://scholar.harvard.edu/files/lamont/files/american_sociological_review-2004-guetzkow-190-212.pdf (fetched directly as PDF; JSTOR stable URL http://www.jstor.org/stable/3593084)

> **[VERIFIED]** "Only one of our fellowship programs (WWNFF) specifically mentioned the 'originality' of the proposal in their guidelines; yet it was of major concern to almost all the reviewers interviewed. In fact, the guidelines provided a baseline set of criteria for judging proposals that most reviewers had already internalized, and few of the reviewers expressed concern for or even knowledge of the institutional guidelines."
> — p. 195, same source.

> **[VERIFIED]** "Applicants' whose proposals were deemed original were often described with such adjectives as adventurous, ambitious, bold, courageous, independent, intellectually honest, curious, and risk-taking... At its core, this lexicon describes qualities that indicate whether or not one possesses intellectual authenticity."
> — p. 203, same source.

> **[VERIFIED]** panelist (historian) quote: "I don't flop over for joy when someone comes in and says, 'I use race, class, and gender as my categories.' That could be OK... but it's what everybody does. It's the line of least resistance... [it's] a nose for a real passion for ideas, regardless of whether they get the grant or not."
> — pp. 204–205 (composite of two adjacent panelist quotes on the same theme; see PDF pp. 204–205), same source.

> **[VERIFIED]** "Panelists often equated originality with the moral character of the researcher (especially his/her authenticity and integrity), and that this judgment plays a role in their evaluation... This contribution constitutes a new approach to the study of peer review and originality that focuses on the meaning of criteria of evaluation."
> — p. 190–191 (abstract/intro), same source.

Methodologically notable: coding required "consensus... among all three authors" for ambiguous statements (p. 196) — i.e., even the *researchers'* own classification of what counts as an originality-statement required inter-coder deliberation, which is itself a small mirror of the panel-level phenomenon being studied.

### #2 — Michèle Lamont, *How Professors Think: Inside the Curious World of Academic Judgment* (Harvard UP, 2009). **[SCHOLARLY BOOK]**

**Why it shows a gap:** The canonical text for this literature. Chapter 4 ("Pragmatic Fairness: Customary Rules of Deliberation") and Chapter 5 ("Recognizing Various Kinds of Excellence") are built entirely around the claim that panels reach agreement through *procedural/interactional norms*, not shared explicit criteria, and that "excellence" is recognized via "gut feelings," intuition, and flair that resist codification. I could not get a direct full-text fetch (Google Books preview and archive.org lending copy are both non-scrapable — see Dead Ends), so the quotes below are drawn from a scholarly review (Bryn Mawr Classical Review) that itself quotes the book, and from search-engine synthesis of multiple secondary sources citing the book. Treat these as secondary-sourced, not independently fetched from the book itself.

> **[SNIPPET]** (quoted directly in review, sourced to the book, p. 227) "a good proposal on the table gets a better judgment because of the institution from which it comes" — and panelists bring "a host of judgments, expectations, and anecdotes that are not to be found in the folders they read, but that strongly influence their judgment."
> Source: Bryn Mawr Classical Review, https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2009/2009.09.42/ (fetched; review quotes Lamont directly with page cite 227).

> **[SNIPPET]** review also notes panelists cite "'significance' and 'originality'... alongside judgments of 'humility,' 'determination,' and 'authenticity,' with 'cultural ease' and 'elegance' also scoring points" — same source, page unknown within that passage.

> **[LEAD]** (search-engine synthesis, not independently fetched) Lamont's stated conclusion: "the technology of deliberation, with all the customary rules... cannot level out personal preferences," because "academia is a highly variegated world, one where qualitatively incommensurate proposals cannot be subsumed under a single standard." Also described (secondhand) as treating "gut feelings," intuition and flair as intrinsic — not corrupting — inputs to "comparative and contextual judgment." Page unknown; needs direct-book verification before quoting in the paper. Chapter titles ("Pragmatic Fairness: Customary Rules of Deliberation"; "Recognizing Various Kinds of Excellence") are confirmed via multiple listings (JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt13x0gx9, Harvard UP page https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674057333).

Recommend: pull actual library/physical copy or Perlego/library-proxy access to get page-cited quotes before final paper use — this is almost certainly your best single source and worth the extra verification effort.

### #3 — Lamont & Huutoniemi (2011), "Comparing Customary Rules of Fairness: Evaluative Practices in Various Types of Peer Review Panels," in *Social Knowledge in the Making* (Univ. of Chicago Press) / Harvard DASH. **[ACADEMIC STUDY / book chapter]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Argues explicitly that panel fairness is produced by *intersubjective, non-codified* "customary rules," not by shared explicit standards, and that panelists actively "calibrate" — i.e., adjust their own private sense of quality to match an emergent group standard, which is a paradigm case of using a criterion you can enact but not fully specify in advance.

> **[SNIPPET]** "the fairness of the process is not undermined by nonrational features but is created through intersubjective rules that evaluators follow to distinguish between legitimate and illegitimate behavior."
> Source URL: https://dash.harvard.edu/handle/1/11320622 / ResearchGate record https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256230063 (both blocked from direct fetch — 403/405; quote reconstructed from WebSearch snippet synthesis of the indexed text, so treat as SNIPPET not VERIFIED). Page unknown.

> **[SNIPPET]** describing Huutoniemi's related concept of calibration: panelists' calibrating activity involves "adjusting their individual senses of quality to a group standard in order to form a concerted evaluation." Page unknown; same access caveat.

Direct-fetch attempts on dash.harvard.edu (405) and researchgate.net (403) both failed; need library/proxy access for page-cited quotes.

### #4 — Guetzkow, Lamont & Mallard (2009), "Fairness as Appropriateness: Negotiating Epistemological Differences in Peer Review," *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 34(5): 573–606. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Same interview corpus as #1 (81 interviews, 5 multidisciplinary fellowship panels). Argues panelists resolve cross-disciplinary disagreement not by appeal to a shared explicit standard of quality but by negotiating what counts as an "appropriate" way to evaluate a given kind of proposal — i.e., the standard of fairness itself is locally constructed in deliberation, not given in advance.

> **[LEAD]** No verbatim quote obtained — paywalled (Sage), and I could not locate an open PDF within the search budget. Abstract-level description confirmed via PhilPapers (https://philpapers.org/rec/GUEFAA) and Harvard Scholar listing (https://scholar.harvard.edu/lamont/publications/fairness-appropriateness-negotiating-epistemological-differences-peer-review). Corpus: IDRF (SSRC/ACLS), WWNFF Women's Studies Dissertation program, ACLS Humanities Fellowship.

### #5 — Roumbanis (2021), "The Oracles of Science: On Grant Peer Review and Competitive Funding," *Social Studies of Science* / STHV-adjacent venue. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Explicitly frames grant assessment of "promise," "innovation," and future impact as resting on individual reviewers' "connoisseurship" and "skilled academic intuition" — i.e., a form of professional taste that functions like the wine/art connoisseurship literature: reliable-feeling to the practitioner, resistant to specification, and arbitrated by panel consensus rather than measurement.

> **[LEAD]** (terms appear to be genuine terms-of-art from the paper per search-engine synthesis, but not independently fetched from full text — journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/05390184211019241 is paywalled, WebFetch returned only nav shell) "connoisseurship," "skilled academic intuition," and description of ex ante project assessment as "deeply dependent on the professional 'gut feelings' of individual reviewers and their ability to guess wisely." Page unknown. Needs direct access (library proxy / ResearchGate author copy) to convert to VERIFIED.

### #6 — Roumbanis (2019), "Peer Review or Lottery? A Critical Analysis of Two Different Forms of Decision-making Mechanisms for Allocation of Research Grants," *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 44(6): 994–1019. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Uses the reliability failures of peer review (cf. Cole et al. 1981, Pier et al. 2018 — both already in our bib) to argue that if panels can't reliably reproduce their own rankings, the implicit standard being applied cannot be a stable, specifiable one — motivating lottery-based funding as an honest alternative to pretending the panel is measuring something well-defined.

> **[LEAD]** Abstract line confirmed via search snippet: "The ex ante evaluation of scientific novelty is a fundamentally uncertain endeavor; bias and chance are embedded in the final outcome." Source: JSTOR https://www.jstor.org/stable/26786299, Sage https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0162243918822744 (both paywalled; WebFetch blocked). Page unknown.

### #7 — Brunet & Müller (2022), "Making the Cut: How Panel Reviewers Use Evaluation Devices to Select Applications at the European Research Council," *Research Evaluation* 31(4): 486–497. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Not in the user's suggested list, but a strong, directly on-topic, fully-verified new find (I initially mis-attributed this to Roumbanis in search snippets — correct authors are Lucas Brunet and Ruth Müller). It documents ERC reviewers using tacit "calibration devices" to develop what they call an "almost embodied" sense of relative quality across a proposal pool, explicitly contrasted with applying any single specifiable rule — and shows individual reviewers disagreeing on how strictly to combine evaluation elements even while reaching consensus on final rankings.

> **[VERIFIED]** "Lacking confidence" to evaluate proposals from various research fields, these reviewers attempted to get a "feel for the sample" and "calibrate" themselves accordingly.
> — p. 492. URL: https://academic.oup.com/rev/article/31/4/486/6845514 (fetched directly).

> **[VERIFIED]** "panel reviewers did not assess any specific aspect of each proposal but rather looked to establish a preliminary, almost embodied, impression of the whole set."
> — p. 493, same source.

> **[VERIFIED]** "There was no consensus between panel reviewers about how strong or loose the articulation between different elements should be; preferences were largely individual."
> — p. 494, same source.

> **[VERIFIED]** reviewers "aim to develop an almost embodied skill to classify proposals quickly in relation to each other."
> — p. 497, same source.

Note on framing: the authors' own gloss is that reviewers *can* name their devices (delegation, calibration, articulation, contribution devices) — they call the overall mode "evaluative pragmatism." So this source cuts slightly against a strong tacit-knowledge reading (the authors would resist "panelists cannot articulate anything"), but the *content* of what gets assessed within each device (the "feel," the "almost embodied impression") is squarely an articulation-gap phenomenon. Use it for the mechanism, cite the authors' pragmatism framing honestly.

### #8 — Huutoniemi (2012), "Communicating and Compromising on Disciplinary Expertise in the Peer Review of Research Proposals" / related work on evaluating interdisciplinary research. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Panelists from different disciplines, each carrying "implicit epistemic standards of research quality," reach agreement on interdisciplinary proposals via negotiated "customary rules of deliberation" rather than a shared explicit rubric; interdisciplinarity itself functions as a "plus" criterion that panelists treat as recognizable but do not (and per the literature, cannot) reduce to a checklist.

> **[LEAD]** "panel members with different backgrounds develop shared customary rules of deliberation that facilitate agreement" despite "different disciplinary evaluative cultures." Source: search-engine synthesis of https://www.researchgate.net/publication/256229253 ("Evaluating interdisciplinary research") — not independently fetched (ResearchGate blocks WebFetch). Page unknown.

### #9 — Langfeldt (2001), "The Decision-Making Constraints and Processes of Grant Peer Review, and Their Effects on the Review Outcome," *Social Studies of Science* 31(6): 820–841. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Empirical study of the Research Council of Norway showing "there are no clear norms for assessments and large variation in what criteria reviewers use," and that who happens to review a given proposal, and how the panel process is organized, can determine the outcome as much as any stated standard — evidence that the written criteria underdetermine the score.

> **[LEAD]** No verbatim text obtained (ResearchGate PDF page returned nav-only). Paraphrase from search synthesis: "there are no clear norms for assessments and large variation in what criteria reviewers use... the determinants of peer review may be accidental." Source: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/238432356. Page unknown.

### #10 — Olbrecht & Bornmann (2010), "Panel Peer Review of Grant Applications: What Do We Know from Research in Social Psychology on Judgment and Decision-Making in Groups?" *Research Evaluation* 19(4): 293–304. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Reviews social-psychology evidence that panel consensus on grant quality is produced by group process dynamics (motivation, mutual persuasion, weighting of arguments in discussion) rather than by convergence on a shared explicit standard — relevant as a mechanism-level account of *how* panels manufacture agreement without a codified criterion.

> **[LEAD]** No verbatim quote obtained (abstract-only access). Source: https://academic.oup.com/rev/article-abstract/19/4/293/1562622. Page unknown.

### #11 — Travis & Collins (1991), "New Light on Old Boys: Cognitive and Institutional Particularism in the Peer Review System," *Science, Technology, & Human Values* 16(3): 322–341. **[ACADEMIC STUDY]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Based on observation of UK Science and Engineering Research Council grant committees. Distinguishes "institutional particularism" (favoritism toward known people/places) from the more interesting "cognitive particularism" — reviewers judge favorably work that shares their own tacit standards of what counts as good science, standards that are local to a research network and not written down anywhere.

> **[LEAD]** No verbatim quote obtained (behind Sage paywall). Paraphrase from search synthesis: reviewers exhibit "cognitive particularism" — evaluating proposals against standards specific to their own research network/tradition rather than a discipline-wide codified standard — "more important than institutional cronyism." Source: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/016224399101600303. Page unknown.

### #12 — Gemma Derrick, *The Evaluators' Eye: Impact Assessment and Academic Peer Review* (Palgrave, 2018). **[SCHOLARLY BOOK]**

**Why it shows a gap:** Interview study of REF2014 Main Panel A evaluators on how they collectively arrived at a working definition of "societal impact" — a criterion introduced into UK research assessment with essentially no prior operational definition. The book's argument is that panels construct a *de facto* dominant reading of an ambiguous criterion through deliberation, not by applying a definition handed to them.

> **[LEAD]** No page-cited quote obtained — Google Books preview pages render as images, not extractable text, via WebFetch; direct book access needed. Description confirmed via multiple publisher/bookseller listings (Palgrave https://www.palgrave.com/gp/book/9783319636269; Amazon; WorldCat).

### #13 — Wennerås & Wold (1997), "Nepotism and Sexism in Peer Review," *Nature* 387: 341–343. **[ACADEMIC STUDY — supporting/secondary relevance]**

**Why it's relevant (weaker fit):** Not primarily about articulation gaps — it's a bias/reliability study — but it is the founding demonstration that Swedish Medical Research Council postdoctoral competence scores did not track objective productivity in the way the stated criteria implied (female applicants needed to be markedly more productive for equal competence scores), i.e., stated scientific-merit criteria were not in fact driving the score. Useful as corroborating evidence of criteria/outcome divergence, less central than #1–#7.

> **[LEAD]** No verbatim quote obtained (Nature paywalled). Widely reported finding: female applicants had to be roughly 2.5x more productive than male applicants to receive the same competence score; applicants with personal connections to reviewers received un-warranted "extra" points. Source: https://www.nature.com/articles/387341a0. Page unknown.
