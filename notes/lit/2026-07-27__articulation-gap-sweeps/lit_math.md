## 2. Top new finds (ranked)

### #1 — Poincaré, H. (1908/1910), "Mathematical Creation" [ESSAY-TESTIMONY]
Originally a 1908 lecture to the Société de Psychologie, published as a chapter of *Science et Méthode* (1908) / English in *The Foundations of Science* (1913, trans. G. B. Halsted); reprinted in *The Monist* 20 (1910) and in Newman's *The World of Mathematics* (1956), and quoted at length in Hadamard (1945).

Why it shows a gap: the founding testimony that mathematical invention is governed by an "aesthetic sieve" whose selection rules the mathematician who uses them **cannot state** — they can only be felt. This is a direct first-person articulation-gap claim, a full 100+ years before empirical HCI/psych work confirmed the pattern.

> "The true work of the inventor consists in choosing among these combinations so as to eliminate the useless ones ... the rules which must guide this choice are extremely fine and delicate. **It is almost impossible to state them precisely; they are felt rather than formulated.**"

> "Thus it is this special esthetic sensibility which plays the role of **the delicate sieve** of which I spoke, and that sufficiently explains why the one lacking it will never be a real creator."

### #2 — Thurston, W. P. (1994), "On Proof and Progress in Mathematics." *Bulletin of the AMS* 30(2), 161–177. [ESSAY-TESTIMONY, by a Fields medalist, but structured as a reflective argument with examples — closest thing to a scholarly essay in this literature]
arXiv: math/9404236.

Why it shows a gap: written explicitly against the idea that mathematics *is* formal proof; argues the community's actual standard of validity is social/tacit, transmitted person-to-person via non-verbal channels, and that most known results have no fully checked formal derivation anyone could point to.

> "People are usually not very good in checking formal correctness of proofs, but they are quite good at detecting potential weaknesses or flaws in proofs."

> "When people are doing mathematics, the flow of ideas and the social standard of validity is much more reliable than formal documents ... it is preposterous to claim that mathematics as we practice it is anywhere near formally correct."

> "One-on-one, people use wide channels of communication that go far beyond formal mathematical language. They use gestures, they draw pictures and diagrams, they make sound effects and use body language."

> "Many of the things that are generally known are things for which there may be no known written source. As long as people in the field are comfortable that the idea works, it doesn't need to have a formal written source." / "Mathematical knowledge and understanding were embedded in the minds and in the social fabric of the community."

> "[the] definition-theorem-proof (DTP) model of mathematics ... doesn't explain the source of the questions."

Page numbers unknown from this rendering (original: *Bull. AMS* 30(2):161–177); page-locate against a numbered copy before citing exact pp.

### #3 — Burton, L. (1999), "Why Is Intuition So Important to Mathematicians but Missing from Mathematics Education?" *For the Learning of Mathematics* 19(3), 27–32. [ACADEMIC STUDY — interview study, n=70 research mathematicians, UK/Ireland; companion to her book *Mathematicians as Enquirers*, 2004]

Why it shows a gap: direct interview evidence that mathematicians (a) rely on intuition/insight as a necessary epistemic tool, (b) cannot agree among themselves on what to call it or how to recognize it, and (c) that the formal write-up of mathematics systematically erases the intuitive process that produced it.

> "insight is seeing a connection; if the light switches on when I look at a problem, I have had an insight" (p. 28)

> "There was no agreement about whether these terms represented different, distinctive states and, if so, how these states might be recognised" (p. 28)

> "you cannot base a theory on intuition; you must show the connections rigorously" (p. 28)

> "The apparent need for mathematics to be infallible and objective requires the removal of the evidence of the very human beings who created and worked with the mathematical objects, missing entirely the stumbling human process that created those results" (p. 29)

> "the literature of mathematics education does not address intuition as a focus of interest in classrooms" (p. 30); "accounts of the deliberate nurturing of intuition and insight is absent from the mathematics education literature" (p. 31)

**[VERIFIED]** — fetched via https://r.jina.ai/https://flm-journal.org/Articles/2F55B04AB06CE01EC5602F306993D7.pdf; all quotes above appeared in the retrieved text with page numbers as rendered by the source.

### #4 — Geist, C., Löwe, B. & Van Kerkhove, B. (2010), "Peer Review and Knowledge by Testimony in Mathematics." In B. Löwe & T. Müller (eds.), *Philosophy of Mathematical Practice: Sociological Aspects and Mathematical Practice*, College Publications, 155–178. [ACADEMIC STUDY — includes an editor survey and interviews]

Why it shows a gap: empirical study of how mathematics referees actually decide accept/reject; shows the community does NOT converge on a stated, uniform standard for "checked" — referees decide how much to verify based on author reputation, plausibility-feel, and division of epistemic responsibility, not a specified rule, yet the process is treated as reliable ("testimony").

> Editor survey: only six of eleven respondents agreed referees should check all proofs in detail; one commented "but to be reasonable, I am happy when I find a referee doing (b)" [i.e., checking only some proofs].

> Interviewed mathematician on deciding whether to trust an unverified step: "Then you say 'yes, this really seems plausible, but I'm not really sure if it's true' ... you end up with the question 'is this because I don't have enough knowledge?' ... Then you have a tendency of believing that it is correct ... he's publishing it, not I, so it's his responsibility."

> Editor: "There are situations where almost nothing needs be checked (e.g., the author is well-known and it is his concern to submit a correct paper). There are situations when I insist to check all the procedures (e.g., when it concerns good results from a less known author)."

### #5 — Wells, D. (1990), "Are These the Most Beautiful?" *The Mathematical Intelligencer* 12(3), 37–41. [ACADEMIC STUDY — reader survey, n=68 usable responses rating 24 theorems 0–10 for beauty]

Why it shows a gap: an empirical beauty-rating survey whose own respondents, in free-text comments, contested the entire premise that theorems (as opposed to proofs/ideas) can be rated for beauty at all, and explicitly called beauty historically/culturally contingent and resistant to "numerical interpretation" — i.e., verdicts were collected, but the criteria behind them were disputed/inarticulable even by the raters themselves.

> Wells's own conclusion: "the idea that mathematicians largely agree in their aesthetic judgements is at best grossly oversimplified."

> Respondent Tony Gardiner: "Theorems aren't usually 'beautiful'. It's the ideas and proofs that appeal."

> Respondent Tito Tonietti: "Beauty, even in mathematics, depends upon historical and cultural contexts, and therefore tends to elude numerical interpretation."

> Respondent Jonathan Watson: "beauty is a human criterion. And beauty is tied to usefulness."

**[VERIFIED]** — fetched via https://r.jina.ai/https://www.matmedia.it/wp-content/uploads/2020/01/1990-wells_beauty.pdf; quotes confirmed present. Page numbers not resolved in the rendered text (original pp. 37–41).

Note: this is a *complicating* find relative to our anchor — Wells argues mathematicians *disagree* on beauty rankings themselves, in some tension with later comparative-judgement work (#6 below) that finds agreement once methodology is fixed. Worth citing both to show the field's own instability on "do experts even agree."

### #6 — Sa, R., Alcock, L., Inglis, M. & Tanswell, F. S. (2024), "Do Mathematicians Agree about Mathematical Beauty?" *Review of Philosophy and Psychology*. https://doi.org/10.1007/s13164-022-00669-3 [ACADEMIC STUDY — comparative-judgement design, British + Chinese mathematicians + British undergraduates]

Why it shows a gap: reconciles Wells's disagreement finding by showing agreement DOES emerge when you ask for *comparative* judgements (this is more beautiful than that) rather than *absolute* ratings — implying mathematicians can reliably discriminate beauty in a forced-choice sense without being able to state absolute, portable criteria. Also finds that "simplicity," when operationalized crudely as character-count, is at best a weak/partial predictor, echoing the 2015 Inglis-Aberdein dissociation.

> "there is a significant and strong positive correlation between the two sets of scores r = .846" (British vs Chinese mathematicians); British undergraduates vs mathematicians r = .781.

> "mathematicians might have different absolute standards for beauty and thus appear to disagree when asked for absolute judgements, but might nevertheless agree about which objects are more or less beautiful."

> "Number of characters in an equation is clearly a crude measure of simplicity" — flagging that even their own operationalized "criterion" is acknowledged as inadequate to what's actually being judged.

**[VERIFIED]** — fetched via https://r.jina.ai/https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13164-022-00669-3; quotes confirmed present in retrieved abstract/results text. Page unknown (online-first pagination).

### #7 — Rota, G.-C. (1997), "The Phenomenology of Mathematical Beauty." *Synthese* 111(2), 171–182. [ESSAY-TESTIMONY, by a working mathematician/philosopher]

Why it shows a gap: argues mathematical beauty is a real, intersubjectively recognized property that nonetheless resists definition — mathematicians "pass judgment" fluently but there is no "mathematical beauty appreciation" curriculum, unlike the arts, implying the standard is transmitted by acculturation/exposure rather than statable rule.

> "Whereas painters and musicians are likely to be embarrassed by references to the beauty in their work, mathematicians instead like to engage in discussions of the beauty of mathematics ... it is however unthinkable to find any 'mathematical beauty appreciation' courses taught anywhere."

> "The most common instance of beauty in mathematics is a brilliant step in an otherwise undistinguished proof."

> "Mathematical beauty and mathematical truth share the fundamental property of objectivity, that of being inescapably context-dependent." / "A theorem that is in one context thought to be beautiful may in a different context appear trivial."

**[SNIPPET]** — quotes drawn from secondary sources quoting Rota verbatim: https://quotablemath.blogspot.com/2019/07/gian-carlo-rota-collected-quotes.html and search-result excerpts citing arXiv:2405.05379 ("Reflecting on beauty: the aesthetics of mathematical discovery"). The Springer/Ovid paywalled original (10.1023/A:1004930722234) was not directly fetched — treat page numbers as unverified until checked against the print original (pp. 171–182).

### #8 — Inglis, M. & Alcock, L. (2012), "Expert and Novice Approaches to Reading Mathematical Proofs." *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education* 43(4), 358–390. [ACADEMIC STUDY — eye-tracking, n=39 (mathematicians + undergrads)]

Why it's relevant: mathematicians validate proofs by *evaluating whether each step follows*, not by parsing surface symbols — a documented behavioral signature of expert judgment that experts themselves rarely describe when asked directly to state "how" they check a proof (per the companion Weber/Mejía-Ramos survey literature, #9). This is the closest thing to Inglis's own eye-tracking analogue of the Inglis & Aberdein beauty-appraisal design, applied to validity rather than beauty.

Reported (via search-result summaries, not fetched primary text): all mathematicians correctly flagged proofs with clear mistakes as invalid; undergraduates did so only ~50% of the time; mathematicians spent more time evaluating step-to-step inference, undergraduates more time on surface symbol manipulation.

### #9 — Weber, K. & Mejía-Ramos, J. P. — proof-reading/conviction survey line:
- Weber, K. & Mejía-Ramos, J. P. (2011), "Why and how mathematicians read proofs: An exploratory study." *Educational Studies in Mathematics* 76(3), 329–344.

### #10 — Inglis, M. & Aberdein, A. (2020), "Are Aesthetic Judgements Purely Aesthetic? Testing the Social Conformity Account." *ZDM – Mathematics Education* 52(6), 1127–1136.

Why relevant: direct evidence that stated aesthetic verdicts are *socially contaminated* — pure mathematicians rated the same proof as more beautiful when told it came from "Proofs from THE BOOK." This is a different, sharper kind of gap than inarticulacy: it shows the appraisal mathematicians report is not even a stable readout of their own private aesthetic reaction, but partly a conformity signal — complicating any claim that stated criteria simply lag behind a fixed "real" internal standard.

**[LEAD]** — abstract/finding only from search-result summary; primary text (repository.lboro.ac.uk, ZDM/Springer) returned 403/paywall on fetch attempts. No verbatim quote verified.

### #11 — Mejía Ramos, J. P., Evans, T., Rittberg, C. & Inglis, M. (2021), "Mathematicians' Assessments of the Explanatory Value of Proofs." *Axiomathes* 31(5), 575–599 (also indexed under *Global Philosophy*).

Why relevant: comparative-judgement study (n=38 mathematicians) on which proofs are "explanatory." Found high inter-rater agreement on explanatoriness rankings but results that were *inconsistent with claims in the philosophical literature* about what makes a proof explanatory — i.e., mathematicians converge on a verdict the philosophy-of-math literature's stated theory does not predict. Structurally this is exactly the Inglis-Aberdein template (agree on the ranking, disagree with/undercut the named criterion) applied to "explanatory value" instead of "beauty."

**[LEAD]** — found via search-result summary only; not fetched. Worth a dedicated follow-up given how close the design is to the anchor paper.

### #12 — Davis, P. J. & Hersh, R. (1981), *The Mathematical Experience*. Birkhäuser. Chapter "The Ideal Mathematician" (excerpted in Tymoczko, ed., *New Directions in the Philosophy of Mathematics*, 1986, pp. 34–43). [ESSAY-TESTIMONY / SCHOLARLY BOOK, semi-satirical composite portrait]
