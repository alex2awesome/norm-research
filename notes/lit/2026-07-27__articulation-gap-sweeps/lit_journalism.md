## 2. Top finds (ranked, best first)

### 1. Herbert J. Gans, *Deciding What's News: A Study of CBS Evening News, NBC Nightly News, Newsweek, and Time* (Northwestern University Press, 1979) — ACADEMIC STUDY (participant-observation newsroom ethnography)

Why it fits: Gans's entire project is to enumerate the explicit "considerations" editors use — yet he repeatedly breaks from his own taxonomy to admit the actual judgment is felt, not ruled.

> "news judgment is 'partly a matter of feel'" (Gans, 1979, p. 171)

> journalists "act on the basis of quick, virtually intuitive judgments, which some ascribe to 'feel'" (Gans, 1979, p. 82)

**[SNIPPET]** — found via De Maeyer, J. (2020), "'A Nose for News': From (News) Values to Valuation," *Sociologica*, 14(2), 109–132, which quotes Gans verbatim with page numbers. I fetched De Maeyer's full HTML text directly at https://sociologica.unibo.it/article/download/11176/11563 and confirmed the quoted strings appear there attributed to Gans 1979 pp. 82 and 171. I did not fetch Gans's book itself, so the primary-text location is SNIPPET-level (via a secondary source), but the secondary source is fully verified and the page numbers come from it.

This is a strong catch precisely because Gans is usually read as the paradigm case of an *explicit*-factors study (his book is a taxonomy of "considerations"), which makes his own admission that the underlying judgment is "a matter of feel" a good example of a scholar's explicit apparatus failing to capture what practitioners actually do.

### 4. Edward Jay Epstein, *News from Nowhere: Television and the News* (Random House, 1973) — ACADEMIC STUDY (participant-observation of NBC News production)

**[VERIFIED]** — I downloaded and OCR'd the full book text (worldradiohistory.com/BOOKSHELF-ARH/News/News-From-Nowhere-Epstein-1973.pdf) and confirmed the following passages appear verbatim (original-book page numbers, from running headers embedded in the OCR):

> "In terms of content, however, the producers' unwritten but generally known preferences for certain types of stories are of much more importance to correspondents, who generally find it necessary to 'sell themselves' to producers of the news programs by the kind of stories they do." (p. 168–169)

> "The producer's subordinates had an unwritten list of about half a dozen correspondents who were not to be used, except in the case of 'earth-shattering news' … The reasons for blacklisting these correspondents, as the practice was known, varied, according to executive producers' offhanded descriptions, from their age ('too old') to their general appearance ('lack of sex appeal') to their presentation ('not punchy enough' or 'no sense of humor')." (p. 189)

> On refusing to explain news judgments to an outside investigator: "Salant said that he would refuse to answer any questions about news judgments and would only agree to rerun film or tape that had already been broadcast." (p. 10, quoting an internal NBC memo)

Why it fits: this is a rare 1970s participant-observation study *inside* a network newsroom (NBC), and it repeatedly documents that the actual selection criteria are "unwritten," known only informally, applied via "offhanded" (unsystematized, ad hoc) descriptions, and that news executives actively resist having their "news judgments" scrutinized or made explicit at all — a strong empirical case of criteria that exist, produce consistent outcomes (correspondents are reliably blacklisted/favored), but are not, and are not meant to be, articulated.

### 6. Warren Breed, "Social Control in the Newsroom: A Functional Analysis," *Social Forces* 33(4) (1955), pp. 326–335 — ACADEMIC STUDY (classic; interviews with reporters/editors)

Core claim (paraphrase, confirmed present on a secondary commentary page but I could not access the JSTOR/Oxford Academic primary text, which is paywalled): new reporters are never told the paper's editorial "policy" directly; they learn it only indirectly — by reading their own paper to see what runs on page one, from the editing of their own copy, from reprimands and compliments, from newsroom gossip, and by observing executives — i.e., normatively-loaded judgment acquired by osmosis, not instruction.

> "New journalists are never told what the policy is, or what slant to put on a story." (paraphrase, not in quotation marks in the source page)

One genuine verbatim quote with a page cite did surface:

> Breed, on how ethical/values talk gets displaced by newsroom pressure: "but not when there is news to get. News comes first ...." (Breed, 1955, p. 117, as reprinted in *Moral Compass* — page number is for the reprint, not the original *Social Forces* pagination)

**[SNIPPET]** — both from https://www.infoamerica.org/teoria_articulos/Warren%20Breed.htm (a professor's reading notes page, not Breed's own text). I could not locate an open-access copy of the original 1955 article (JSTOR/Oxford Academic paywalled; ResearchGate/Semantic Scholar links to a related retrospective essay were blocked by Cloudflare). This is the single most load-bearing "osmosis, not instruction" citation in the newsroom-ethnography literature and is very likely worth a direct JSTOR pull if the user has institutional access — I would expect the primary text to contain much stronger verbatim material than what I could verify here.
