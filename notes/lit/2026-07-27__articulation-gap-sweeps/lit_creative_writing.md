## 2. Top finds, ranked

### (1) The Chuck Ross "Steps" experiment — Jerzy Kosinski's award-winning novel, retyped, rejected by every publisher and agent it went to
**TRADE-PRACTITIONER / journalistic case study (widely documented natural experiment)**

Why it's a gap: this is a natural experiment in which the *same text*, judged blind of authorial reputation, was read by professional editors who (a) sometimes praised its craft in writing but (b) still rejected it, and (c) uniformly failed to recognize a National Book Award winner. It shows the operative criterion in acquisition decisions is not the stated literary-quality criteria editors would give if asked, but something else (name recognition / marketability) that overrides their own express praise.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "Several of us read your untitled novel here with admiration for writing and style... The drawback...is that it doesn't add up to a satisfactory whole. It has some very impressive moments, but gives the impression of sketchiness and incompleteness." — Houghton Mifflin's rejection letter, quoted in hoaxes.org, "The Steps Experiment (1975)," https://hoaxes.org/archive/permalink/the_steps_experiment (page unknown — web archive)

> [VERIFIED, fetched] Ross's own gloss: "Evidently, Kosinski is not as good as Kosinski when Demos is the name on the envelope." — same source.

Caveat (report honestly): critic M.A. Orthofer counters that *Steps* itself is a relatively minor, forgettable Kosinski work, so the experiment may show the novel's inherent obscurity more than editorial blindness — a live methodological objection, not a clean demonstration. Still, the Houghton Mifflin quote is independently useful: praise of craft that does not predict the accept/reject decision is exactly an articulation-gap signature (stated positive assessment ≠ actual verdict).

### (2) Doris Lessing's "Jane Somers" pseudonym experiment (1983–84)
**TRADE-PRACTITIONER (author's own account, contemporaneous NYT interview)**

A Nobel laureate submitted two novels under a pseudonym to her own long-term British publisher, who rejected both as not commercially viable / "too depressing." Some critics who reviewed the pseudonymous novels favorably had no idea it was Lessing. This is one of the cleanest anecdotal demonstrations that literary "quality" judgments made by professional gatekeepers are entangled with authorial reputation in ways the judges themselves would not endorse if asked to state their criteria.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] Lessing, NYT interview (1984): "I wanted to highlight that whole dreadful process in book publishing that 'nothing succeeds like success.'" — National Library of Scotland blog, https://blog.nls.uk/pen-names-doris-lessing-to-jane-somers/ (page unknown)

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "Some of the so-called experts on my work (…) didn't recognise it was me." — same source.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "…what happens mostly is that an immense amount of space will be given to not very good books by established writers." — same source.

### (3) David Lassman's "Rejecting Jane" experiment (2007) — Jane Austen's opening chapters resubmitted under a pseudonym
**TRADE-PRACTITIONER / journalistic case study**

Lassman submitted lightly altered opening chapters of *Pride and Prejudice*, *Northanger Abbey*, and *Persuasion* (with Austen's famous opening line left completely intact) to 18 major UK publishers/agents under the pseudonym "Alison Laydee." All but one failed to recognize the text; most rejections were generic form language.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] Form rejections: "not right for our list," "not publishing any new fiction," "not confident of placing this material with a publisher." — Victoria Strauss, "Whoops, They Did It Again," Writer Beware blog, https://writerbeware.blog/2007/08/17/victoria-strauss-whoops-they-did-it-again/ (page unknown)

> [VERIFIED, fetched] The one editor who caught it, Alex Bowler (Jonathan Cape): "Thank you for sending us the first two chapters of First Impressions; my first impressions on reading these were ones of disbelief and mild annoyance—along, of course, with a moment's laughter." — same source.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] Lassman's own conclusion: "If the major publishers can't recognise great literature, who knows what might be slipping through the net?" — same source.

Caveat (report honestly): Strauss's own post pushes back hard — she argues the experiment is confounded (wrong genre targets for several submissions, agent-only houses queried directly, a period prose style that reads as amateurish by contemporary standards, and the fact the opening lines were recognizable to begin with). Use this as a *contested* case, not a clean one — good material for a "trade lore vs. methodological scrutiny" discussion.

### (4) Amabile's Consensual Assessment Technique (CAT) — the premise is explicitly that there is no articulable definition of the construct
**ACADEMIC / SCHOLARLY (we already cite `amabile1982social`; this adds the exact operational-definition language plus a companion 2012 restatement)**

This is the load-bearing academic anchor for the whole "experts agree without being able to state criteria" claim in the creative domain. Verified via a fetched, page-numbered secondary source that quotes the 1982 definition directly and explicitly frames it as solving the problem that *no objective criteria for creativity exist*.

> [VERIFIED, fetched, p.8] "The CAT is rooted in a consensual operational definition of creativity: a product is creative to the extent that expert raters independently agree upon this judgment... Judges should be external observers with experience in the target domain, and should use their own subjective judgments of creativity to independently rate the products relative to one another... Because it relies on subjective assessment (as does real-world creativity), the CAT overcomes the difficulty of defining ultimate 'objective' criteria for creativity." — Amabile, T.M. & Pillemer, J. (2012 draft/submitted to *Journal of Creative Behavior* Dec. 2011), "Perspectives on the Social Psychology of Creativity," https://www.hbs.edu/ris/Publication%20Files/Perspectives_Soc%20Psych%20of%20Cre_Amabile-Pillemer_JCB_v07_12%2011%2011_08f9e648-8326-41a5-a93d-7f1d45c3d00c.pdf

**New citation to add**: `amabile2012perspectives` (Amabile & Pillemer, "Perspectives on the Social Psychology of Creativity," *Journal of Creative Behavior*).

### (6) Rebecca Makkai, "Book Prizes Don't Work How You Think" (Substack essay by a novelist and prize judge)
**TRADE-PRACTITIONER (verified, fetched directly)**

Excellent first-person account of literary-prize deliberation showing (a) panels never agree in advance on what dimension of quality they're even judging, (b) outcomes are driven by who can persuade whom rather than by scoring against stated criteria, and (c) advancement through rounds depends on essentially random first-reader mood/compatibility rather than a stable rubric.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "Judges usually communicate...and often at some point define their own criteria. Are they simply looking for the best-executed book...Or the most innovative? ... The book that everyone should still be reading in fifty years? The book that affected them the most?" — Rebecca Makkai, https://rebeccamakkai.substack.com/p/book-prizes-dont-work-how-you-think (page unknown; Substack essay, undated but self-described as 2020s)

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "Who can sway other judges? Who freezes and backs down?" — same source.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] On a book that judges voted for and then reversed after "reconsidering over lunch and wine" — decisions driven by social reconsideration, not fixed principles. Paraphrase of a documented anecdote in the same essay; get exact wording from the source before quoting verbatim in the paper.

> [VERIFIED, fetched] "...whether a book makes it to the next round can depend entirely on the tastes of one person...A person who...might not have had the luxury of putting a book down on a bad day and picking it up again in the right mood." — same source.

## 4. Dead ends

- **Csikszentmihalyi, *Creativity: Flow and the Psychology of Discovery and Invention* (1996)** — Honest verdict: I could NOT find evidence that this book contains material specifically about gatekeepers being *unable to articulate* their criteria. What it does contain (confirmed via multiple secondary summaries and a quotes compilation) is the systems model: creativity is validated by a "field" of expert gatekeepers via "social evaluation," and "there is no way to know whether a thought is new except with reference to some standards, and there is no way to tell whether it is valuable until it passes social evaluation." That is a sociological claim about *where* validation authority sits (in a field of experts, not in the object itself), not a claim that the experts' own criteria are inarticulable. It is adjacent to Bourdieu/Becker (both already cited) but does not itself supply an articulation-gap quote. Do not cite this book for the specific claim this paper needs; it fits better (if at all) as generic background on gatekeeping structure, which is already better covered by Becker and Bourdieu.
- **Slush-pile agreement/disagreement empirical studies** — searched directly; found none. All results were how-to/industry-explainer content (MasterClass, Jericho Writers, Well-Storied), not empirical studies of agreement rates among slush readers or editors. If such a study exists it is not well-indexed under obvious search terms; may need a database search (PsycINFO, Web of Science) rather than open web search.
- **"New Writing" journal, general "impossibility of assessment criteria" search** — did not surface a specific article making that exact argument. One tangentially related article was found (Suphap, "Workshopping failure pedagogy for creative writing studies," *New Writing* 20(2), 2022) but its subject is pedagogy around student failure/rejection, not assessment-criteria validity — not a good fit, do not cite for this claim.
- **Paris Review "Art of Editing" full interviews** — both Gottlieb's and Lish's interview pages are Cloudflare-protected (confirmed 403 via both WebFetch and a spoofed-user-agent curl request from this environment); could not get past the block to verify quotes beyond what search-engine snippets already surfaced.
