You are a BLIND adjudicator in a controlled measurement.

Below are 50 CANDIDATE channels (ids K01..K50) that a panel of writers proposed
while hunting for SUSPECTED-SPURIOUS scoring channels for machine-learning paper
abstracts (features that might predict how an abstract fares WITHOUT being a mark of
quality -- surface style, formatting, topic, or an upstream factor's textual
fingerprint), and 12 TARGET channels (ids T01..T12) drawn from a separate,
earlier census of declared-nuisance channels for the same corpus.

For EACH target, decide whether any candidate names the SAME underlying channel --
i.e. a judge scoring an abstract on the target would be scoring essentially the same
surface/upstream property as a judge scoring it on that candidate.

Calibration, and it matters:
  * Phrasing will differ -- the targets and candidates were written by different
    panels at different times. Judge the underlying property being scored, NOT the
    exact wording.
  * Do NOT accept mere topical adjacency. If the candidate is clearly broader and
    merely contains the target as one sub-case, or narrower and covers only a
    fragment of it, or shares a surface topic while scoring a different property --
    that is NOT a match.
  * A match means substitutable in practice: swapping one for the other as a scoring
    instruction would produce nearly the same per-abstract scores.
  * Some targets will have NO match. Reporting no match is a perfectly good answer and
    is expected for a substantial share of them. Do not force matches.

OUTPUT. Emit exactly one JSON object and nothing else -- no markdown fences, no
commentary:

{"results": [
  {"target": "T01",
   "match": true or false,
   "candidate_ids": ["K07", ...],   // every candidate you judge to be the same channel; [] if none
   "confidence": "high" or "low",
   "why": "<one sentence>"},
  ... one entry for every target T01..T12 ...
]}

=== CANDIDATES ===

K05. Abstract length and elaboration

K07. Self-described warning/whistleblower framing about risk or safety
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract explicitly frames its own findings as a warning, caution, or exposure of risk/vulnerability to the community (language like 'acts as a whistleblower,' 'emphasizes the need for caution,' 'highlights vulnerabilities,' or similar alarm-raising framing about safety/security implications). K08. Decimal-precision numeric result density
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much of the abstract's claimed results are expressed as precise numeric figures with two or more decimal places or exact percentages/ratios (e.g., '81.88%', '1.897x10^-3', '15~30x'), as opposed to results described only in qualitative or rounded terms. Score only how much fine-grained numeric precision appears in the text; do not judge whether the reported numbers indicate good or bad performance, or whether the precision is warranted.

K09. Generic Field-Importance Opening Platitudes
     Score how much the abstract opens with a sweeping, obvious statement about the importance of its subfield that reads like a generic problem statement (e.g., 'X is crucial for...', 'X has attracted much attention'). 0 means it dives straight into specifics, 10 means it opens with a generic platitude. Do not judge whether the platitude is true.

K10. Pioneering 'first-to-do-X' framing vs. named-single-antecedent refinement framing
     On a 0-10 scale, rate where the abstract's framing falls on this spectrum: a LOW score (0) means the text presents itself as one of the first, most obvious responses to a broad opportunity, using words like 'first,' 'novel,' 'we introduce' without naming one single specific prior method it is directly extending; a HIGH score (10) means the text presents itself as a late, already-informed refinement that names one specific prior method/paper and explains precisely what limitation of that specific antecedent it fixes. Score only where the framing falls on this spectrum; do not judge whether the paper is actually early or late in reality, and do not judge which framing produced better research.

K11. Recency anchoring
     Rate 0–10 for explicit references to recent, emerging, current, or latest work and newly released model or benchmark names; judge temporal-marking extent only, not topical importance or quality.

K12. Template scaffolding: enumerated connectives and uniform cadence
     Score 0-10 for the EXTENT of visible drafting scaffolding: explicit enumeration of contributions ('Firstly... Secondly', 'first... then... finally', '(1)... (2)... (3)'), a dense chain of formal discourse connectives ('Specifically', 'Moreover', 'Furthermore', 'Additionally', 'To address these issues', 'In this work'), a two-component method presented as exactly two symmetrically described parts, tricolon phrasing, near-uniform sentence lengths, and recurring intensifier boilerplate ('comprehensive', 'extensive experiments', 'consistently outperforms', 'significant improvements'). 0 = the abstract moves without enumeration or formulaic connectives; 10 = nearly every sentence is hinged on a connective or list marker and the intensifier boilerplate recurs. Score the scaffolding only. You must NOT judge whether the organization aids comprehension, and you must NOT reward or penalize clarity.

K13. Fashionable topic markers
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract uses currently prominent field labels such as foundation model, agent, alignment, multimodal, generative AI, or large language model; judge only extent, and do not judge whether this feature is good or bad.

K14. Coined acronym branding
     Rate 0–10 for the presence and prominence of newly coined capitalized names for methods, datasets, frameworks, or components, including acronym expansions; judge naming extent only, not memorability or quality.

K15. Late-arrival riff: patching named incumbents in a crowded line
     Score 0-10 for the EXTENT to which the abstract positions itself as an increment on an already-crowded line of prior attempts, rather than as an opening move on a fresh problem. High-scoring fingerprints: naming a specific prior method and extending or repairing it; phrases of the form 'previous methods attempted X, however'; describing the existing literature as a set of variants; conceding that the obvious approaches are taken and claiming the remaining gap; the contribution being an added mechanism, stage, or regularizer bolted onto a named predecessor. Low-scoring fingerprints: the problem is introduced as barely explored or newly posed, with few or no named incumbents to displace. 0 = reads as one of the first, most obvious readings of its problem; 10 = reads as a late entry written by someone who has already seen the obvious takes taken. Score the positioning only. You must NOT judge whether incremental or foundational work is more valuable.

K16. Deadline-crunch tells: promissory artifacts and preliminary results
     Score 0-10 for the EXTENT of promissory or unfinished-artifact language: code or data promised in the future tense ('will be made publicly available', 'will be open-sourced upon acceptance/publication'), conditional release tied to a later event, unresolved placeholders or bare 'anonymized' where a link belongs, and results hedged as early, preliminary, initial, or a first pass. Contrast with present-tense availability ('code is available at <link>', 'available on Zenodo and GitHub'). 0 = every artifact is described as already existing and reachable, and no result is hedged as preliminary; 10 = all artifacts are future-tense or placeholder and results are explicitly labelled early or preliminary. Score the tense and completeness of these statements only. You must NOT judge whether releasing code is good practice, and you must NOT judge whether preliminary results are acceptable.

K17. Abstract length and detail
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much text volume, paragraphing, and methodological detail are present; judge only extent, and do not judge whether this feature is good or bad.

K18. Appeals to Mathematical Theory
     Score how much the text explicitly invokes formal mathematical theory, proofs, or theorems to justify its claims (e.g., 'We provide theoretical guarantees', 'Eckart-Young-Mirsky theorem'). 0 means no mention of theory, 10 means heavy reliance. Do not judge if the theory is correct.

K19. Anonymized code-repository or 'released upon acceptance' promise
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how strongly the abstract text contains an anonymized code/data hosting reference (e.g., an anonymous.4open.science-style URL, or the word 'anonymous'/'anonymized' attached to a repository) or an explicit promise that code/data 'will be released' or 'made available upon acceptance/publication.' Score only the presence and prominence of this specific textual convention; do not judge whether releasing code is good practice or whether the promise is credible.

K20. System-branding: coined acronym density and typographic stylization
     Score 0-10 for the EXTENT to which the abstract brands its contribution with coined names: count how many capitalized system/method/dataset names are minted (e.g. one-off names, backronyms whose letters are pulled out of a phrase, hyphenated compound names, names typographically stylized with bold or letter-level markup), how early the first coined name appears, and how many times coined names are repeated. 0 = no coined name at all, the method is described only in ordinary technical words; 10 = three or more coined names, introduced in the first two sentences and repeated throughout, at least one of them a stylized backronym. Score naming behaviour only. You must NOT judge whether the names are apt, memorable, clever, or gimmicky, and you must NOT consider whether the underlying method sounds substantial.

K21. Density of generic templated abstract-writing transition phrases
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract relies on generic, interchangeable template transitions that could be dropped into almost any machine-learning paper (e.g., 'To address this issue, we propose...', 'Extensive experiments demonstrate...', 'Our approach significantly outperforms...', 'Experimental results show that...'), as opposed to sentences with content specific to this particular contribution. Score only the density of such generic template phrasing; do not judge whether the paper's actual contribution is significant.

K22. Prior-work anchoring
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract names prior systems, canonical benchmarks, historical methods, or explicit baselines and lineage; judge only extent, and do not judge whether this feature is good or bad.

K23. First-Person Plural Action Count

K25. Length, clause complexity and paragraph bulk
     Score 0-10 for the EXTENT of sheer textual bulk and syntactic heaviness: approximate word count, number of paragraphs or visual blocks, average sentence length, depth of subordinate clauses, and density of parenthetical asides and em-dash insertions. 0 = a short, plainly-built abstract of roughly 100-140 words in one block with mostly simple sentences; 10 = a very long abstract (400+ words) or multiple blocks, built from long multi-clause sentences with stacked qualifiers. Measure size and structural heaviness only. You must NOT judge whether the length is appropriate, whether the abstract is verbose or well-edited, and you must NOT reward thoroughness.

K26. Contemporary Buzzword Concentration

K27. Numeric result density

K28. Revision seams and residue
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract contains literal placeholders, unresolved naming, inconsistent terminology, abrupt joins, or other visible editing residue; judge only extent, and do not judge whether this feature is good or bad.

K30. Benchmark/Dataset Introduction Framing

K31. Primary framing as a new benchmark/dataset/resource rather than a new method

K32. Scale and resource signaling

K33. Inline math/LaTeX notation density

K34. Polished academic fluency

K39. Public-release rhetoric
     On a 0-10 scale, rate how much the abstract emphasizes code, data, demos, APIs, public availability, practitioner use, or broad societal and application impact; judge only extent, and do not judge whether this feature is good or bad.

=== TARGETS ===

T01. Belief-change — the output is a changed understanding rather than a delivered artifact
     Score 0-10 on whether the main thing a reader takes away is a revised belief about how something works, as opposed to a tool, model, or dataset they can now use. Both are legitimate contributions; score only which one the abstract is offering.

T02. Explicit count-signalling of experimental scale
     Score 0-10 for how much the abstract advertises the COUNT of its experiments — 'extensive experiments on six benchmarks', 'four datasets', 'across three tasks', 'comprehensive evaluations'. Score the advertising of counts only, not the actual breadth of the evaluation.

T03. Magnitude of the engineering or data resource the work required
     Score 0-10 on how much construction effort the abstract evidences — corpora built, systems trained at multiple scales, pipelines assembled, annotations collected — judged from what is described as having been made, not from claims about importance.

T04. Multiplicative speedup notation
     Score 0-10 by how many multiplicative performance figures the abstract states — '3.04x', '91 times', '5 to 10 times faster', 'orders of magnitude'. Count the notation only; do not judge whether the speedup is real, fairly measured, or worth its cost.

T05. Membership in the learning-theory / optimization subfield
     Score 0-10 for how far the abstract belongs to the learning-theory, optimization, or statistical-guarantees cluster — convergence rates, regret bounds, complexity results, estimator analysis. Score topic membership only; work of any quality in any other subfield scores 0.

T06. Passive-voice density
     Score 0-10 for how much of the abstract is written in the passive voice ('it is shown that', 'experiments were conducted', 'the model is trained') rather than the active voice. Score the grammatical construction only.

T07. A public repository URL appears in the abstract
     Score 0-10 for whether the abstract contains a real, public code or data link — a repository URL or hosted project address. Score 0 when there is no link. This is about a live public link, not about anonymised submission placeholders or promises to release later.

T08. Density of promotional superlatives and hype vocabulary
     Score 0-10 for how heavily the abstract uses promotional vocabulary — remarkable, impressive, striking, significant, substantial, superior, novel, state-of-the-art, comprehensive, extensive. Count the promotional register, not whether the claims are true.

T09. Branded method name or acronym
     Score 0-10 for how strongly the work brands itself with a coined capitalised name or acronym for its method, system, dataset, or benchmark, and repeats that name through the abstract. Score the branding practice only.

T10. Scare-quoted or coined terms in quotation marks
     Score 0-10 for how much the abstract puts terms in quotation marks to coin, hedge, or distance itself from them — 'false negatives', 'tabula rasa', 'middle way'. Score the quotation habit only.

T11. Anonymized-repository or release-on-acceptance code boilerplate
     Score 0-10 by how prominently the abstract carries submission-time artifact boilerplate: an anonymised repository link (for example an anonymous code-hosting URL or a placeholder), or a promise that code or data will be released later or upon publication. Score 0 when no such statement appears; score by presence and prominence of the boilerplate, not by whether sharing is a good practice.

T12. "Framework" as the contribution noun
     Score 0-10 for how much the abstract labels its own contribution a framework, paradigm, pipeline, or scheme rather than naming a concrete object such as an algorithm, model, dataset, bound, or result. Score the choice of noun only.
